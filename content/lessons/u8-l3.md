Once you know where bias comes from, the next questions are how to measure fairness and how to repair what you find. Fairness definitions are precise enough to compute from a small table, and a test question can hand you one and ask which definition a model meets. This lesson teaches the main definitions through one running example, shows why they cannot all hold at once, and ends with how teams detect and reduce bias.

## Group fairness: demographic parity

In AI, **fairness** means that a system's decisions do not unjustly favor or disadvantage people, especially on the basis of protected characteristics such as race, sex, or age. "Unjustly" can be made precise in several ways, and each way is a different fairness definition. **Group fairness** definitions compare a statistic across groups; individual fairness, later in this lesson, compares individuals.

Throughout this lesson, one loan model is applied to 100 applicants from each of two groups. In group A, 50 applicants would repay a loan and 50 would not. In group B, 20 would repay and 80 would not. The model approves 80 percent of the applicants who would repay and 20 percent of those who would not, in both groups.

| Group | Would repay | Approved (would repay) | Would not repay | Approved (would not repay) | Total approved |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 50 | 40 | 50 | 10 | 50 |
| B | 20 | 16 | 80 | 16 | 32 |

**Demographic parity**, also called statistical parity, requires every group to receive the positive outcome at the same rate, whatever the true outcomes. A group's positive rate, or selection rate, is its number of positive decisions divided by its size:

$$
\text{approval rate} = \frac{\text{number approved}}{\text{number in the group}}
$$

::::example[Checking demographic parity]
Does the running example's loan model satisfy demographic parity?

:::step
Group A: 50 approved out of 100, an approval rate of 50 percent.
:::

:::step
Group B: 32 approved out of 100, an approval rate of 32 percent.
:::

:::step
The rates differ. The ratio of the lower rate to the higher is 0.32 / 0.50 = 0.64.
:::

:::answer
No. Group B is approved at 32 percent and group A at 50 percent, a ratio of 0.64.
:::
::::

In U.S. hiring, the **four-fifths rule** from the 1978 Uniform Guidelines on Employee Selection Procedures turns that ratio into a screening test. A selection rate for any race, sex, or ethnic group that is less than four-fifths (80 percent) of the rate for the group with the highest rate is generally regarded as evidence of **adverse impact**. It is a rule of thumb, not a verdict: smaller differences can still count, and larger ones may not when the numbers are too small to be meaningful. A ratio of 0.64 would be flagged.

Demographic parity is easy to check, but it ignores who actually qualifies. In the running example, 50 percent of group A would repay against 20 percent of group B, so forcing equal approval rates would mean approving more group B applicants who would not repay or rejecting group A applicants who would. Hardt, Price, and Srebro (2016) argued that demographic parity often cripples a model's usefulness.

:::tip
**Test tip.** Demographic parity looks only at decisions. If a definition in a question mentions true outcomes, such as who would repay or who reoffended, it is not demographic parity.
:::

## Equal opportunity and equalized odds [Competitive]

In 2016, Moritz Hardt, Eric Price, and Nathan Srebro proposed definitions that look at the true outcomes, not just the decisions. They use two rates from the confusion matrix (lesson 3.8):

- The **true positive rate** (TPR), also called recall: of the people who truly qualify, the share the model approves.
- The **false positive rate** (FPR): of the people who truly do not qualify, the share the model approves anyway.

**Equal opportunity** requires equal true positive rates across groups: people who truly qualify are equally likely to be approved in every group. Because the false negative rate is 1 minus the true positive rate, equal opportunity also means equal miss rates. **Equalized odds** is stricter: it requires equal true positive rates *and* equal false positive rates.

::::example[Checking equal opportunity and equalized odds]
Does the running example's model satisfy equal opportunity? Equalized odds?

:::step
True positive rates: group A approves 40 of its 50 applicants who would repay, 40 / 50 = 0.8; group B approves 16 of 20, 16 / 20 = 0.8. They match.
:::

:::step
False positive rates: group A approves 10 of its 50 applicants who would not repay, 10 / 50 = 0.2; group B approves 16 of 80, 16 / 80 = 0.2. They match too.
:::

:::answer
Yes to both. The same model that fails demographic parity satisfies equal opportunity and equalized odds.
:::
::::

| Definition | What must be equal across groups | Uses true outcomes? |
|---|---|---|
| Demographic parity | The approval (positive-decision) rate | No |
| Equal opportunity | The true positive rate | Yes |
| Equalized odds | The true positive rate and the false positive rate | Yes |
| Predictive parity | Precision: the share of positive decisions that are correct | Yes |

:::trap
**Common trap.** Equal opportunity does not mean equal approval rates. It means equal approval rates *among the people who qualify*. Equal approval rates for everyone is demographic parity.
:::

## Individual fairness [Competitive]

Group definitions can hold on average while two nearly identical people receive different decisions. **Individual fairness**, from Cynthia Dwork and colleagues (2012), requires that similar individuals be treated similarly, with similarity measured by a metric suited to the task. Two applicants with the same income, debts, and repayment history should get the same decision, whatever group they belong to.

The hard part is the metric. Deciding which differences between two people matter for a loan, and which do not, is a value judgment that people must agree on before any math begins.

A tempting shortcut is **fairness through unawareness**: simply hiding protected attributes from the model. It fails on its own because other features can act as proxies (lesson 8.2). A model that never sees race can still learn it from ZIP code in a city whose neighborhoods are divided by race.

::check{id="fairness-05"}

## Why you cannot satisfy every definition at once [Champion]

The running example already shows two definitions in conflict: the model satisfies equalized odds but fails demographic parity. A third definition fails as well. **Predictive parity** requires equal precision across groups: among the people the model approves, the same share should repay in every group.

::::example[Predictive parity fails]
In the running example, what share of each group's approved applicants would repay?

:::step
Group A approves 50 people, 40 of whom would repay: 40 / 50 = 0.8.
:::

:::step
Group B approves 32 people, 16 of whom would repay: 16 / 32 = 0.5.
:::

:::answer
80 percent against 50 percent, so predictive parity fails, even though the model's error rates are identical in both groups.
:::
::::

The cause is the difference in **base rates**, the share of each group with the positive outcome: 50 percent of group A would repay, but only 20 percent of group B. Two results make the conflict precise:

- Chouldechova (2017) showed that when base rates differ, a risk score with predictive parity must have unequal false positive or false negative rates across groups.
- Kleinberg, Mullainathan, and Raghavan (2016) proved that three conditions for risk scores, calibration within groups and balance for both the positive and the negative class, cannot all hold at once except with perfect prediction or equal base rates.

This is exactly the COMPAS dispute from lesson 8.2. ProPublica compared error rates and found far more false positives among Black defendants. Northpointe, the tool's maker, checked predictive parity and found that among defendants labeled higher risk, similar shares of each group reoffended. Neither side's arithmetic was simply wrong; they used different definitions of fairness, and those definitions cannot all hold when base rates differ.

:::key
**Key idea.** When groups have different base rates, no imperfect model can satisfy every fairness definition at once. Choosing a definition is a value judgment about which errors matter most, and it should involve the people the system affects.
:::

## Detecting and reducing bias

**Bias detection** is the work of finding unfair patterns in data and models. The core habit is disaggregated evaluation: compute approval rates, true positive rates, false positive rates, and precision for each group, then compare them. Teams also check whether the training data represent every group, document results in model cards (lesson 8.7), and commission audits; Gender Shades was an outside audit of commercial systems.

Toolkits make this routine. AI Fairness 360, an open-source Python toolkit released by IBM Research in 2018, provides fairness metrics for datasets and models along with algorithms for reducing bias. Those algorithms, like all mitigation methods, are grouped by where they act:

| Stage | What it changes | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-processing | The training data, before learning | Collecting more representative data; reweighing examples; transforming features to strip out proxy information |
| In-processing | The learning procedure itself | Adding a fairness penalty or constraint to the training objective; adversarial debiasing |
| Post-processing | A trained model's outputs, treating the model as a black box | Group-specific decision thresholds; equalized odds post-processing |

Hardt, Price, and Srebro showed how to optimally adjust any learned predictor to meet their definitions, a post-processing method. Post-processing is often the only option when you use a model you cannot retrain, such as one bought from a vendor.

Every fix involves a trade-off. Closing one gap can widen another or cost some accuracy, so teams choose their fairness goals with the affected stakeholders, test again after every change, and keep monitoring after deployment, where feedback loops can bring bias back.

::check{id="fairness-07"}

:::takeaways
- Fairness means decisions that do not unjustly favor or disadvantage people. Group definitions compare statistics across groups; individual fairness compares people.
- Demographic parity: equal approval rates across groups, ignoring true outcomes. Four-fifths rule: in U.S. hiring, a group's selection rate below 80 percent of the highest group's rate is generally treated as evidence of adverse impact.
- Equal opportunity (Hardt and colleagues, 2016): equal true positive rates. Equalized odds: equal true positive and false positive rates. Predictive parity: equal precision.
- Per group: approval rate = approved / group size; TPR = qualified and approved / qualified; FPR = unqualified but approved / unqualified; precision = qualified and approved / approved.
- Individual fairness (Dwork and colleagues, 2012): similar people treated similarly; the hard part is agreeing on the similarity metric. Fairness through unawareness fails because of proxies.
- When base rates differ, the definitions conflict (Chouldechova, 2017; Kleinberg, Mullainathan, and Raghavan, 2016). In the COMPAS dispute, ProPublica used error rates and Northpointe used predictive parity, and both computed correctly.
- Bias detection: compare metrics by group, check the data, audit, document. Mitigation: pre-processing (data), in-processing (training), post-processing (outputs of a black-box model). AI Fairness 360 is an open-source toolkit for both.
:::

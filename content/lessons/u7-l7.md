A Bayesian network turns the probability-based reasoning of lesson 7.6 into a picture: random variables as circles, influences as arrows, and a small table of probabilities at each circle. One objective asks you to describe Bayesian networks and their components, so expect questions on nodes, edges, and directed acyclic graphs, plus short calculations that multiply probabilities from the tables. At the champion level come parameter counts and the independence patterns, including the famous effect called explaining away.

## What a Bayesian network is

A **Bayesian network**, also called a belief network, is a probabilistic model that represents a set of random variables and the dependencies among them with a directed acyclic graph, where each variable has a table of probabilities given the variables that directly influence it. It packs a large joint probability distribution into a compact form and lets a system reason from evidence in either direction: from causes to effects, or from effects back to causes. Judea Pearl, whose work on probabilistic reasoning earned the 2011 Turing Award, developed and championed them.

The classic example, used throughout this lesson, models a lawn. Whether it is cloudy influences whether the sprinkler runs and whether it rains, and both of those influence whether the grass is wet:

## Nodes, edges, and why the graph must be acyclic

A Bayesian network has two kinds of parts:

- A **node** represents a random variable, such as Rain, which is true or false. Variables can have more than two values, such as weather with values sunny, cloudy, and rainy.
- An **edge** is a directed arrow from one node to another, representing a direct influence or dependence: the arrow from Rain to WetGrass says that rain directly affects whether the grass is wet. Edges often follow cause and effect.

The node an arrow leaves is the **parent**; the node it enters is the **child**. Cloudy has two children, Sprinkler and Rain, and WetGrass has two parents. A node with no parents, such as Cloudy, is a **root**. A missing edge is also information: it says there is no direct dependence between those two variables.

The graph must be a **directed acyclic graph** (DAG): every edge has a direction, and there are no directed cycles, so you can never follow the arrows and return to where you started. The reason is mathematical. Each variable's probability is defined given its parents, and multiplying those tables together must produce one consistent joint distribution. With a cycle, such as A → B → C → A, a variable would in effect depend on itself, and the product would not define a valid distribution.

:::trap
**Common trap.** Acyclic does not mean the arrows never meet. WetGrass has two parents, and the sprinkler network still contains no directed cycle, because following the arrows always leads downward and never back up.
:::

## Conditional probability tables

Each node has a **conditional probability table** (CPT) that gives the probability of each of its values for every combination of its parents' values. A root node's table is just its prior probability. For true-or-false variables, listing the probability of true is enough, since the probability of false is 1 minus it.

| Node | Parents | Probabilities |
|---|---|---|
| Cloudy | none | $$P(C) = 0.5$$ |
| Sprinkler | Cloudy | $$P(S \mid C) = 0.1$$, $$P(S \mid \neg C) = 0.5$$ |
| Rain | Cloudy | $$P(R \mid C) = 0.8$$, $$P(R \mid \neg C) = 0.2$$ |
| WetGrass | Sprinkler, Rain | $$P(W \mid S, R) = 0.99$$, $$P(W \mid S, \neg R) = 0.9$$, $$P(W \mid \neg S, R) = 0.9$$, $$P(W \mid \neg S, \neg R) = 0$$ |

Read the tables as stories. On cloudy days the sprinkler rarely runs (0.1) but rain is likely (0.8). The grass is almost certainly wet if either one happens, and never wet if neither does.

::check{id="bn-structure-06"}

## Computing a joint probability [Competitive]

The point of the tables is that they determine the probability of every complete assignment of values. The rule: multiply each variable's probability given its parents.

$$
P(X_1, \ldots, X_n) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(X_i \mid \text{Parents}(X_i))
$$

For the sprinkler network, that factorization is $$P(C, S, R, W) = P(C)\, P(S \mid C)\, P(R \mid C)\, P(W \mid S, R)$$.

::::example[Probability of one complete situation]
What is the probability that it is cloudy, the sprinkler is off, it rains, and the grass is wet?

:::step
Cloudy is true: $$P(C) = 0.5$$.
:::

:::step
The sprinkler is off on a cloudy day: $$P(\neg S \mid C) = 1 - 0.1 = 0.9$$.
:::

:::step
It rains on a cloudy day: $$P(R \mid C) = 0.8$$. The grass is wet with the sprinkler off and rain: $$P(W \mid \neg S, R) = 0.9$$.
:::

:::answer
Multiply: 0.5 × 0.9 × 0.8 × 0.9 = 0.324. Each factor uses only the node's own parents; the most common mistake is forgetting to subtract from 1 for a false value.
:::
::::

Try it: toggle each variable and watch the four factors and their product change.

## Counting parameters [Champion]

How many numbers does a network need? A true-or-false node with k true-or-false parents needs one number for each combination of its parents' values: $$2^k$$ numbers. In the sprinkler network, that is 1 for Cloudy, 2 each for Sprinkler and Rain, and 4 for WetGrass, a total of 9.

A full joint table, listing a probability for every combination of n true-or-false variables directly, needs $$2^n - 1$$ numbers, minus 1 because all the probabilities must add up to 1. For the sprinkler network, that is $$2^4 - 1 = 15$$. The gap explodes with size: 20 true-or-false variables with at most 2 parents each need at most 20 × 4 = 80 numbers, while their full joint table needs $$2^{20} - 1 = 1{,}048{,}575$$.

The savings come from the missing edges: every absent arrow is an independence assumption that the network does not have to pay for with extra numbers. (For a variable with more than two values, each row of its table needs one fewer number than the variable has values, since each row sums to 1.)

::check{id="bn-joint-04"}

## Conditional independence and explaining away [Competitive]

A Bayesian network's arrows also tell you when learning one thing changes your beliefs about another. Three basic patterns cover most questions:

| Pattern | Shape | Without evidence in the middle | Once the middle node is known |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Chain** | A → B → C | A and C are dependent | A and C become independent |
| **Fork** (common cause) | A ← B → C | A and C are dependent | A and C become independent |
| **Collider** (common effect) | A → C ← B | A and B are independent | A and B become dependent |

**Chain.** In Fire → Smoke → Alarm, a fire makes the alarm more likely, but once you know whether there is smoke, knowing about the fire tells you nothing more about the alarm, because the alarm responds only to smoke.

**Fork.** In the sprinkler network, Sprinkler ← Cloudy → Rain. Seeing the sprinkler running hints that the day is not cloudy, which makes rain less likely, so the two are dependent. But once you know whether it is cloudy, the sprinkler tells you nothing more about the rain.

**Collider.** In Burglary → Alarm ← Earthquake, a burglary and an earthquake are independent events. But suppose the alarm goes off. Both causes become more likely, and now they compete: if you then learn there was an earthquake, the burglary becomes less likely, because the earthquake already explains the alarm. This is **explaining away**, and it is why observing a common effect makes its causes dependent. The same happens with Sprinkler → WetGrass ← Rain: learning that it rained makes a running sprinkler a less likely explanation for wet grass.

A node's **Markov blanket** is its parents, its children, and its children's other parents. Given its Markov blanket, a node is independent of every other node in the network. In the sprinkler network, Sprinkler's Markov blanket is Cloudy (its parent), WetGrass (its child), and Rain (its child's other parent).

::check{id="bn-independence-01"}

## Inference: predictive and diagnostic reasoning

**Inference** in a Bayesian network means computing the probability of some variables given evidence about others. It runs in two directions:

- **Predictive** (causal) reasoning goes from causes to effects, down the arrows: given that it is cloudy, how likely is wet grass?
- **Diagnostic** reasoning goes from effects to causes, up the arrows: given that the grass is wet, how likely is rain? It uses Bayes' rule.

::::example[Diagnostic reasoning in a two-node network]
In a small network Rain → WetGrass, rain has prior probability 0.2, the grass is wet with probability 0.9 when it rains, and 0.1 when it does not (dew, perhaps). The grass is wet. How likely is rain?

:::step
Probability of rain and wet grass: 0.2 × 0.9 = 0.18.
:::

:::step
Probability of no rain and wet grass: 0.8 × 0.1 = 0.08. So the evidence, wet grass, has probability 0.18 + 0.08 = 0.26.
:::

:::answer
P(rain given wet grass) = 0.18 / 0.26, about 0.69. Seeing the effect raised the belief in its cause from 0.2 to about 0.69.
:::
::::

For small networks, **exact inference** adds up the joint probabilities of all the relevant assignments, with methods such as variable elimination that avoid repeating work. In large, densely connected networks, exact inference can take time that grows exponentially, so systems use **approximate inference**: they simulate many random samples of the network and count how often each outcome occurs, trading a little accuracy for speed.

Bayesian networks are used wherever reasoning must combine uncertain evidence: medical diagnosis (diseases as parents, symptoms and test results as children), fault diagnosis in machines and networks, risk assessment, and decision support.

:::takeaways
- A Bayesian network represents random variables and their dependencies with a directed acyclic graph plus a conditional probability table at each node. Judea Pearl developed and championed them.
- A node is a random variable; an edge is a directed arrow from parent to child showing direct influence. A missing edge means no direct dependence.
- The graph must be a DAG, with no directed cycles, so the product of the tables defines one consistent distribution. A cycle such as A → B → C → A makes a network invalid.
- A CPT gives a node's probabilities for every combination of its parents' values; a root node's table is its prior. P(false) = 1 - P(true).
- Joint probability = the product of each node's probability given its parents: P(C, ¬S, R, W) = 0.5 × 0.9 × 0.8 × 0.9 = 0.324.
- A true-or-false node with k true-or-false parents needs 2^k numbers (sprinkler network: 1 + 2 + 2 + 4 = 9), versus 2^n - 1 for a full joint table (15).
- Chains and forks: the ends become independent once the middle is known. Colliders: the causes become dependent once the effect is known, which is explaining away. The Markov blanket is a node's parents, children, and children's other parents.
- Predictive reasoning goes from causes to effects; diagnostic reasoning goes from effects to causes with Bayes' rule. Exact inference is exponential in the worst case, so large networks use sampling.
:::

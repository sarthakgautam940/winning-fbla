title: How AI inherits bias
---

An AI system learns from data that people collected and pursues goals that people chose, so it can absorb people's unfairness and repeat it across millions of decisions. The test asks you to name the kind of bias in a scenario, spot proxy variables and feedback loops, and connect famous cases to what went wrong in them. This lesson follows a model from its data to its use and shows where bias can enter at each stage.

## What algorithmic bias is

**Algorithmic bias** is systematic error in an AI system that produces unfair outcomes, favoring or disadvantaging certain people or groups. The word *systematic* matters. Random mistakes land on everyone about equally; biased mistakes land again and again on the same people.

Bias rarely comes from a programmer writing prejudice into code. A model learns whatever patterns its training data and its objective reward. If the data record an unfair past, leave some groups out, or measure the wrong thing, the model learns those patterns too, and then applies them at a scale no single person could.

A framework by Harini Suresh and John Guttag (2021) names seven sources of harm across the machine learning life cycle. Three enter with the data; four enter as the model is built, tested, and used.

| Stage | Source of bias | What goes wrong |
|---|---|---|
| Data | Historical | The data accurately record a world that was unfair |
| Data | Representation | Part of the population is missing or underrepresented in the sample |
| Data | Measurement | A feature or label is a poor proxy for what matters, or is measured differently across groups |
| Model | Aggregation | One model is used for groups whose data mean different things |
| Model | Learning | Modeling choices, such as the objective, widen gaps between groups |
| Model | Evaluation | The test data or metric hides poor performance for some groups |
| Use | Deployment | The model is used for a purpose or setting it was not built for |

:::trap
**Common trap.** Do not confuse algorithmic bias with the *bias* of the bias-variance tradeoff (lesson 5.6). That bias is the error a model makes by being too simple, and it hurts everyone's predictions. Algorithmic bias is about unfair differences between people or groups.
:::

## Bias from data: historical, representation, measurement, labels

**Historical bias** exists even when data are collected perfectly, because the world the data describe was unfair. Amazon learned this with an experimental recruiting tool. Trained on résumés submitted over a 10-year period, mostly by men, the tool learned to penalize résumés containing the word "women's," as in "women's chess club captain," and it downgraded graduates of two all-women's colleges. Amazon edited out those terms, but with no guarantee that the model would not find other ways to sort candidates, it disbanded the team by the start of 2017. Reuters revealed the story in October 2018.

**Representation bias** appears when part of the population is missing or underrepresented in the training sample, so the model works worse for them. Suresh and Guttag point out that about 45 percent of the images in ImageNet, a famous training set, were taken in the United States, against 1 percent in China and 2.1 percent in India. A model trained on it sees mostly American streets, homes, and faces.

**Measurement bias** comes from measuring the wrong thing, or measuring it differently for different groups. Often a model needs a quantity that cannot be observed directly, such as health need or criminal behavior, so its designers use a **proxy**: an observable variable that stands in for it. In 2019, Ziad Obermeyer and colleagues studied a widely used commercial algorithm that chose patients for extra care. It predicted future health-care costs as a proxy for health needs, but less money is spent on Black patients with the same level of need. At the same risk score, Black patients were considerably sicker than white patients, and the researchers estimated that removing the disparity would raise the share of Black patients receiving extra help from 17.7 percent to 46.5 percent. Criminal-justice risk tools face the same problem: they learn from arrests, a proxy for crime that is measured differently across communities.

**Labels** deserve special suspicion. When the "right answers" in training data are past human decisions, such as who was hired, who got a loan, or who was arrested, a supervised model learns to reproduce those decisions, including any unfairness in them.

:::key
**Key idea.** A model can be biased even when every number in its training data is accurate. Historical bias is about an unfair world, not sloppy data collection, and a proxy can be measured perfectly while standing in badly for what you care about.
:::

## Bias from design: proxies, aggregation, evaluation [Competitive]

**Proxies for protected attributes.** Deleting a sensitive attribute such as race or gender does not delete the information when other features carry it. Amazon's tool penalized words like "women's" and the names of women's colleges, which revealed gender without any gender field. In a city where neighborhoods differ sharply by income, a ZIP code can stand in for income the same way. Ignoring the sensitive attribute is called fairness through unawareness, and lesson 8.3 shows why it fails.

**Aggregation bias** comes from using one model for groups whose data mean different things. Suresh and Guttag's example is social media posts in which certain emojis and hashtags carry local meanings that a general-purpose model misreads. One model can be accurate on average and still systematically wrong for a group whose patterns differ.

**Learning bias** comes from modeling choices themselves. An objective that rewards only overall accuracy lets a model sacrifice a small group, since that group adds little to the total. Suresh and Guttag also note that compressing a model, for example by pruning it, can widen gaps for underrepresented groups.

**Evaluation bias** hides problems at test time. If the benchmark does not look like the people who will use the system, or results are reported only as one overall number, a subgroup's poor performance goes unnoticed. The Gender Shades study (2018) by Joy Buolamwini and Timnit Gebru showed this. Two common face benchmarks were mostly lighter-skinned (IJB-A 79.6 percent and Adience 86.2 percent), and darker-skinned women made up only 4.4 percent and 7.4 percent of them. On a new benchmark of 1,270 parliamentarians from six countries, labeled by skin type, the commercial gender classifiers of Microsoft, IBM, and Face++ had error rates up to 34.7 percent for darker-skinned women, against at most 0.8 percent for lighter-skinned men.

::::example[An overall accuracy that hides a gap]
A face-analysis model is tested on 1,000 photos: 900 of group A and 100 of group B. It is correct on 873 of the group A photos and 77 of the group B photos. What is its overall accuracy, and what does that number hide?

:::step
Group A: 873 / 900 = 0.97, so 97 percent.
:::

:::step
Group B: 77 / 100 = 0.77, so 77 percent.
:::

:::step
Overall: (873 + 77) / 1,000 = 950 / 1,000 = 0.95, so 95 percent.
:::

:::answer
The model reports 95 percent accuracy, yet it errs on 23 percent of group B's photos against 3 percent of group A's. Because group B is small, its failures barely move the total. Report accuracy for each group, not only overall.
:::
::::

:::tip
**Test tip.** When a question gives results for each group, compute each group's rate before trusting the overall number. An overall rate is a weighted average, so it sits close to the largest group's rate.
:::

## Bias from use: deployment and feedback loops [Competitive]

**Deployment bias** arises when a model is used for a purpose, population, or decision it was not designed for. A model can be reasonable in its intended setting and harmful in another. Suresh and Guttag's example is risk tools built to predict whether a defendant will reoffend that have been used "off-label" to help decide the length of a sentence, a judgment they were never designed to make.

A **feedback loop** occurs when a model's outputs change the data it later learns from, so its early patterns reinforce themselves. Predictive policing shows how. The model sends more officers to the areas it predicts will have more crime. More officers there record more crime there, and those records update the model, which then predicts even more crime in the same areas. Danielle Ensign and colleagues (2018) showed that such systems can keep sending police back to the same neighborhoods regardless of the true crime rate.

```figure
type: flow
caption: A runaway feedback loop. The model's predictions decide where new data come from, so its first guess keeps confirming itself.
steps: [Model predicts more crime in area A, More officers patrol area A, More crime is recorded in area A, The new records retrain the model]
cycle: true
```

Systems that keep learning from live users face a related risk: they can absorb the worst of their users. Microsoft's Twitter chatbot Tay, launched in March 2016, was taken offline within a day after users manipulated it into posting offensive tweets.

:::key
**Key idea.** Bias can enter at every stage: in the data (historical, representation, measurement), in the design (proxies, aggregation, learning, evaluation), and in use (deployment, feedback loops). Fixing one stage does not fix the others.
:::

## Documented cases

These cases come up again and again in discussions of AI bias. For each one, know what happened and which source of bias it shows.

| Case | What happened | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| Amazon recruiting tool (reported 2018) | Trained on 10 years of résumés, mostly from men; penalized "women's" and graduates of two all-women's colleges; scrapped | Historical bias; deleting words does not delete proxies |
| COMPAS risk scores (ProPublica, 2016) | Among defendants who did not reoffend, Black defendants were nearly twice as likely as white defendants to be labeled higher risk (false-positive rates about 45 versus 23 percent) | Measurement bias from arrests as a proxy, and a fairness trade-off (lesson 8.3) |
| Gender Shades (2018) | Commercial gender classifiers erred up to 34.7 percent on darker-skinned women and at most 0.8 percent on lighter-skinned men | Representation and evaluation bias; test every group |
| Health-care algorithm (Obermeyer and colleagues, 2019) | Used costs as a proxy for need, so Black patients at the same score were sicker | Measurement bias from a convenient proxy |
| Google Photos (2015) | Labeled photos of a Black software developer and a friend as "gorillas"; testing in 2018 found the fix was to block the labels "gorilla," "chimp," and "chimpanzee" | Blocking an output hides a problem without fixing the model |
| Dutch childcare benefits scandal | A risk model used non-Dutch nationality as a risk factor; about 26,000 parents and caregivers were wrongly accused of fraud; the cabinet resigned in January 2021 | A sensitive attribute used directly, with no meaningful human oversight |
| A-level grades (England, 2020) | With exams cancelled, an algorithm meant to keep grades broadly in line with previous years lowered 39.1 percent of teacher-assessed grades; reversed four days later | Group patterns overriding individual evidence, at national scale |
| Apple Card (2019) | Viral claims of lower credit limits for women; New York's financial regulator found no evidence of unlawful sex discrimination (2021) | Investigate before concluding; not every allegation is confirmed |

:::trap
**Common trap.** Gender Shades measured how well systems *classified the gender* of a face, not face recognition for identifying people. And the Apple Card investigation found no sex discrimination; a separate 2024 federal penalty of over $89 million was about mishandling transaction disputes, not gender.
:::

:::takeaways
- Algorithmic bias is systematic error that produces unfair outcomes for certain people or groups. It usually enters through data and design choices, not intent, and it is different from the bias in the bias-variance tradeoff.
- From data: historical bias (an accurate record of an unfair world, as with Amazon's recruiting tool), representation bias (groups missing or underrepresented), and measurement bias (a poor proxy or uneven measurement, as with the health-care algorithm that used cost for need).
- Labels copied from past human decisions carry those decisions' unfairness into the model.
- From design: proxies for protected attributes (removing the attribute is not enough), aggregation bias (one model for groups that differ), learning bias (choices that widen gaps), and evaluation bias (benchmarks or overall metrics that hide subgroup failures, as Gender Shades exposed).
- From use: deployment bias (a model used for a purpose it was not built for) and feedback loops (outputs shape future data, as in predictive policing).
- An overall accuracy is a weighted average. Always compute each group's rate: 95 percent overall can hide 77 percent for a small group.
- Cases: Amazon (historical), COMPAS (arrests as a proxy; fairness trade-off), Gender Shades (representation and evaluation), Obermeyer (cost as a proxy), Google Photos (blocking labels is not a fix), Dutch benefits (nationality as a risk factor), A-levels 2020 (reversed in four days), Apple Card (no discrimination found).
:::

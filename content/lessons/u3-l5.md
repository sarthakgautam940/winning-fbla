Cleaning is the step that most often decides whether an analysis is right or wrong. The objectives ask you to discuss why cleaning data matters, and test questions go further: they describe a problem in a dataset and ask for the best fix, or describe a fix and ask what side effect it has. This lesson covers why cleaning matters, the three kinds of missing data, when to delete and when to impute, how to handle duplicates, inconsistent formats, and outliers, and how to prove that the cleaning worked.

## Garbage in, garbage out

**Data cleaning** is the process of detecting and correcting (or removing) errors, inconsistencies, and missing values so that data is accurate and fit for analysis. The principle behind it is **garbage in, garbage out**: no analysis or model can be better than the data it is given.

Dirty data does damage in predictable ways:

- A duplicated customer inflates the customer count and tilts every average toward that customer.
- One typo, such as a salary of $5,000,000 entered instead of $50,000, drags the mean and the regression line far from the truth.
- Mislabeled training examples teach a machine learning model the wrong patterns, and no amount of tuning recovers what the labels got wrong.

Cleaning and wrangling (lesson 1.7) overlap, but they have different aims. **Wrangling** reshapes and transforms data into a usable form: tidying, converting types, scaling, and encoding. **Cleaning** fixes what is wrong: errors, duplicates, inconsistencies, and gaps. Both belong to data preparation.

A typical cleaning workflow runs in this order:

1. **Profile** the data: check types, ranges, counts of missing values, and duplicates before changing anything.
2. **Fix structure**: correct types, formats, and units.
3. **Remove duplicates**.
4. **Handle missing values**.
5. **Investigate outliers and invalid values**.
6. **Validate and document** the result.

## Missing data: MCAR, MAR, MNAR [Competitive]

Why data is missing matters more than how much is missing. Statisticians describe three mechanisms, which grew out of Donald Rubin's 1976 work on missing data:

- **Missing completely at random (MCAR)**: the probability that a value is missing is the same for every case and unrelated to any data. A lab technician accidentally drops a few randomly chosen samples.
- **Missing at random (MAR)**: the probability of being missing depends only on data you did observe. Younger survey respondents skip the income question more often, and every respondent's age is recorded.
- **Missing not at random (MNAR)**: the probability of being missing depends on information you did not observe, often the missing value itself. People with very high incomes are the ones who skip the income question.

:::trap
**Common trap.** "Missing at random" does not mean random in the everyday sense. MAR means the missingness can be explained by variables you have. Truly random loss, with no link to anything, is MCAR.
:::

The mechanism decides how much harm missing data does. Under MCAR, deleting incomplete rows loses information but does not bias the results. Under MAR, methods that use the observed variables (such as filling in income using age) can correct much of the bias. Under MNAR, bias remains unless you model why the values are missing, which is why MNAR is the hardest case.

::check{id="missing-data-01"}

## Deleting versus imputing

There are two broad ways to handle missing values: delete them or fill them in.

**Deletion** is simple. **Listwise deletion** drops every row that has any missing value; you can also drop a column that is mostly empty. Deletion is safe under MCAR but shrinks the sample, and when the data are MAR or MNAR, the deleted rows differ from the kept ones, so the remaining data are biased.

**Imputation** fills in each missing value with an estimate. Common methods:

- **Mean imputation**: fill with the column's mean. Reasonable for roughly symmetric data.
- **Median imputation**: fill with the median. Better for skewed data, because the median is not pulled by outliers.
- **Mode imputation**: fill a categorical column with its most common category.
- **Group-wise imputation**: fill with the mean or median of the case's own group, such as its region.
- **Model-based imputation**: predict each missing value from the other variables, for example with regression or with the most similar complete rows (k-nearest neighbors).
- **Forward fill** for time series: carry the last observed value forward, or interpolate between neighboring readings. A sensor's reading one minute ago is a better guess than its yearly average.

Imputation has side effects. Filling many gaps with one central value **shrinks the variance** and weakens correlations, because every filled value sits exactly at the center with no spread.

::::example[Mean imputation shrinks the variance]
A column holds 10, 12, 14, 16, and 18, plus three missing values. Fill the gaps with the mean and compare the sample variances.

:::step
The mean of the five observed values is 14. The squared deviations are 16, 4, 0, 4, and 16, which sum to 40, so the sample variance is 40 / (5 - 1) = 10.
:::

:::step
Fill the three gaps with 14. Each filled value has a deviation of 0, so the sum of squared deviations stays 40, but n grows to 8.
:::

:::step
The new sample variance is 40 / (8 - 1) = 40 / 7, about 5.71.
:::

:::answer
The variance falls from 10 to about 5.71, even though no real value changed. Mean imputation makes the data look more consistent than it is.
:::
::::

A good habit is to add a **missing indicator**, a 0/1 column that marks which values were imputed. It lets a model learn whether being missing carries information of its own, as it often does under MNAR.

:::tip
**Test tip.** If the stem mentions skewed data or outliers, choose median imputation over mean imputation. If it mentions time order, choose forward fill or interpolation. If it asks for a side effect of mean imputation, choose reduced variance.
:::

## Duplicates and inconsistent formats

**Duplicates** come in two kinds. **Exact duplicates** are identical rows, often from a form submitted twice or a file loaded twice. **Near-duplicates** are the same real-world item recorded slightly differently: "Jon Smith" and "John Smith" with the same email address, or "ACME Inc." and "Acme, Inc." Detect them by checking the columns that should identify an item, such as a customer ID or email, after standardizing spelling and spacing. Then decide which record to keep, usually the most complete or most recent, and merge or drop the rest.

**Inconsistent formats** split one value into several. A state column might hold "NY", "N.Y.", "new york", and "New York " with a trailing space; to a computer those are four different categories. Fix them by **standardizing**: trim spaces, choose one case, and map every variant to a single form. The same goes for dates written several ways, for prices stored as text with symbols, and for **mixed units** such as pounds in some rows and kilograms in others, which must all be converted to one unit.

**Invalid values** break the rules of the field: an age of 210, a negative quantity ordered, an end date before its start date. Correct them from the original source when you can. When you cannot, treat the value as missing rather than guessing.

::check{id="clean-issues-01"}

## Outliers: investigate before you remove

An outlier is a value far from the rest of the data, flagged by a rule such as 1.5 × IQR beyond the quartiles (lesson 2.2) or a z-score beyond 3 (lesson 2.5). An outlier is a question, not a verdict. Ask what caused it:

- **An error**: a typo, a unit mix-up, or a sensor glitch. Fix it from the source or remove it.
- **A genuine but rare value**: a billionaire's income, a video that went viral. Keep it, because it is real. If it overwhelms an analysis, use **resistant** summaries, which extremes barely move, such as the median and IQR, or transform the variable (for example, with a log).
- **A case from a different population**: a business account miscoded as a household in a study of home electricity use. Separate it, since it does not belong in this analysis.

Never delete a value just because it is extreme. Record every removal and its reason, and when in doubt, run the analysis with and without the outliers to see whether the conclusion changes. In some tasks, such as fraud detection, the outliers are the whole point.

:::key
**Key idea.** Fix errors, keep genuine extremes, and separate values from other populations. The cause of an outlier decides what to do with it.
:::

::check{id="clean-issues-02"}

## Validating that cleaning worked

Cleaning changes data, and every change can introduce new errors. **Validation** checks the result before anyone relies on it:

- **Counts**: how many rows were removed, and is that the number expected?
- **Keys**: are there any repeated IDs left?
- **Missing values**: are the remaining gaps the ones you chose to leave?
- **Ranges and rules**: are all values within their valid ranges?
- **Totals**: do sums reconcile with an outside source, such as the finance department's official sales total?
- **Distributions**: compare summary statistics before and after. A standard deviation that suddenly drops may signal heavy imputation.

Write these checks as code so they run every time. An **assertion** stops a script with an error the moment a rule is broken:

```python run
import pandas as pd

df = pd.DataFrame({"id": [101, 102, 103], "age": [34, 51, 28]})
assert df["id"].is_unique
assert df["age"].between(0, 120).all()
print(df["age"].isna().sum(), len(df))
```

```output
0 3
```

Databases enforce the same idea with **constraints** such as NOT NULL, UNIQUE, and CHECK, which reject bad rows before they are ever saved (lesson 4.1).

Finally, **document** the cleaning. Keep the raw data untouched, write every step as code, and record what changed and why. Documented cleaning can be reviewed, repeated on new data, and undone, which is the reproducibility habit from lesson 1.8.

::check{id="validate-clean-01"}

:::takeaways
- Data cleaning detects and fixes errors, inconsistencies, and missing values. Garbage in, garbage out: no model beats its data.
- Workflow: profile, fix structure, remove duplicates, handle missing values, investigate outliers, then validate and document.
- MCAR: missingness unrelated to anything. MAR: explained by observed variables. MNAR: depends on unobserved values, often the missing value itself.
- Listwise deletion is safe only under MCAR. Imputation fills gaps: mean for symmetric data, median for skewed data, mode for categories, forward fill for time series.
- Mean or median imputation shrinks variance and weakens correlations; a missing indicator records which values were filled.
- Standardize inconsistent formats and units; find duplicates by their identifying columns; treat unfixable invalid values as missing.
- Investigate outliers before acting: fix errors, keep genuine extremes, separate other populations, and never delete a value only because it is extreme.
- Validate with counts, key checks, ranges, reconciled totals, and before-and-after summaries; use assertions and constraints; document every step.
:::

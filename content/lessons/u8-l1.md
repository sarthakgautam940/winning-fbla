The last test area asks you to discuss the dilemmas AI systems create, naming self-driving vehicles, generative AI, and surveillance as examples. Ethics questions on a multiple-choice test are not about opinions: they ask you to recognize which values conflict in a scenario, match a principle to a practice, and know the real cases and rules that shaped the debate. This lesson covers the dilemmas themselves and the frameworks for reasoning about them.

## What makes something an ethical dilemma

An **ethical dilemma** is a situation in which every available choice conflicts with some important moral value or duty, so no option is free of cost and thoughtful people can reasonably disagree. That is different from a plain wrong. Selling customers' data in secret is simply unethical; deciding whether a city should use facial recognition to find missing children, at some cost to everyone's privacy, is a dilemma, because both options protect something that matters.

AI multiplies such dilemmas for three reasons. It makes or shapes decisions at enormous scale, so small choices affect millions of people. It affects many **stakeholders**, everyone with something at stake, whose interests differ. And it forces trade-offs between values that usually coexist: safety and freedom, accuracy and privacy, efficiency and employment, innovation and harm.

:::tip
**Test tip.** To identify the dilemma in a scenario, name the two values in tension, such as "public safety versus privacy" or "efficiency versus jobs." The answer choice that states the conflict precisely is usually right.
:::

## Self-driving vehicles

The best-known AI dilemma began as a thought experiment. In a 1967 essay, the philosopher Philippa Foot described the driver of a runaway tram who can steer only from one track to another: five workers are on one track and one worker is on the other. Judith Jarvis Thomson named it the **trolley problem** in 1976. Applied to self-driving cars, it asks how a vehicle should be programmed to choose between harms when a crash cannot be avoided.

The Moral Machine experiment, published in Nature in 2018, collected 40 million decisions about such crash dilemmas from millions of people in 233 countries and territories. The strongest preferences were to spare humans over animals, more lives over fewer, and the young over the old, and countries fell into three broad clusters, Western, Eastern, and Southern, with different patterns of preference. A real car rarely knows the outcomes of its choices with the certainty a thought experiment assumes, so the everyday ethical questions are about safety and responsibility.

**Levels of automation.** The engineering standard SAE J3016 defines six levels of driving automation, from Level 0 (no driving automation) through Level 1 (driver assistance), Level 2 (partial automation), Level 3 (conditional automation), and Level 4 (high automation) to Level 5 (full automation). At Levels 0 to 2, the features only support a human driver, who must supervise at all times. At Levels 3 to 5, an automated driving system performs the whole driving task while engaged.

:::trap
**Common trap.** Level 2 is not self-driving. A car that steers and controls its speed at the same time still needs a human watching the road every second.
:::

**Liability.** When an automated vehicle crashes, who is responsible: the manufacturer, the software developer, the person in the driver's seat, or the owner? On March 18, 2018, an Uber test vehicle driving in autonomous mode, with a safety operator on board, struck and killed a pedestrian in Tempe, Arizona. The National Transportation Safety Board found that the automated system detected the pedestrian 5.6 seconds before impact but never accurately classified the pedestrian or predicted the pedestrian's path, and that its design relied on the human operator, not the system, for emergency braking. The board named the operator's distraction by a personal phone as the probable cause, with Uber's inadequate safety culture among the contributing factors. Prosecutors declined to charge Uber as a company; in 2023 the operator pleaded guilty to endangerment. Criminal responsibility fell on the human backup, even though the system's design relied on that person to brake. Cases like this are why liability is one of the central dilemmas of automated driving.

**Safety testing.** Testing on public roads exposes people who never agreed to take part to risk, but simulation cannot capture every real situation. And how safe is safe enough: as safe as an average human driver, or far safer? Each answer trades lives saved by deploying sooner against lives lost to an immature system.

## Surveillance and facial recognition

**Surveillance** is the systematic monitoring of people's behavior, communications, or movements, often to influence, manage, or control them. AI makes it cheaper and larger: face recognition on public cameras, license-plate readers that log where cars go, and tracking of searches and web activity to build detailed profiles.

**Privacy and consent.** People walking down a street have not agreed to be identified, and a face cannot be changed like a password. Clearview AI built a face-search tool from more than three billion images scraped from the internet. In 2021, Canada's federal privacy commissioner and three provincial commissioners concluded that it amounted to mass identification and surveillance, and data protection regulators in Italy, Greece, and France each fined the company 20 million euros, with the Netherlands following at 30.5 million euros in 2024.

**Misidentification.** In January 2020, Detroit police arrested Robert Williams at home, in front of family, for a theft of watches, after a facial-recognition search wrongly matched surveillance video to an old driver's-license photo; Williams was held for about 30 hours. In a 2024 settlement, Detroit paid $300,000 and adopted rules barring arrests based solely on facial-recognition results. Error rates that differ across groups (lesson 8.2) make such harms fall unevenly.

**Function creep and chilling effects.** **Function creep** is the gradual expansion of a system beyond its original purpose: cameras installed to manage traffic end up tracking who attends a protest. A **chilling effect** is the self-censorship that follows when people know they are watched: they avoid lawful activities such as protests, religious services, or support groups.

**Security versus liberty.** Surveillance can find missing people and solve crimes, but it can also suppress free speech and assembly. Societies draw the line differently. The EU AI Act bans untargeted scraping of faces to build recognition databases, and it bans police use of real-time remote biometric identification in public spaces apart from narrow exceptions (lesson 8.7).

## Generative AI: deepfakes, copyright, jobs, and energy

Generative AI (lesson 6.3) raises dilemmas of its own.

**Deepfakes** are synthetic images, audio, or video that realistically show people saying or doing things they never did. They enable fraud: in January 2024, an employee in the Hong Kong office of the engineering firm Arup joined a video call with deepfaked versions of the company's chief financial officer and other colleagues, then made 15 transfers totaling about US$25 million to fraudsters. They also enable non-consensual sexual imagery and election misinformation (lesson 8.6). A 2026 amendment to the EU AI Act added AI systems that generate non-consensual sexually explicit content to its list of banned practices, applying from 2 December 2026.

**Copyright and training data.** Generative models learn from enormous amounts of text, images, and music, much of it copyrighted and used without the creators' permission or payment. Whether such training is lawful is a live legal question: the U.S. Copyright Office devoted Part 3 of its report on copyright and AI to generative AI training, releasing a pre-publication version in May 2025 with a final version still to come. On ownership of the output, the Office's 2023 guidance says copyright protects only material that is the product of human creativity, so expression determined by an AI is not protected (lesson 6.6).

**Academic integrity.** Submitting AI-written work as your own is dishonest, just like copying from a classmate. Responsible use means following your class's rules, disclosing how you used AI, and checking facts and citations, which models sometimes invent.

**Jobs and energy.** Generative AI automates tasks in writing, coding, design, and customer service, raising hard questions about who benefits and who bears the cost. Training and running large models also takes heavy computation. NIST's Generative AI Profile lists environmental impacts, caused by the high compute used to train and operate models, among the 12 risks of generative AI.

## AI and society: work, intellectual property, and AI for good [Competitive]

**Work.** AI automates some tasks and creates new kinds of work. AI4K12 names skills for AI-enabled careers, such as curating datasets, working with intelligent agents, and training robots, and it stresses lifelong learning. Forecasting how fast the change will come is hard, and **Amara's Law**, which AI4K12 cites, describes the usual error: we tend to overestimate the effect of a technology in the short run and underestimate it in the long run.

**Intellectual property.** Four kinds of protection come up in AI questions:

| Type | Protects | AI example |
|---|---|---|
| **Copyright** | Original creative works, such as writing, art, music, and software code, once they are fixed in a tangible form. Protection starts the moment the work is created; registration is voluntary. | Whether training on others' work is permitted, and whether AI output is protected |
| **Patent** | Inventions that are new, useful, and not obvious. The government grants the inventor the right to exclude others, usually for up to 20 years from filing. | A novel method for training models |
| **Trademark** | A word, phrase, design, or combination that identifies the source of goods or services | A generated logo that imitates a famous brand |
| **Trade secret** | Information that is valuable because it is not generally known and that its owner takes reasonable steps to keep secret. Protection lasts as long as it stays secret. | A company's model weights, or source code pasted into a public chatbot |

The last example is real: in 2023, Samsung temporarily restricted employees' use of generative AI tools such as ChatGPT after engineers accidentally leaked internal source code by uploading it. NIST's Generative AI Profile counts this kind of exposure of trade secrets, along with copying protected content and plagiarism, under its intellectual property risk.

**AI for social good.** The same technology can do great good. AlphaFold 2 predicts protein structures with accuracy competitive with laboratory methods. Two of its creators, Demis Hassabis and John Jumper, shared in the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, and the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database, developed by Google DeepMind and EMBL-EBI, gives researchers open access to more than 200 million predicted structures. AI4K12 points to AI-for-good projects in energy, the environment, endangered species, public health, and the fight against human trafficking, and connects them to the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals. Many AI tools are also **dual-use**: the capability that drafts a helpful email for a business can draft a convincing phishing email for a scammer.

## Ethical frameworks and principles [Competitive]

Two classic frameworks reason about dilemmas in opposite ways:

- **Utilitarian** (consequentialist) ethics judges an action by its consequences and chooses the option that produces the greatest overall good, or the least total harm. A purely utilitarian car would steer to minimize total casualties.
- **Deontological** (duty-based) ethics holds that some actions are right or wrong in themselves, whatever the consequences. A deontologist might say a car must never be programmed to deliberately target anyone, or that personal data must never be sold without consent, even when breaking the rule would produce more good.

Ethics guidelines for AI tend to share a short list of principles. After comparing six sets of AI ethics principles, the AI4People framework (Floridi and colleagues, 2018) adopted the four core principles of **bioethics**, the ethics of medicine and biology, and added a fifth:

- **Beneficence**: do good, promoting the well-being of people and the planet.
- **Non-maleficence**: do no harm, including harm to privacy and security.
- **Autonomy**: people keep the power to decide, including whether to hand a decision to a machine.
- **Justice**: promote prosperity and preserve solidarity, so that benefits are shared fairly.
- **Explicability**: make AI intelligible and someone accountable for it, which makes the other four possible.

AI4K12 lists the ethical design criteria a system that makes decisions about people should meet:

| Criterion | What it asks | Example practice |
|---|---|---|
| Fairness | Does the system treat people and groups equitably? | Test accuracy separately for each group |
| Transparency | Is it open about how it works and how it is used? | Publish a model card describing data, uses, and limits |
| Explainability | Can it give understandable reasons for a decision? | Show the main factors behind a loan denial |
| Accountability | Is someone answerable for its decisions and harms? | Name an owner and give people a way to appeal |
| Respect for privacy | Does it protect personal data? | Collect only the data the task needs |
| Adherence to societal values | Does it respect laws and widely shared values? | Review a system before deployment against legal and community norms |

**Accountability** means that identifiable people and organizations are answerable for an AI system's decisions and impacts, with ways to explain, audit, contest, and correct them. "The algorithm did it" is never an acceptable answer.

Two practical tools make these principles concrete. An **ethical matrix** lists the stakeholders down one side and the values across the top, and each cell records how the system affects that stakeholder on that value, which makes conflicts visible before launch. A **human in the loop** design keeps a person reviewing or approving high-stakes decisions, such as arrests, loans, or medical treatment, instead of letting the system act alone.

:::takeaways
- An ethical dilemma is a choice in which every option conflicts with an important value; identify it by naming the values in tension. AI multiplies dilemmas through scale, many stakeholders, and value trade-offs.
- Self-driving cars: the trolley problem (Foot, 1967; named by Thomson, 1976), the Moral Machine (2018: spare humans, more lives, the young), SAE J3016's Levels 0 to 5 (0 to 2 need constant human supervision), and liability, as in Uber's 2018 Tempe crash, where the backup operator, not the company, was charged.
- Surveillance is systematic monitoring of people. Concerns are privacy and consent (Clearview AI's scraped faces), misidentification (Robert Williams, 2020), function creep, chilling effects, and security versus liberty.
- Generative AI dilemmas: deepfakes (the US$25 million Arup fraud), copyright and training data, academic integrity, jobs, and energy use.
- Amara's Law: we overestimate a technology's short-term effects and underestimate its long-term ones. Intellectual property comes in four kinds: copyright (creative works, automatic on creation), patent (new, useful, non-obvious inventions, granted for a term), trademark (brand identifiers), and trade secret (valuable information kept secret). AI can also do great good, as AlphaFold 2 shows; many tools are dual-use.
- Utilitarian ethics judges consequences; deontological ethics judges duties and rules. AI4People (2018) uses the four bioethics principles, beneficence, non-maleficence, autonomy, and justice, plus explicability.
- AI4K12's design criteria: fairness, transparency, explainability, accountability, respect for privacy, and adherence to societal values. Accountability means someone answers for the system. Ethical matrices and human-in-the-loop review put the principles into practice.
:::

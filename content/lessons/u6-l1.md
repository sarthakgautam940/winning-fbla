The AI Basics area starts with the ideas every other AI question rests on: what counts as artificial intelligence, how it relates to machine learning and deep learning, which kinds of AI exist today and which exist only in fiction, and how the field got here. Expect definition questions, "which type of AI is this?" scenarios, and history questions that ask you to order events or match a person to a contribution.

## Defining artificial intelligence

**Artificial intelligence** (AI) is the field of computer science that builds systems able to perform tasks that normally require human intelligence: understanding language, recognizing images, reasoning, learning from experience, and making decisions. AI is defined by those abilities, not by any one technique. A program that plans delivery routes, a spam filter, and a chatbot can all count as AI, even though they work very differently.

The standard textbook, Stuart Russell and Peter Norvig's *Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach*, sorts definitions of AI into four approaches by asking two questions. Should the system think or act? And should it be measured against humans or against an ideal of rationality?

| | Measured against humans | Measured against rationality |
|---|---|---|
| Thinking | **Thinking humanly**: model how the human mind works (cognitive modeling) | **Thinking rationally**: reason by the rules of logic (the "laws of thought") |
| Acting | **Acting humanly**: behave in a way people cannot tell from a human (the Turing test approach) | **Acting rationally**: choose the best expected action (the rational agent approach) |

The book is built around the last approach. An **agent** is anything that perceives its environment and acts on it. A **rational agent** chooses, given everything it has perceived so far and the knowledge it was built with, the action expected to maximize its performance measure. Rational does not mean all-knowing: with incomplete information, a sound choice can still turn out badly.

Whatever the approach, today's AI rests on three ingredients: **data** to learn from, **algorithms** that define how it learns and decides, and **computing power** to train and run it.

:::trap
**Common trap.** The Turing test belongs to *acting* humanly, because it judges behavior only. Thinking humanly would require the machine's inner processes to work like a human mind.
:::

## AI, machine learning, and deep learning

The three terms nest inside one another:

- **Artificial intelligence** is the whole field: any technique that lets machines do tasks that need intelligence, including hand-written rules and search.
- **Machine learning** is the part of AI in which systems learn patterns from data instead of following rules written by people (lesson 5.1).
- **Deep learning** is the part of machine learning that uses neural networks with many layers (lesson 5.10).
- **Generative AI**, which creates new text, images, and other content (lesson 6.3), is built today on deep learning.

```figure
type: nested
layers: [Artificial intelligence, Machine learning, Deep learning, Generative AI]
caption: Each ring sits inside the one around it. Today's generative AI is built on deep learning, which is a kind of machine learning, which is one way of building AI.
```

So a chess program that picks moves using rules written by experts is AI but not machine learning; a spam filter trained on labeled emails is machine learning but not deep learning; and an image classifier built from a convolutional network is deep learning.

### Symbolic and connectionist AI

The field has two long traditions. **Symbolic AI** represents knowledge as human-readable symbols and rules, such as "if an animal has feathers, then it is a bird," and reasons with logic and search. Expert systems (lesson 7.5) are the classic example. Symbolic knowledge is transparent and easy to inspect, but people must write it all by hand, and it breaks on situations its rules never anticipated.

**Connectionist AI** learns from examples with networks of simple connected units: the neural networks of Unit 5. Its knowledge is spread across millions of learned weights, so it handles messy inputs such as images and speech well but is hard to explain. Many modern systems combine the two, using learned models for perception and rules or search for reasoning.

## Narrow, general, and super AI

A common way to classify AI is by **capability**, meaning how broad its intelligence is:

- **Artificial narrow intelligence** (ANI), also called narrow or weak AI, performs a specific task or a limited set of tasks: recommending songs, recognizing faces, translating text, playing chess. Every AI system that exists today is narrow, including generative models that write and draw; none can learn and reason across any task the way a person can.
- **Artificial general intelligence** (AGI), also called general or strong AI, would match human ability across any intellectual task, learning new skills and adapting to new problems the way people do. It is hypothetical; no one has built it.
- **Artificial superintelligence** (ASI) would surpass humans in essentially every area. It is theoretical, and researchers debate whether it is possible and how it could be kept safe.

Popular coverage often blurs these levels, so the test likes questions on what AI is and is not:

| Myth | Reality |
|---|---|
| AI is conscious and has feelings. | Today's AI matches patterns; it has no consciousness or feelings. |
| AI is objective because it runs on math. | AI learns from data and can repeat the biases in that data (lesson 8.2). |
| AI will replace every job. | AI is more likely to change jobs and assist workers than to replace all of them. |
| AI understands what it says. | Language models produce fluent text from learned patterns and can state falsehoods confidently (lesson 6.6). |

## Types of AI by functionality: reactive machines to self-aware [Competitive]

A second scheme classifies AI by **functionality**: what it can remember and understand. Many guides list four types, from simplest to most advanced:

| Type | What it can do | Exists today? | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Reactive machine** | Responds to the current input with fixed programming and keeps no memory of the past | Yes | IBM's Deep Blue chess computer |
| **Limited memory** | Uses recent observations and learned data to decide; most AI in use today | Yes | Self-driving cars tracking nearby vehicles; chatbots tracking a conversation |
| **Theory of mind** | Would understand people's emotions, beliefs, and intentions, and respond to them | No | None yet |
| **Self-aware** | Would have consciousness and a sense of its own existence | No | Purely hypothetical |

The two schemes measure different things, so every system gets one label from each. A self-driving car is narrow AI by capability and limited memory by functionality.

:::tip
**Test tip.** Match the clue to the type: "no memory, reacts to the present" is a reactive machine; "learns from data, uses recent history" is limited memory; "understands emotions and intentions" is theory of mind; "conscious of itself" is self-aware. Only the first two exist.
:::

## The Turing test

In 1950, Alan Turing published "Computing Machinery and Intelligence." It opens with the question "Can machines think?" and, finding that question too vague, replaces it with a game. In Turing's **imitation game**, an interrogator who communicates only in writing questions two hidden players and tries to tell them apart; then a machine takes one player's place. If the interrogator cannot reliably tell the machine from the human, the machine has played the game well. Today this is called the **Turing test**, usually in a simplified form: a judge holds text conversations with a hidden person and a hidden machine and must say which is which.

Three details are popular test material:

- Turing called it the imitation game. He never used the phrase "Turing test," which came later.
- He predicted that in about fifty years, computers would play so well that an average interrogator would have no more than a 70 percent chance of a correct identification after five minutes of questioning. He did not predict that machines would simply "think" by 2000.
- The test judges **behavior only**. A machine could pass by imitating conversation without understanding anything. The ELIZA program of the 1960s showed how easily people credit a simple program with understanding: it only matched keywords and filled in scripted replies, yet users responded as if it understood them.

## A timeline of milestones [Competitive]

History questions ask you to order events, match an event to its year, or match a person to a contribution. These are the milestones worth knowing:

| Year | Milestone | Who |
|---|---|---|
| 1950 | The imitation game, in "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" | Alan Turing |
| 1955 | The term "artificial intelligence" first appears, in a proposal for a summer workshop | John McCarthy (who chose the name), Marvin Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester, Claude Shannon |
| 1956 | The Dartmouth workshop, often called the birth of AI as a field | McCarthy and colleagues |
| 1958 | The perceptron, an artificial neuron that learns from examples | Frank Rosenblatt |
| 1959 | A checkers program that learned to beat its own programmer; the term "machine learning" spreads | Arthur Samuel, IBM |
| 1966 | ELIZA, an early program that holds typed conversations | Joseph Weizenbaum, MIT |
| 1970s | Expert systems such as MYCIN, which advised doctors on antibiotics | Stanford researchers |
| 1980 | XCON in regular use configuring computer orders at Digital Equipment Corporation | John McDermott |
| 1986 | Backpropagation popularized for training multilayer networks | Rumelhart, Hinton, Williams |
| 1997 | Deep Blue defeats world chess champion Garry Kasparov in a match | IBM |
| 2011 | Watson wins Jeopardy! against two champions | IBM |
| 2012 | AlexNet wins the ImageNet challenge, starting the deep learning boom | Krizhevsky, Sutskever, Hinton |
| 2016 | AlphaGo defeats Go champion Lee Sedol, 4 games to 1 | DeepMind |
| 2017 | The transformer, in "Attention Is All You Need" | Vaswani and colleagues |
| 2022 | ChatGPT released to the public (November 30) | OpenAI |

:::trap
**Common trap.** Several famous dates have near misses. The term "artificial intelligence" was coined in the 1955 proposal, not at the 1956 workshop. Deep Blue lost its first match with Kasparov in 1996, 4 to 2, and won the 1997 rematch, 3.5 to 2.5. ChatGPT (2022) came five years after the transformer (2017).
:::

## AI winters and why they happened [Champion]

AI's history is not a smooth climb. Twice, a burst of excitement was followed by an **AI winter**: a period of reduced funding and interest after promises ran ahead of results. The name itself is a warning. It first appeared in 1984 as the title of a panel at the annual AAAI conference, a major meeting of AI researchers, where Marvin Minsky and Roger Schank warned that inflated expectations would bring another collapse. So the first winter was named after it had ended.

**The first AI winter (about 1974 to 1980).** Early researchers made bold predictions, and programs that worked on small, simplified problems did not scale up to messy real-world ones. In 1969, Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert's book *Perceptrons* laid out the limits of single-layer perceptrons, and enthusiasm for neural networks faded for years. In the UK, Sir James Lighthill's report, published in 1973, criticized the field's progress and led to funding cuts and a loss of confidence. In the United States, the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA) cut back its support for open-ended AI research.

**Revival, then the second AI winter (from about 1987).** Expert systems brought AI back in the 1980s: XCON, for example, was in regular use at Digital Equipment Corporation by 1980. Companies bought specialized **Lisp machines**, computers built to run Lisp, the language much AI software was written in. Then, around 1987, cheaper general-purpose workstations caught up, and the Lisp machine market collapsed. Expert systems also proved expensive to maintain, unable to learn, and brittle outside their narrow rules, so they fell short of their hype. Funding and interest shrank again, into the 1990s.

Both winters followed the same cycle:

```figure
type: flow
steps: [Breakthrough and excitement, Promises outrun results, Disappointment, Funding and interest collapse, New methods revive the field]
cycle: true
caption: The boom-and-bust cycle behind both AI winters. Practical successes of machine learning, and later deep learning with large datasets and GPUs, brought the field back.
```

:::key
**Key idea.** Both winters came from the same gap: expectations ran far ahead of what the technology could deliver, so funding and interest collapsed when results disappointed. Neither came from a ban on AI or from a proof that machines cannot learn.
:::

:::takeaways
- AI builds systems that perform tasks that normally need human intelligence. Russell and Norvig's four approaches: thinking humanly, thinking rationally, acting humanly (the Turing test), and acting rationally (the rational agent, the book's focus).
- AI contains machine learning, which contains deep learning; today's generative AI sits inside deep learning. Symbolic AI uses readable rules and logic; connectionist AI learns with neural networks.
- By capability: narrow AI (ANI, the only kind that exists, generative models included), general AI (AGI, hypothetical), and superintelligence (ASI, theoretical).
- By functionality: reactive machines (no memory; Deep Blue), limited memory (most AI today), theory of mind (not built), and self-aware (hypothetical).
- Turing's 1950 imitation game judges behavior, not understanding, and he never called it the Turing test.
- Key dates: 1950 Turing, 1955 the term "AI," 1956 Dartmouth, 1958 perceptron, 1966 ELIZA, 1997 Deep Blue, 2011 Watson, 2012 AlexNet, 2016 AlphaGo, 2017 transformer, 2022 ChatGPT.
- AI winters (about 1974 to 1980, and from about 1987 into the 1990s) followed hype that outran results: the Lighthill report and funding cuts, then the Lisp machine collapse and brittle expert systems.
:::

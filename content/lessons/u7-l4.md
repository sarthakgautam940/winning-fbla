Lesson 7.3 built the language of predicate logic. This lesson puts it to work in the two ways tests ask about most: translating English sentences into logic, where one classic error catches almost everyone, and reasoning with logic, where valid rules of inference sit beside fallacies that look just as convincing. At the champion level come nested quantifiers and the two procedures automated reasoners actually run, unification and resolution.

## All, some, no: the four patterns

Almost every English sentence about groups of things fits one of four patterns. Learn their translations and most questions become pattern matching:

| English pattern | Predicate logic | Example |
|---|---|---|
| All A are B | $$\forall x\, (A(x) \rightarrow B(x))$$ | All squares are rectangles. |
| No A are B | $$\forall x\, (A(x) \rightarrow \neg B(x))$$ | No fish is a mammal. |
| Some A are B | $$\exists x\, (A(x) \land B(x))$$ | Some clubs meet on Mondays. |
| Some A are not B | $$\exists x\, (A(x) \land \neg B(x))$$ | Some students are not seniors. |

"No A are B" can also be written as "there is no x that is both A and B," $$\neg \exists x\, (A(x) \land B(x))$$, which means the same thing. Watch the word **only**: "Only seniors may vote" means anyone who votes is a senior, $$\forall x\, (\text{Votes}(x) \rightarrow \text{Senior}(x))$$, which reverses the direction that "all" suggests.

::::example[Translate two sentences]
Translate "Every Robotics member builds a robot" and "Some student has a GPA above 3.8."

:::step
The first is "All A are B," where A is being a Robotics member and B is building a robot. Use the universal quantifier with an implication: $$\forall x\, (\text{MemberOf}(x, \text{Robotics}) \rightarrow \text{BuildsRobot}(x))$$.
:::

:::step
The second is "Some A are B," where A is being a student and B is having a GPA above 3.8. Use the existential quantifier with AND: $$\exists x\, (\text{Student}(x) \land \text{GPA}(x) > 3.8)$$.
:::

:::answer
Universal sentences use $$\forall$$ with an implication; existential sentences use $$\exists$$ with AND. In the sample school database, the second sentence is true: Chloe and Hiro each have a GPA of 3.9.
:::
::::

## The classic trap: for-all with and

The most common translation error swaps the connectives:

| Sentence | Wrong translation | What the wrong version actually says | Right translation |
|---|---|---|---|
| All cats purr. | $$\forall x\, (\text{Cat}(x) \land \text{Purrs}(x))$$ | Everything in the universe is a cat, and everything purrs. | $$\forall x\, (\text{Cat}(x) \rightarrow \text{Purrs}(x))$$ |
| Some cat purrs. | $$\exists x\, (\text{Cat}(x) \rightarrow \text{Purrs}(x))$$ | Almost nothing: it is true as soon as anything at all is not a cat. | $$\exists x\, (\text{Cat}(x) \land \text{Purrs}(x))$$ |

The first error is far too strong, because a single dog makes it false. The second is far too weak, because of the vacuous truth of lesson 7.3: for any x that is not a cat, "if x is a cat, then x purrs" is true, so the existential sentence is satisfied even if no cat purrs.

:::key
**Key idea.** For all goes with an implication; there exists goes with AND. A universal claim talks only about the things that meet its condition, while an existential claim needs one thing that has both properties.
:::

## Nested quantifiers and order [Champion]

Sentences can stack quantifiers. When the quantifiers are of the same kind, their order does not matter: $$\forall x\, \forall y$$ means the same as $$\forall y\, \forall x$$, and likewise for two existentials. When they are mixed, the order changes the meaning. Let Likes(x, y) mean "x likes y":

- $$\forall x\, \exists y\, \text{Likes}(x, y)$$: for every person x, there is someone y whom x likes. Everyone likes somebody, but each person may like a different somebody.
- $$\exists y\, \forall x\, \text{Likes}(x, y)$$: there is one person y whom every x likes. A single person is liked by everyone.

Read the quantifiers from left to right: a variable introduced by "there exists" after "for all" may depend on the variable before it, while one introduced first must work for everything that follows. The second sentence implies the first, since if everyone likes one person, everyone likes somebody, but not the other way around.

## Negating quantified statements

To negate a quantified statement, move the negation inside, switch the quantifier, and negate what follows:

$$
\neg \forall x\, P(x) \equiv \exists x\, \neg P(x) \qquad \neg \exists x\, P(x) \equiv \forall x\, \neg P(x)
$$

"Not every club met this week" means "some club did not meet." "No club met" means "every club did not meet." Combined with lesson 7.3's rule for negating an implication, $$\neg(P \rightarrow Q) \equiv P \land \neg Q$$, these handle the four patterns:

| Statement | Its negation |
|---|---|
| All A are B: $$\forall x\, (A(x) \rightarrow B(x))$$ | Some A is not B: $$\exists x\, (A(x) \land \neg B(x))$$ |
| Some A are B: $$\exists x\, (A(x) \land B(x))$$ | No A is B: $$\forall x\, (A(x) \rightarrow \neg B(x))$$ |

:::trap
**Common trap.** The negation of "All students passed" is not "All students failed." One student who did not pass is enough to make the original false, so the negation is "At least one student did not pass." Likewise, the negation of "Some student failed" is "No student failed."
:::

## Modus ponens and modus tollens

**Inference** is the process of deriving new conclusions from known facts and rules by applying valid rules of reasoning. A rule of inference is **valid** when its conclusion must be true whenever its premises are true. Two rules do most of the work:

| Rule | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Modus ponens** | From $$P \rightarrow Q$$ and $$P$$, conclude $$Q$$. | If it is a school day, the bus runs. It is a school day. So the bus runs. |
| **Modus tollens** | From $$P \rightarrow Q$$ and $$\neg Q$$, conclude $$\neg P$$. | If it is a school day, the bus runs. The bus did not run. So it is not a school day. |

Chaining implications is also valid: from $$P \rightarrow Q$$ and $$Q \rightarrow R$$, conclude $$P \rightarrow R$$. A classic **syllogism** puts these ideas together with quantifiers. It has a **major premise**, a general rule; a **minor premise**, a specific or narrower case; and a **conclusion** that follows from both. AI4K12 uses this example:

- Major premise: all mammals are living things: $$\forall x\, (\text{Mammal}(x) \rightarrow \text{LivingThing}(x))$$.
- Minor premise: all humans are mammals: $$\forall x\, (\text{Human}(x) \rightarrow \text{Mammal}(x))$$.
- Conclusion: all humans are living things: $$\forall x\, (\text{Human}(x) \rightarrow \text{LivingThing}(x))$$.

Syllogisms like this are the logical deduction that AI reasoners use with taxonomies (lesson 7.2). An argument is **sound** when it is valid and all its premises are actually true: "All fish can fly; a salmon is a fish; so a salmon can fly" is valid but unsound.

## Fallacies that look valid [Competitive]

Two invalid forms look so much like the valid ones that they appear on tests constantly:

| Fallacy | Form | Example, with the same rule | Why it fails |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Affirming the consequent** | From $$P \rightarrow Q$$ and $$Q$$, conclude $$P$$. | The bus runs, so it must be a school day. | The bus might also run for summer school. |
| **Denying the antecedent** | From $$P \rightarrow Q$$ and $$\neg P$$, conclude $$\neg Q$$. | It is not a school day, so the bus does not run. | The rule says nothing about other days. |

A quick check: the valid rules either affirm the condition (modus ponens) or deny the result (modus tollens). The fallacies do the reverse, affirming the result or denying the condition.

Affirming the consequent is not useless, though. Reasoning from an observed effect to its likely cause, as a doctor does from symptoms to a disease, is called **abduction**. It gives plausible explanations rather than certain ones, which is why diagnosis is better handled with probability (lessons 7.6 and 7.7) than with strict logic.

## Unification and resolution [Champion]

Automated reasoners need mechanical procedures, and two are central.

**Unification** finds a substitution of terms for variables that makes two expressions identical. Advises(x, Robotics) and Advises(MsPark, y) unify with the substitution {x/MsPark, y/Robotics}, read "replace x with MsPark and y with Robotics." Both become Advises(MsPark, Robotics). Unification fails when two different constants must match: Advises(MsPark, Robotics) and Advises(MrReyes, z) cannot unify, because MsPark and MrReyes are different.

Unification is how a reasoner applies a general rule to specific facts. Given the rule $$\forall x\, ((\text{Student}(x) \land \text{Passed}(x)) \rightarrow \text{Certified}(x))$$ and the facts Student(Ava) and Passed(Ava), unifying x with Ava lets modus ponens conclude Certified(Ava).

**Resolution** is a single rule of inference that can prove anything that follows in logic. Facts and rules are first rewritten as **clauses**, ORs of possibly negated statements, using $$P \rightarrow Q \equiv \neg P \lor Q$$. When one clause contains a statement and another contains its negation, resolution cancels the pair and joins what is left: from $$P \lor Q$$ and $$\neg Q \lor R$$, conclude $$P \lor R$$.

Resolution proves goals by contradiction, a method called **refutation**: add the negation of the goal, and keep resolving until two clauses cancel completely, leaving the **empty clause**, a contradiction.

::::example[A proof by resolution]
The knowledge base says "If it rains, the field is wet" and "It rains." Prove "The field is wet."

:::step
Write everything as clauses: $$\neg \text{Rain} \lor \text{Wet}$$ and $$\text{Rain}$$. Add the negated goal: $$\neg \text{Wet}$$.
:::

:::step
Resolve Rain with $$\neg \text{Rain} \lor \text{Wet}$$: Rain and its negation cancel, leaving Wet.
:::

:::step
Resolve Wet with $$\neg \text{Wet}$$: they cancel completely, leaving the empty clause.
:::

:::answer
Assuming the field is not wet led to a contradiction, so the field is wet. AI4K12 names resolution theorem proving and IF-THEN rules as the algorithms for logical deduction problems.
:::
::::

:::takeaways
- Four patterns: All A are B is ∀x (A(x) → B(x)); No A are B is ∀x (A(x) → ¬B(x)); Some A are B is ∃x (A(x) ∧ B(x)); Some A are not B is ∃x (A(x) ∧ ¬B(x)). "Only A are B" means B implies A.
- For all goes with an implication; there exists goes with AND. For all with AND is far too strong, and there exists with an implication is almost always true.
- Same-kind quantifiers can be reordered; mixed ones cannot. ∀x ∃y means each x has its own y; ∃y ∀x means one y works for every x.
- To negate, switch the quantifier and negate the inside: ¬∀x P(x) ≡ ∃x ¬P(x), and ¬∃x P(x) ≡ ∀x ¬P(x). The negation of "all passed" is "some did not pass."
- Inference derives new conclusions from facts and rules. Valid: modus ponens (P → Q, P, so Q), modus tollens (P → Q, ¬Q, so ¬P), and chaining. A syllogism has a major premise, a minor premise, and a conclusion. Sound means valid with true premises.
- Fallacies: affirming the consequent (Q, so P) and denying the antecedent (¬P, so ¬Q). Abduction reasons from effects to likely causes and needs probability.
- Unification finds substitutions such as {x/MsPark}. Resolution cancels complementary pairs, and refutation adds the negated goal until it reaches the empty clause.
:::

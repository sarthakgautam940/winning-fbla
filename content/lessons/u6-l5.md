Knowing how an LLM works (lesson 6.4) tells you how to use one well. This lesson covers the levers you control: the prompt, the sampling settings that make answers focused or varied, the embeddings that let systems search by meaning, retrieval that grounds answers in real documents, and the tools and agents that let models act. The objectives ask you to discuss what LLMs can do, and these techniques are how those capabilities are put to work.

## Prompts: zero-shot, few-shot, chain-of-thought

A **prompt** is the input given to a generative AI model to produce a response: the instructions, the question, any context, and any examples you supply. A model's output depends entirely on what is in its context window, so the prompt is your main way to steer it.

- **Zero-shot prompting** asks for a task with no examples: "Classify this review as positive or negative."
- **Few-shot prompting** includes a few worked examples first, so the model can copy the pattern:

```text
Review: "Arrived broken and late." Sentiment: negative
Review: "Works perfectly, great value." Sentiment: positive
Review: "The battery died after two days." Sentiment:
```

The model continues with "negative." Learning a task from examples inside the prompt is called **in-context learning**, and OpenAI's 2020 GPT-3 paper showed how far it could go. It changes **no weights**: the examples shape only the current response, and the model stops using them once they leave the context window. That is the key difference from fine-tuning (lesson 6.4).

**Chain-of-thought prompting** shows or asks for intermediate reasoning steps before the final answer. Introduced by Jason Wei and colleagues at Google in 2022, it uses examples that work through problems step by step, and it improved accuracy on multi-step math word problems; simply asking a model to reason step by step is a common variant. It helps because each written step becomes context for the next prediction, giving the model room to work a problem out instead of jumping to a guess.

Many chat applications also use a **system prompt**: instructions set by the developer, usually hidden from users, that frame every conversation with the assistant's role, tone, rules, and limits. A **role prompt** asks the model to take a perspective, such as "You are a patient chemistry tutor." In general, strong prompts are specific about the task, the audience, the format, and the constraints, and they include the context the model needs.

| Weak prompt | Stronger prompt |
|---|---|
| "Write about climate." | "In 150 words, explain to ninth graders how greenhouse gases warm the Earth, with one everyday analogy." |

## Temperature and sampling

At each step, an LLM produces a probability for every possible next token. The way it picks one from that list is called **sampling**, and a few settings control it.

**Temperature** reshapes the probabilities before a token is drawn: the model's raw scores are divided by the temperature before the softmax (lesson 5.9).

- A **low temperature**, near 0, sharpens the distribution. The top token wins almost every time, so answers are focused, predictable, and repeatable. This suits factual answers, code, and extracting data.
- A temperature of 1 uses the model's probabilities as they are.
- A **high temperature** flattens the distribution. Unlikely tokens get real chances, so answers are more varied and creative, and more error-prone. This suits brainstorming and fiction.

For the prompt "The cat sat on the," a model might give "mat" a probability of 55.8 percent at temperature 1. At temperature 0.25, "mat" rises to 95.7 percent; at temperature 2, it falls to 38.6 percent, while "moon" climbs from 1.2 to 5.8 percent. The ranking never changes, only how peaked it is.

Try it: drag the temperature from 0.1 to 2 and watch the bars sharpen and flatten.

::widget{name="temperature-explorer"}

Two other settings cut off the unlikely tail before sampling:

- **Top-k sampling** keeps only the k most likely tokens. With k = 2, only "mat" and "floor" can be chosen.
- **Top-p sampling**, also called nucleus sampling, keeps the smallest set of top tokens whose probabilities add up to at least p. With p = 0.9, "mat" (0.558) plus "floor" (0.251) reaches only about 0.81, so "sofa" (0.138) joins and the total passes 0.9 at about 0.95. Those three are the candidates.

This is why the same prompt can produce different answers. Whenever the temperature is above 0, the model samples, so a different token can be drawn at any step, and one different word early on changes everything after it. Always taking the top token, called **greedy decoding**, makes outputs nearly repeatable.

## Embeddings and similarity [Competitive]

An **embedding** is a list of numbers, a vector, that represents the meaning of a piece of data such as a word, a sentence, a document, or an image, so that items with similar meanings get nearby vectors. Word embeddings became widely known with Google's word2vec models in 2013; today, embedding models turn whole passages into vectors with hundreds or thousands of numbers.

Closeness is usually measured with **cosine similarity**, the cosine of the angle between two vectors:

$$
\cos\theta = \frac{A \cdot B}{\lVert A \rVert \, \lVert B \rVert}
$$

The top is the **dot product**: multiply matching entries and add. The bottom multiplies the two vectors' lengths, where a length is the square root of the sum of the squared entries. Cosine similarity is 1 when two vectors point the same way, 0 when they are unrelated (perpendicular), and negative when they point in opposite directions.

::::example[Compute cosine similarity]
In a tiny made-up embedding, "cat" is (1, 2, 2), "kitten" is (2, 1, 2), and "tractor" is (2, -2, 1). Which word is closer to "cat"?

:::step
Dot product of cat and kitten: 1 × 2 + 2 × 1 + 2 × 2 = 8. Both vectors have length $$\sqrt{1 + 4 + 4} = 3$$.
:::

:::step
Cosine similarity of cat and kitten: 8 / (3 × 3) = 8/9, about 0.89.
:::

:::step
Dot product of cat and tractor: 1 × 2 + 2 × (-2) + 2 × 1 = 0, so their cosine similarity is 0.
:::

:::answer
"Kitten" is far closer to "cat" (about 0.89) than "tractor" is (0, unrelated). Real embeddings have hundreds of dimensions, but the arithmetic is the same.
:::
::::

Embeddings power **semantic search**, which matches meaning rather than exact words. A keyword search for "cheap flights" misses a page titled "inexpensive airfare," but a semantic search finds it, because the two phrases have nearby embeddings. A **vector database** stores large numbers of embeddings and quickly finds the ones nearest to a query's embedding. Embeddings also drive recommendations, grouping similar documents, and the retrieval step of the next section.

## Retrieval-augmented generation

An LLM knows only what was in its training data, up to its knowledge cutoff, and it cannot see a company's private documents. **Retrieval-augmented generation** (RAG) fixes this by looking things up first. The term comes from a 2020 paper by Patrick Lewis and colleagues at Facebook AI Research, University College London, and New York University. It works in three steps:

1. **Retrieve.** Turn the user's question into an embedding and search a vector database of documents for the most relevant passages.
2. **Augment.** Insert those passages into the prompt along with the question.
3. **Generate.** The LLM writes an answer based on the supplied passages, often citing them.

```figure
type: flow
steps: [User question, Search documents by embedding, Add top passages to the prompt, LLM writes a grounded answer]
caption: Retrieval-augmented generation. The model answers from documents placed in its context window, so it can use current or private information without retraining.
```

RAG reduces hallucination because the model can draw facts from real sources instead of reconstructing them from memory, and the sources can be cited and checked. It also keeps answers current: update the documents and the answers change, with no retraining. It is not a guarantee, though. If retrieval finds the wrong or outdated passages, or the model misreads the right ones, the answer can still be wrong.

## Tools, agents, and multimodal models [Competitive]

LLMs are weak at some jobs that ordinary software does perfectly, such as exact arithmetic, looking up today's information, or running code. **Tool use**, also called function calling, lets the model hand those jobs off. The model writes a structured request, such as "call the calculator with 1,847 × 362," the application runs the tool, and the result goes back into the context for the model to use.

An **AI agent** goes further. Given a goal, it works through a loop:

1. **Perceive**: take in the goal and the current situation.
2. **Plan**: decide on the next steps.
3. **Act**: use a tool, such as searching the web, running code, or sending a request.
4. **Observe**: read the result.
5. **Repeat** until the goal is met.

Systems that work this way are called **agentic AI**. An agent might research a topic across many sources and write a report, or find a bug in a program, test a fix, and submit it. Autonomy brings risk: a mistake early in the loop can compound, and an agent that can send emails or move money can do real harm. Careful systems limit an agent's permissions and ask a person to approve important actions (lesson 8.4).

**Multimodal models** take in or produce more than one kind of data: describing a photo, answering questions about a chart, generating an image from text, or holding a spoken conversation (lesson 6.3). Combined with tools, they let assistants act on what they see and hear.

:::takeaways
- A prompt is the input that steers a generative model. Zero-shot gives no examples; few-shot gives a few. Both are in-context learning, which changes no weights. Chain-of-thought prompting (2022) shows or requests step-by-step reasoning. A system prompt sets the role and rules.
- Low temperature gives focused, repeatable answers (near 0, nearly always the top token); high temperature gives varied, creative, more error-prone ones. Temperature changes how peaked the probabilities are, never their ranking.
- Top-k samples from the k likeliest tokens; top-p from the smallest set whose probabilities reach p. Answers vary because tokens are sampled.
- An embedding is a vector that represents meaning, with similar meanings nearby. Cosine similarity = dot product / (product of lengths): 1 means the same direction, 0 unrelated.
- Semantic search matches meaning, not keywords, and vector databases find the nearest embeddings fast.
- RAG retrieves relevant passages, adds them to the prompt, and generates a grounded answer. It reduces hallucination and adds current or private knowledge without retraining, but it is only as good as the documents it retrieves.
- Tool use lets a model call calculators, search, or code. Agents loop through perceive, plan, act, observe, and repeat, so they need limited permissions and human approval for important actions. Multimodal models work across text, images, and audio.
:::

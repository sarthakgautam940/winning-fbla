title: Data quality and where bad data comes from

Every model, chart, and conclusion is only as good as the data behind it. The objectives ask you to identify the factors that affect data quality, such as duplicates, low-quality sources, and incomplete datasets. Questions usually describe a problem and ask you to name it: which dimension of quality is damaged, which sampling method was used, or which bias crept into the collection. This lesson gives you the vocabulary to answer each one precisely.

## The dimensions of data quality

**Data quality** is how fit data is for its intended use. Quality is not one property but several, called dimensions. A widely used set comes from a 2013 white paper by DAMA UK (the UK chapter of the Data Management Association), and the UK government's Data Quality Framework adopted the same six core dimensions in 2020:

| Dimension | The question it asks | Example of a problem |
|---|---|---|
| **Accuracy** | Does the data match reality? | The system shows 40 units in stock, but the shelf holds 25. |
| **Completeness** | Are all the expected records and values present? | 30% of survey forms have no answer for income. |
| **Consistency** | Do values agree across records and systems? | An order totals $120 in the sales system and $102 in accounting. |
| **Timeliness** | Is the data up to date and available when it is needed? | A traffic app updates its road data once a week. |
| **Validity** | Is the data in the expected format and range? | An age of -4, or a date of February 30. |
| **Uniqueness** | Is each real-world item recorded only once? | The same customer appears three times under slightly different spellings. |

Other frameworks list more dimensions, but these six are the standard core.

:::trap
**Common trap.** Validity and accuracy are different. A value can be valid (the right format and a plausible range) and still be inaccurate. A phone number with ten correct-looking digits that belongs to someone else passes every validity check, yet it does not match reality. Validation rules catch impossible values; only comparing with the real world catches plausible wrong ones.
:::

## Factors that hurt quality: duplicates, low-quality sources, incomplete data

The three factors named in the objective each damage quality in a specific way.

**Duplicates** are repeated records of the same item. They often appear when two lists are merged or when a form is submitted twice. Duplicates inflate counts and totals (one customer counted three times), and they give the duplicated records extra weight in averages and in model training.

**Low-quality sources** produce data you cannot trust. Examples include anonymous websites, unverified user input, poorly calibrated sensors, outdated reports, and secondary data collected for a different purpose. Before using a source, check its **provenance**: who collected the data, how, when, and why. A dataset with unknown provenance should be treated with suspicion, however neat it looks.

**Incomplete datasets** are missing values or whole groups of records. Missing values shrink the usable sample and can bias results if the missing cases differ from the rest. Missing records are often worse, because nothing in the data warns you: a health dataset with no records from rural clinics may produce conclusions that simply do not apply to rural patients.

Other common causes of poor quality include typing errors during data entry, inconsistent units or formats (pounds in one file and kilograms in another), measurement error, data that has gone out of date, badly worded survey questions, and errors introduced when systems are combined. Lesson 3.5 shows how to fix many of these problems.

## Sampling methods

Most data comes from a **sample**, a part of the population (lesson 2.6). The list the sample is drawn from is the **sampling frame**. How the sample is chosen decides whether it can represent the population.

**Probability samples** use chance to choose individuals:

- A **simple random sample** gives every possible group of n individuals the same chance of being chosen, as if drawing names from a hat.
- A **stratified random sample** divides the population into groups that share a trait, called **strata** (such as grade levels), then takes a separate random sample from each stratum. It guarantees that every group is represented.
- A **cluster sample** divides the population into groups, usually by location (such as classrooms or neighborhoods), randomly selects some whole clusters, and includes everyone in the chosen clusters. It is cheaper when the population is spread out.
- A **systematic sample** chooses every kth individual from a list, starting at a random point.

**Nonprobability samples** do not use chance and are prone to bias:

- A **convenience sample** includes whoever is easiest to reach, such as shoppers at one mall entrance.
- A **voluntary response sample** includes only people who choose to respond, such as an online poll. People with strong opinions are far more likely to take part.

:::tip
**Test tip.** Stratified sampling takes **some individuals from every group**; cluster sampling takes **every individual from some groups**. If the stem says "randomly selects 5 of the 40 schools and surveys all teachers in them," it is a cluster sample.
:::

::::example[Name the sampling method]
Identify each method: (1) A school picks 20 students at random from each grade. (2) A pollster picks 10 city blocks at random and interviews every household on them. (3) A website posts a poll and counts every reply. (4) A factory tests every 50th item off the line, starting at a random item.

:::step
Plan 1 samples randomly within every grade, a group that shares a trait, so it is a stratified random sample.
:::

:::step
Plan 2 selects whole groups at random and includes everyone in them, so it is a cluster sample.
:::

:::step
Plan 3 lets people decide whether to respond, so it is a voluntary response sample.
:::

:::step
Plan 4 takes every kth item after a random start, so it is a systematic sample.
:::

:::answer
Stratified, cluster, voluntary response, systematic.
:::
::::

## Collection biases: selection, survivorship, nonresponse, and more [Competitive]

A **bias** is a systematic error: something in how data is collected pushes the results consistently in one direction. Random noise averages out over many observations; bias does not. The test describes a study and asks you to name its bias.

- **Selection bias**: the way individuals are chosen makes the sample unrepresentative. One common form is **undercoverage**, when part of the population has little or no chance of being included. A survey drawn from a list of landline numbers undercovers people who use only cell phones.
- **Survivorship bias**: analyzing only the cases that survived some selection process and ignoring those that did not. A study of 50 successful companies that finds most founders took big risks ignores the many failed companies whose founders took the same risks.
- **Nonresponse bias**: people chosen for the sample do not respond, and the nonresponders differ from those who do. If a mailed survey comes back mostly from retirees, the results describe retirees, not the town.
- **Voluntary response bias**: respondents select themselves, so strong opinions are overrepresented.
- **Response bias**: respondents give inaccurate answers. Causes include **leading questions** ("Don't you agree the park is a waste of money?"), the wish to give socially approved answers, and the presence of an interviewer.
- **Measurement bias**: the measuring tool itself is off, such as a scale that always reads 2 pounds heavy.

These collection biases are one of the main ways AI systems inherit bias, the topic of lesson 8.2.

:::trap
**Common trap.** Nonresponse and voluntary response are different. In nonresponse, the researcher chose the people, but some did not answer. In voluntary response, the researcher chose no one; the people chose themselves.
:::

::check{id="sampling-bias-02"}

## A bigger biased sample is still biased [Competitive]

Collecting more data reduces **random error** (variability): the standard error shrinks with the square root of n (lesson 2.6). But more data does nothing about **bias**. If the collection method systematically misses or distorts part of the population, a bigger sample just gives a more precise wrong answer.

| | Low variability | High variability |
|---|---|---|
| **Low bias** | Accurate and precise: the goal | Centered on the truth, but noisy |
| **High bias** | Precisely wrong, like a huge biased sample | Off target and noisy |

An online poll with 200,000 self-selected responses is less trustworthy than a well-run random sample of 1,000 adults, because the poll's voluntary response bias does not shrink as responses pile up.

To fix a biased sample, change the method, not the size:

- select at random from a sampling frame that covers the whole population;
- follow up with people who did not respond, and keep surveys short and neutral;
- stratify so every important group is represented;
- reweight the responses so each group counts in proportion to its share of the population, when some groups are underrepresented.

:::key
**Key idea.** Sample size controls variability. Sampling method controls bias. Only good methods produce representative data.
:::

:::takeaways
- Data quality is fitness for use. DAMA UK's six core dimensions: accuracy, completeness, consistency, timeliness, validity, and uniqueness.
- Valid means the right format and range; accurate means matching reality. A value can be valid but wrong.
- Duplicates inflate counts and overweight records; low-quality sources need a provenance check; incomplete data shrinks samples and can bias results, especially when whole groups are missing.
- Probability samples: simple random, stratified (some from every group), cluster (all from some groups), and systematic (every kth). Convenience and voluntary response samples are prone to bias.
- Biases: selection (including undercoverage), survivorship, nonresponse, voluntary response, response (such as leading questions), and measurement.
- A bigger sample reduces variability, not bias. Fix bias by improving the method: random selection from a complete frame, follow-up, stratification, and weighting.
:::

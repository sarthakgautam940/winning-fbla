Probability is how data science talks about uncertainty. A spam filter estimates the chance an email is junk, a doctor weighs the chance a positive test is real, and every AI system that reasons under uncertainty (Unit 7) runs on the rules in this lesson. The test asks short, sharp questions here: a probability from a table, a complement, an "and" or an "or", a conditional probability, and Bayes' theorem with a base rate. Each takes seconds once you know which rule applies.

## Probability as a number from 0 to 1

An **experiment** is any process with an uncertain result, such as rolling a die. Each possible result is an **outcome**, the set of all outcomes is the **sample space**, and an **event** is a set of outcomes you care about, such as "roll an even number."

The **probability** of an event A, written P(A), is a number from 0 to 1. A probability of 0 means the event cannot happen, 1 means it is certain, and 0.5 means it is as likely as not. The probabilities of all the outcomes in a sample space add up to exactly 1.

When every outcome is equally likely, probability is a count:

$$
P(A) = \frac{\text{number of outcomes in } A}{\text{total number of outcomes}}
$$

A fair die has 6 equally likely outcomes, and 3 of them (2, 4, and 6) are even, so P(even) = 3/6 = 1/2.

Two dice give 6 × 6 = 36 equally likely ordered outcomes. Rolling (1, 6) and rolling (6, 1) are different outcomes, so a sum of 7 happens 6 ways: (1, 6), (2, 5), (3, 4), (4, 3), (5, 2), and (6, 1). P(sum of 7) = 6/36 = 1/6, which makes 7 the most likely sum. The 11 possible sums, 2 through 12, are not equally likely: a sum of 2 happens only one way.

**Theoretical probability** comes from reasoning about equally likely outcomes, like the 1/2 above. **Experimental probability** (also called empirical probability or relative frequency) comes from data: if 29 of 50 flips land heads, the experimental probability of heads is 29/50 = 0.58. By the law of large numbers (lesson 2.6), the experimental probability tends to approach the theoretical one as the number of trials grows.

:::trap
**Common trap.** The law of large numbers is about proportions in the long run. It does not make a coin "due" for heads after a streak of tails; each flip is still 50-50. Believing in that correction is called the **gambler's fallacy**. The law also does not promise that the count of heads lands exactly on half the flips. Only the proportion settles.
:::

**Odds** express the same information as a ratio of the chances for an event to the chances against it:

$$
\text{odds in favor of } A = \frac{P(A)}{1 - P(A)}
$$

An event with probability 0.2 has odds of 0.2 to 0.8, which simplifies to 1 to 4. Going the other way, odds of a to b in favor mean P(A) = a / (a + b), so odds of 3 to 2 mean a probability of 3/5 = 0.6. Odds of 1 to 4 do not mean a probability of 1/4: the 1 chance for and the 4 against make 5 chances in all. Odds come back in logistic regression (lesson 3.6).

## Complements and "at least one"

The **complement** of an event A, written "not A," is every outcome in the sample space that is not in A. Because A and not A together cover everything:

$$
P(\text{not } A) = 1 - P(A)
$$

If the chance of rain is 0.3, the chance of no rain is 0.7.

The complement's best use is the phrase **at least one**. "At least one" covers many cases (exactly one, exactly two, and so on), but its complement is a single case: none. So:

$$
P(\text{at least one}) = 1 - P(\text{none})
$$

::::example[At least one six]
You roll a fair die 4 times. What is the probability of rolling at least one six?

:::step
The complement of "at least one six" is "no sixes in 4 rolls."
:::

:::step
Each roll misses a six with probability 5/6, and the rolls are independent, so P(no sixes) = $$(5/6)^4 = 625/1{,}296$$, about 0.482.
:::

:::step
Subtract from 1: 1 - 0.482 = 0.518.
:::

:::answer
The probability of at least one six is about 0.518, slightly better than even.
:::
::::

:::trap
**Common trap.** Do not add the chances. 1/6 + 1/6 + 1/6 + 1/6 = 4/6 counts every set of rolls with two or more sixes more than once. With seven rolls, adding would give a "probability" greater than 1.
:::

::check{id="prob-basics-02"}

## Addition rule: mutually exclusive or not

In probability, "A or B" means at least one of the two events happens, possibly both. Suppose 50% of a school's students take a science elective, 35% take an art elective, and 15% take both. If you add 50% and 35%, you count the 15% who take both twice. Subtract them once:

$$
P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \text{ and } B)
$$

So P(science or art) = 50% + 35% - 15% = 70%. This is the **addition rule**.

```figure

caption: Adding both circles counts the overlap twice, so the addition rule subtracts it once.

  - { label: Science elective }
  - { label: Art elective }

```

Events are **mutually exclusive** (or **disjoint**) when they cannot happen together, such as rolling a 1 and rolling a 6 on the same roll, or a student being both a freshman and a senior. Then P(A and B) = 0, and the rule simplifies to adding: P(roll a 1 or a 6) = 1/6 + 1/6 = 1/3.

::check{id="prob-rules-01"}

## Multiplication rule: independent or not

"A and B" means both events happen. The probability that both happen, P(A and B), is called a **joint probability**.

Two events are **independent** when knowing that one happened does not change the probability of the other. Coin flips are independent: the coin has no memory. For independent events, multiply:

$$
P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A) \times P(B)
$$

The probability of heads on two flips is 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4.

When events are **dependent**, the second probability must account for the first:

$$
P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A) \times P(B \mid A)
$$

Here $$P(B \mid A)$$, read "B given A," is the probability of B once A has happened. Drawing **without replacement** (not putting an item back) makes draws dependent, because each draw changes what remains. Drawing **with replacement** keeps them independent.

::::example[Drawing without replacement]
A bag holds 4 red and 6 blue marbles. You draw 2 without putting the first one back. What is the probability that both are red?

:::step
First draw: 4 of the 10 marbles are red, so P(first red) = 4/10.
:::

:::step
Second draw: if the first was red, 3 red marbles remain among 9, so P(second red | first red) = 3/9.
:::

:::step
Multiply: 4/10 × 3/9 = 12/90 = 2/15, about 0.133.
:::

:::answer
P(both red) = 2/15, about 0.133. With replacement, the draws would be independent and the answer would be 4/10 × 4/10 = 0.16.
:::
::::

**Mutually exclusive and independent sound similar but are nearly opposites.** Mutually exclusive is about overlap: can the events happen together? Independent is about influence: does one change the chance of the other? If two events with positive probabilities are mutually exclusive, then the moment one happens, the other's probability drops to 0. That is the strongest possible influence, so mutually exclusive events are never independent.

:::key
**Key idea.** "Or" adds (and subtracts the overlap). "And" multiplies (using P(B | A) when the events are dependent). Mutually exclusive events with positive probabilities are always dependent.
:::

::check{id="prob-rules-03"}

## Counting outcomes [Competitive]

To use "favorable over total," you often need to count outcomes. Three tools cover the test:

- The **multiplication principle**: if one choice has m options and a second has n, there are m × n combinations in all. A sandwich shop with 3 breads and 5 fillings offers 3 × 5 = 15 sandwiches, and a code of 4 letters from A to Z has $$26^4 = 456{,}976$$ possibilities.
- **Permutations** count arrangements where **order matters**. Choosing a president and a vice president from 5 people gives 5 × 4 = 20 possibilities, because Ana as president with Ben as vice president differs from the reverse. In general, arranging k of n items gives $$\frac{n!}{(n-k)!}$$.
- **Combinations** count selections where **order does not matter**. Choosing a 2-person committee from 5 people gives 20 / 2 = 10, because the permutation count lists each pair twice. In general, $$C(n, k) = \frac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!}$$.

The **factorial** n! means n × (n - 1) × ... × 2 × 1, so 5! = 120, and by definition 0! = 1. Combinations return in lesson 2.8, where they count the ways to get k successes in n trials.

:::tip
**Test tip.** Ask whether swapping two chosen items gives a different result. Positions, rankings, and passwords: order matters, so use permutations. Teams, committees, and hands of cards: order does not matter, so use combinations.
:::

::check{id="prob-rules-05"}

## Conditional probability and two-way tables

A **conditional probability** is the probability of an event given that another event has occurred. $$P(A \mid B)$$, read "the probability of A given B," restricts attention to the cases where B happened:

$$
P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \text{ and } B)}{P(B)}
$$

Two-way tables make this concrete. A school tracked 200 students who did or did not take a test-prep course:

| | Passed | Failed | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Took the course | 60 | 20 | 80 |
| Did not take it | 70 | 50 | 120 |
| Total | 130 | 70 | 200 |

- P(passed) = 130/200 = 0.65, using the whole table.
- P(passed | took the course) = 60/80 = 0.75, using only the "took the course" row.
- P(took the course | passed) = 60/130, about 0.46, using only the "passed" column.

The event after "given" names the group you restrict to, and that group's total is the denominator.

:::trap
**Common trap.** $$P(A \mid B)$$ and $$P(B \mid A)$$ are different numbers. Here P(passed | took the course) is 0.75, but P(took the course | passed) is about 0.46. Swapping them is one of the most common errors in probability, and test writers build distractors from it.
:::

**Testing independence.** Events A and B are independent exactly when $$P(A \mid B) = P(A)$$, or equivalently when P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B). In the table, P(passed | took the course) = 0.75 but P(passed) = 0.65, so passing and taking the course are not independent: knowing a student took the course changes the chance that they passed. That is an association, not proof that the course caused the passing (lesson 3.3 explains why).

**Tree diagrams** organize events that happen in stages. Multiply along the branches to get the probability of each path, then add the paths that end in the outcome you want. Suppose it rains on 30% of days, a bus runs late on 40% of rainy days, and it runs late on 10% of dry days:

```figure

```

Adding the paths this way is the **law of total probability**: P(late) = 0.3 × 0.4 + 0.7 × 0.1 = 0.12 + 0.07 = 0.19.

## Bayes' theorem and base rates [Competitive]

Often you know P(evidence | cause) but need P(cause | evidence). A test's accuracy tells you P(positive | disease), but a patient wants P(disease | positive). **Bayes' theorem** turns one into the other:

$$
P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A) \times P(A)}{P(B)}
$$

Each piece has a name:

- The **prior**, P(A), is the probability of the hypothesis before seeing the evidence. For a disease it is the **base rate**, the share of people who have it.
- The **likelihood**, $$P(B \mid A)$$, is the probability of the evidence if the hypothesis is true.
- The **posterior**, $$P(A \mid B)$$, is the updated probability after seeing the evidence.
- The **evidence**, P(B), is the overall probability of seeing the evidence. Find it with the tree method: P(B) = P(B | A) P(A) + P(B | not A) P(not A).

The bus tree shows the theorem at work. If the bus was late, the chance it rained is the rain path's share of all late paths: 0.12 / 0.19, about 0.63. Lateness raised the probability of rain from the prior of 0.3 to a posterior of 0.63.

::check{id="bayes-01"}

The formula is easy to misuse under time pressure. The **natural frequency method** is faster and safer: imagine a round number of people, split them by the base rate, then by how the test behaves, and count.

::::example[A positive test for a rare condition]
A condition affects 1% of people. A test is positive for 95% of people who have the condition (its sensitivity) and for 5% of people who do not (its false positive rate). A person tests positive. What is the probability that they have the condition?

:::step
Imagine 10,000 people. At 1%, 100 have the condition and 9,900 do not.
:::

:::step
Of the 100 with the condition, 95% test positive: 95 true positives.
:::

:::step
Of the 9,900 without it, 5% test positive anyway: 495 false positives.
:::

:::step
Of all 95 + 495 = 590 positive tests, 95 are real: 95 / 590 = 0.161.
:::

:::answer
Only about 16% of the people who test positive have the condition, even though the test catches 95% of cases.
:::
::::

The answer is low because the condition is rare. The healthy group is huge, so 5% of it (495 people) outnumbers 95% of the small sick group (95 people). Ignoring the prior like this is the **base rate fallacy**. The same test in a group where 20% have the condition gives 1,900 true positives and 400 false positives out of 10,000, so a positive result there means about an 83% chance.

Try it: drag the prevalence down from 20% toward 1% and watch the share of real cases among the positives collapse, while the test itself stays exactly the same.

::widget{name="bayes-explorer"}

The same reasoning powers spam filters. If 40% of email is spam and the word "free" appears in 30% of spam but only 2% of legitimate email, then out of 1,000 emails, 400 are spam (120 contain "free") and 600 are not (12 contain "free"). P(spam | "free") = 120 / 132, about 0.91. A **naive Bayes** classifier (lesson 5.8) combines many words this way, and Bayesian networks (lesson 7.7) chain many such updates together.

Bayes' theorem can also be applied again and again. When new evidence arrives that is independent of the old, today's posterior becomes tomorrow's prior: run the same calculation, starting from the updated probability.

:::tip
**Test tip.** For any "given a positive result" question, sketch four numbers out of 1,000 or 10,000: true positives, false negatives, false positives, true negatives. The answer is true positives divided by all positives.
:::

::check{id="bayes-02"}

:::takeaways
- Probability runs from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain). With equally likely outcomes, P = favorable / total. Two dice have 36 outcomes, and P(sum of 7) = 1/6.
- Odds in favor = P / (1 - P). Odds of a to b mean P = a / (a + b), so 1 to 4 is a probability of 1/5.
- P(not A) = 1 - P(A), and P(at least one) = 1 - P(none). Never add the chances for "at least one."
- Or: P(A) + P(B) - P(A and B). Subtract the overlap unless the events are mutually exclusive.
- And: P(A) × P(B) if independent, P(A) × P(B | A) if dependent. Drawing without replacement makes events dependent.
- Mutually exclusive events with positive probabilities are never independent.
- Counting: multiply the choices; use permutations when order matters and combinations when it does not.
- P(A | B) = P(A and B) / P(B). The group after "given" is the denominator, and P(A | B) is not P(B | A).
- Bayes: posterior = likelihood × prior / evidence. With a low base rate, most positive results can be false, so count it out with 1,000 or 10,000 imaginary people.
:::

title: How large language models work
---

Chatbots can feel like magic, but they run on a few ideas you can learn in one sitting: text broken into tokens, a context window that limits what the model can see, a transformer that relates every token to every other, and a training pipeline that runs from next-token prediction to human feedback. The objectives ask you to define large language models and discuss what they can do. This lesson covers how they work; lessons 6.5 and 6.6 cover how to use them well and where they fail.

## What an LLM is

A **large language model** (LLM) is a neural network, almost always a transformer, trained on enormous amounts of text to predict the next token, which lets it understand and generate human language. From that one skill, learned at scale, come many abilities: answering questions, summarizing, translating, writing and explaining code, and holding a conversation.

"Large" refers to scale in three senses:

- **Parameters**: billions of learned weights. GPT-3, described in 2020, had 175 billion.
- **Data**: training text drawn from huge collections of web pages, books, articles, and code.
- **Compute**: enormous amounts of computing power to train.

LLMs are foundation models (lesson 6.3): one model, trained once on broad data, then adapted to many tasks.

A model fresh from pretraining is a **base model**. It is excellent at continuing text, which is not the same as following instructions: give it "What is the capital of Peru?" and it may continue with "What is the capital of Chile?" as if writing a quiz. A **chat model**, the kind behind assistants, is a base model trained further to follow instructions and hold a conversation, as the last section of this lesson shows. ChatGPT, released on November 30, 2022, was fine-tuned in this way from a GPT-3.5-series model.

## Tokens and tokenization

An LLM does not read letters or whole words. **Tokenization** splits text into **tokens**, the units a model reads and writes. A token may be a whole common word, a piece of a longer or rarer word, a number, or a punctuation mark; one tokenizer might split "unbelievably" into "un," "believ," and "ably," for example, though the exact pieces depend on the tokenizer. Each token becomes an ID number, and each ID becomes a vector, an embedding (lesson 6.5), that the network processes. GPT models build their vocabulary of pieces with **byte-pair encoding** (BPE), a method that merges frequently occurring character sequences into single tokens.

For English text, OpenAI's rule of thumb is:

$$
1 \text{ token} \approx 4 \text{ characters} \approx 0.75 \text{ words}
$$

So 100 tokens is about 75 words, and one word is about 1.33 tokens. This is only an average for English. Each model family has its own tokenizer, so the same text gives different counts in different models, and other languages often need more tokens.

::::example[Estimate a token count]
About how many tokens is a 1,500-word report?

:::step
One token is about 0.75 words, so divide the word count by 0.75: 1,500 / 0.75 = 2,000.
:::

:::step
Check with the other form of the rule: 1,500 words × 1.33 tokens per word is also about 2,000.
:::

:::answer
About 2,000 tokens. Dividing by 0.75 and multiplying by 1.33 give the same estimate.
:::
::::

Tokens matter in practice. Usage limits and prices are counted in tokens, and because the model sees chunks rather than letters, tasks about individual letters, such as counting the letters in a long word or spelling it backward, are surprisingly error-prone.

## Context windows

The **context window** is the maximum number of tokens a model can consider at one time. Everything counts against it: the system's instructions, the whole conversation so far, any pasted documents, and the model's own reply as it writes. So the window limits how long a document the model can read in one go, how much of a conversation it can keep in view, and how long its answer can be.

When a conversation outgrows the window, something has to give. Applications usually drop or summarize the oldest messages so the newest fit, and an input that is too long is cut off or rejected. Anything outside the window is invisible to the model, which is why a chatbot can "forget" an instruction given early in a long chat. Starting a fresh conversation, or repeating the key instructions, fixes it.

Larger windows let a model read whole books or code bases at once, but they cost more: in a standard transformer, attention compares every token with every other token, so doubling the input roughly quadruples that part of the work.

::::example[Budget a context window]
A model's context window is 4,096 tokens. You paste in a 1,200-word article. About how many tokens remain for your instructions and the reply?

:::step
Convert the article to tokens: 1,200 / 0.75 = 1,600 tokens.
:::

:::step
Subtract from the window: 4,096 - 1,600 = 2,496.
:::

:::answer
About 2,500 tokens remain for the instructions and the reply. If the reply needs more than that, the application must drop or shorten something.
:::
::::

## The transformer and self-attention [Competitive]

The **transformer** is the neural network architecture behind modern LLMs, introduced in the 2017 paper "Attention Is All You Need" by Ashish Vaswani and seven co-authors, mostly at Google. It processes all the tokens of a sequence at once and uses self-attention to relate every token to every other token.

```figure
type: flow
steps: [Tokens, Embeddings plus positions, Stacked attention layers, Next-token probabilities]
caption: Inside a GPT-style transformer. Each layer mixes information between tokens with attention and then processes each token further; the final layer gives a probability for every possible next token.
```

**Self-attention** lets each token look at all the other tokens in the context, decide how much each one matters for its own meaning, and blend in their information. In "The bank raised its interest rates," attention from "bank" to "interest rates" pulls its meaning toward money rather than a riverbank. Attention connects words directly, however far apart they are.

How does a token decide what to attend to? For each token, the model computes three vectors:

- a **query**: what this token is looking for;
- a **key**: what this token offers, to be matched against other tokens' queries;
- a **value**: the information this token passes along.

A token's query is compared with every token's key (a scaled dot product), and a softmax turns the scores into **attention weights** that sum to 1. The token's new representation is the weighted sum of the values. It works like a search: the query is what you are searching for, the keys are the labels you match against, and the values are the content you collect. Transformers run several of these at once, called **multi-head attention**, so different heads can track different relationships, such as grammar in one head and pronoun references in another.

Two more details complete the picture. Attention compares tokens regardless of where they sit, so on its own it would treat "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" alike; **positional encodings** add each token's position to its vector, giving the model a sense of order. And because no step waits for the one before it, all positions are computed in parallel, which is why transformers train so much faster than recurrent networks on GPUs (lesson 5.10).

The original transformer had two halves, and today's models use one or both:

| Design | How it reads | Best at | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Encoder-only | Sees the whole input in both directions at once | Understanding: classification, search | BERT (Google, 2018) |
| Decoder-only | Each token sees only the tokens before it | Generating text one token at a time | GPT models |
| Encoder-decoder | The encoder reads the input; the decoder writes the output | Turning one sequence into another, such as translation | The original 2017 transformer |

A decoder is trained to predict the next token, so each position is **masked** from attending to the tokens after it; otherwise it could simply look at the answer. BERT, by contrast, was pretrained by hiding random words and predicting them from the context on both sides.

## Pretraining: predicting the next token

Training an LLM happens in stages. The first, **pretraining**, is by far the largest: the model reads an enormous collection of text and, at every position, learns to predict the next token. Its loss is the cross-entropy of lesson 5.7, the negative log of the probability it gave to the token that actually came next.

Pretraining is **self-supervised**: the text supplies its own labels. In "The cat sat on the mat," the model practices predicting "cat" from "The," then "sat" from "The cat," and so on. Every token after the first is the label for the tokens before it, so a text of n tokens gives n - 1 predictions. No person labels anything, which is what makes training on such vast collections possible.

To predict the next token well across so much text, the model must absorb grammar, facts, styles, and patterns of reasoning. It also absorbs the errors, stereotypes, and biases in that text, and its knowledge stops at its training data's **knowledge cutoff** (lesson 6.6). The result is a base model: knowledgeable and fluent, but not yet an assistant.

## Fine-tuning, instruction tuning, and RLHF [Competitive]

**Fine-tuning** is further training of a pretrained model on a smaller, focused dataset. It changes the model's weights so the model specializes, for example in medical notes, legal contracts, or a company's support style, and it costs far less than pretraining because the model already knows the language. Prompting (lesson 6.5), by contrast, steers a model without changing any weights.

Turning a base model into a helpful assistant takes two kinds of fine-tuning:

1. **Instruction tuning**, a form of supervised fine-tuning. The model trains on many examples of requests paired with good responses, often written by people, and learns to answer, follow directions, and keep a conversational format.
2. **Reinforcement learning from human feedback** (RLHF). The model writes several answers to the same prompt, and people rank them. A separate **reward model** learns to predict those rankings. Then reinforcement learning (lesson 5.3) adjusts the LLM to produce answers the reward model scores highly, so the model keeps improving without a person rating every answer.

```figure
type: flow
steps: [Pretraining on huge text, Instruction tuning on demonstrations, Reward model from human rankings, Reinforcement learning, Chat assistant]
caption: From base model to assistant. Pretraining supplies most of the knowledge; the later steps shape behavior so the model follows instructions and answers helpfully.
```

The approach grew from 2017 work by Paul Christiano and colleagues, who trained agents from human comparisons of their behavior. OpenAI's InstructGPT (2022) applied it to language, and human evaluators preferred answers from a 1.3-billion-parameter InstructGPT over those of the 175-billion-parameter GPT-3, a model more than 100 times larger. ChatGPT was trained in much the same way.

:::trap
**Common trap.** RLHF does not mainly add knowledge; pretraining does that. Instruction tuning and RLHF shape how the model behaves: following instructions, answering helpfully, and declining harmful requests.
:::

:::takeaways
- An LLM is a neural network, usually a transformer, trained on huge amounts of text to predict the next token. "Large" means billions of parameters (GPT-3 had 175 billion), vast data, and heavy computing. LLMs are foundation models.
- A base model continues text; a chat model is tuned further to follow instructions. ChatGPT (2022) was fine-tuned from a GPT-3.5-series model.
- Tokenization splits text into tokens: words, word pieces, or symbols. For English, 1 token is about 4 characters or 0.75 words, so tokens = words / 0.75. Counts differ by tokenizer.
- The context window is the most tokens a model can consider at once, counting instructions, history, documents, and the reply. When it fills, old text is dropped and the model loses track of it.
- The transformer (2017) processes all tokens in parallel with self-attention: queries match keys to weight values, the weights sum to 1, multiple heads track different relationships, and positional encodings supply word order.
- Encoder-only models (BERT) read in both directions for understanding; decoder-only models (GPT) predict each next token from earlier ones, with later tokens masked.
- Pretraining is self-supervised next-token prediction: n tokens give n - 1 predictions, with no human labels.
- Fine-tuning changes weights on focused data. Instruction tuning uses demonstrations; RLHF trains a reward model on human rankings, then optimizes the LLM against it. InstructGPT (1.3 billion parameters) was preferred over GPT-3 (175 billion).
:::

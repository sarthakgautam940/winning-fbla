title: The normal distribution and z-scores

Heights, measurement errors, standardized test scores, and the averages of almost any large samples all pile up in the same bell shape: the **normal distribution**, also called the **Gaussian distribution** after the mathematician Carl Friedrich Gauss. The official objective asks you to "discuss the characteristics and importance of Gaussian (normal) distribution." On the test that means knowing its shape and properties, using the 68-95-99.7 rule, computing z-scores, and turning z-scores into percentiles. This lesson builds all four skills.

## The shape and its two parameters

A normal distribution is a smooth, **bell-shaped** curve with these properties:

- It is **symmetric** around its center, with a single peak.
- Its **mean, median, and mode are all equal**, at the center.
- It is **continuous**: values can fall anywhere on the number line.
- Its two **tails** stretch out forever in both directions, getting closer and closer to the horizontal axis without ever touching it.
- The **total area under the curve is 1**, and the area over any interval is the probability of a value landing there.

```figure
type: normal
caption: A normal curve, marked in standard deviations from the mean. It is symmetric, and its tails never touch the axis.
axis: sd
```

Just two numbers define any normal distribution: the **mean** $$\mu$$, which sets the center, and the **standard deviation** $$\sigma$$, which sets the spread. It is written $$N(\mu, \sigma)$$. (Some books write $$N(\mu, \sigma^2)$$ with the variance instead; read the notation carefully.) Changing the mean slides the curve left or right. A larger standard deviation makes the curve wider and flatter, because the total area must stay 1; a smaller one makes it narrower and taller.

Because probability is area, the chance of landing on one exact value is 0: a single point has no width. For a continuous variable you always ask about intervals, such as "between 60 and 70" or "above 80."

## Why the normal distribution matters, including in AI

The normal distribution earns its central place for three reasons.

1. **Many real measurements are approximately normal**: heights, weights within a group, errors in measurement, scores on large standardized tests.
2. **Averages become normal.** The central limit theorem (lesson 2.6) says that means of large samples are approximately normal even when the original data are not. That is why so much of statistical inference rests on the normal curve.
3. **Many methods assume it.** Confidence intervals, many hypothesis tests, and the residuals of linear regression are built on normal assumptions.

It shows up all over machine learning as well:

- **Weight initialization**: neural networks usually start with small random weights drawn from a normal distribution.
- **Noise models**: many models assume measurement errors are normal; diffusion models (Unit 6) generate images by learning to remove Gaussian noise.
- **Gaussian naive Bayes** assumes each feature is normally distributed within each class.
- **Anomaly detection**: flagging points with large z-scores assumes roughly normal data.
- **Standardization** (lesson 1.7) produces z-scores, the language of the normal distribution.

Not everything is normal, though. Incomes, home prices, and waiting times are skewed right, and treating them as normal leads to wrong conclusions.

## The 68-95-99.7 rule

For every normal distribution, the share of data within a given number of standard deviations of the mean is the same. The **empirical rule** gives the key values:

| Within | Share of data | Exact value |
|---|---|---|
| 1 standard deviation | about 68% | 68.27% |
| 2 standard deviations | about 95% | 95.45% |
| 3 standard deviations | about 99.7% | 99.73% |

```figure
type: normal
caption: About 68% of a normal distribution lies within one standard deviation of the mean, leaving about 16% in each tail.
axis: sd
shade:
  - { from: -1, to: 1 }
```

Because the curve is symmetric, the leftover area splits evenly between the two tails, which gives the numbers you will use most:

- about 34% lies between the mean and 1 standard deviation above it, and 34% between the mean and 1 below;
- about **16%** lies more than 1 standard deviation above the mean (and 16% more than 1 below);
- about **2.5%** lies more than 2 standard deviations above (and 2.5% below);
- about **0.15%** lies more than 3 standard deviations above (and 0.15% below).

::::example[Using the empirical rule]
Scores on a test are normal with mean 500 and standard deviation 100. About what percent score above 700, and what percent score between 400 and 700?

:::step
700 is (700 - 500) / 100 = 2 standard deviations above the mean. About 95% lies within 2, leaving 5% in the two tails, so 2.5% lies above 700.
:::

:::step
400 is 1 standard deviation below the mean, so 34% lies between 400 and 500.
:::

:::step
Between 500 and 700 lies half of 95%, which is 47.5%.
:::

:::answer
About 2.5% score above 700, and about 34% + 47.5% = 81.5% score between 400 and 700.
:::
::::

:::tip
**Test tip.** Sketch a bell, mark the mean and three standard deviations each way, and write 34, 13.5, 2.35, and 0.15 in the bands on each side (they come from halving 68, 95 minus 68, and 99.7 minus 95). Any empirical-rule question then becomes adding bands.
:::

## z-scores: how many standard deviations from the mean

A **z-score** measures how far a value is from the mean, in standard deviations:

$$
z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}
$$

- The **sign** says which side of the mean: positive is above, negative is below, and the mean itself has z = 0.
- The **size** says how far: z = 2 is twice as far as z = 1.

If quiz scores have mean 70 and standard deviation 8, a score of 82 has z = (82 - 70) / 8 = 1.5, one and a half standard deviations above the mean. Going the other way, the score with z = -2 is $$x = \mu + z\sigma = 70 + (-2)(8) = 54$$.

Converting every value to z-scores produces the **standard normal distribution**, $$N(0, 1)$$, with mean 0 and standard deviation 1. Every normal distribution becomes this one after standardizing, which is why one table of areas works for all of them.

:::trap
**Common trap.** Divide by the standard deviation, not the variance. If the variance is 64, the standard deviation is 8.
:::

::check{id="z-scores-01"}

## Comparing across different scales with z [Competitive]

z-scores let you compare values measured on different scales. Raw scores cannot be compared directly when the tests differ in difficulty and spread; their z-scores can.

::::example[Which score is more impressive?]
Ana scored 85 on a chemistry test with mean 75 and standard deviation 5. Ben scored 90 on a history test with mean 80 and standard deviation 8. Who did better relative to their class?

:::step
Ana: z = (85 - 75) / 5 = 2.0.
:::

:::step
Ben: z = (90 - 80) / 8 = 1.25.
:::

:::answer
Ana did better relative to her class. She is 2 standard deviations above her mean while Ben is 1.25 above his, even though Ben's raw score is higher.
:::
::::

::check{id="z-scores-02"}

## Percentiles from common z values [Champion]

Every z-score corresponds to a percentile: the share of the distribution below it. The empirical rule gives the common ones directly:

| z | -3 | -2 | -1 | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Percentile (about) | 0.15 | 2.5 | 16 | 50 | 84 | 97.5 | 99.85 |

For example, z = 1 sits at about the 84th percentile, because 50% lies below the mean and another 34% lies between the mean and z = 1. (Exact tables give 84.1 for z = 1 and 97.7 for z = 2; the empirical rule rounds these.)

Three other z-values come up constantly, because they cut off convenient tail areas:

| z | Area above it | Percentile | Used for |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.645 | 5% | 95th | 90% confidence intervals |
| 1.96 | 2.5% | 97.5th | 95% confidence intervals |
| 2.576 | 0.5% | 99.5th | 99% confidence intervals |

So exactly 95% of a normal distribution lies within 1.96 standard deviations of the mean; the "2" in the empirical rule is a convenient rounding. You will use these values in lesson 2.6.

:::takeaways
- The normal (Gaussian) distribution is bell-shaped, symmetric, and continuous. Its mean, median, and mode are equal, its tails never touch the axis, and its total area is 1.
- Two parameters: the mean sets the center and the standard deviation sets the spread. Larger SD means wider and flatter.
- It matters because many measurements and all large-sample means are about normal, and many methods assume it. In AI it appears in weight initialization, noise, Gaussian naive Bayes, and anomaly detection.
- 68-95-99.7 (exactly 68.27, 95.45, 99.73) within 1, 2, 3 SD. Tails: 16% beyond 1 SD, 2.5% beyond 2, 0.15% beyond 3 on each side.
- z = (x - mean) / SD, and x = mean + z x SD. The standard normal is N(0, 1).
- Compare values on different scales by their z-scores.
- Percentiles: z = -1 is about the 16th, 0 the 50th, 1 the 84th, 2 the 97.5th. z = 1.645, 1.96, 2.576 leave 5%, 2.5%, 0.5% above.
:::

title: Variance and standard deviation

Two basketball players both average 15 points a game. One scores between 12 and 18 every night. The other scores 5 one night and 25 the next. The mean cannot tell them apart, so you need a second number: a measure of **spread**, which says how far values typically sit from the center.

Variance and standard deviation are the measures of spread that data science and machine learning use most. On the test you will compute them for small datasets, choose between the population and sample versions, predict how they change when data are shifted or rescaled, and interpret what they mean. This lesson covers all four.

## Deviations from the mean

Start with the two players. Here are five games for each.

| Game | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Player A | 12 | 14 | 15 | 16 | 18 |
| Player B | 5 | 10 | 15 | 20 | 25 |

Both means are 15. To measure spread, look at how far each value is from the mean. That distance, with its sign, is the value's **deviation**: $$x - \bar{x}$$, where $$\bar{x}$$ (read "x-bar") is the mean.

- Player A's deviations: negative 3, negative 1, 0, 1, 3.
- Player B's deviations: negative 10, negative 5, 0, 5, 10.

Player B's deviations are bigger, which matches what you see. The natural next move is to average the deviations, but it fails: the deviations of any dataset always add up to exactly zero. The values above the mean balance the values below it, because that is what the mean is, the balance point.

:::key
**Key idea.** Deviations from the mean always sum to zero, so their plain average is always zero and tells you nothing. Measures of spread must remove the signs first.
:::

There are two ways to remove the signs. You could take absolute values (that gives the mean absolute deviation, which you will rarely see on this test), or you could square each deviation. Squaring is the standard choice, and it leads to variance.

::check{id="variance-sd-01"}

## Variance: the average squared deviation

The **variance** is the average of the squared deviations from the mean. Square each deviation, add the squares, and divide.

For Player A, the squared deviations are 9, 1, 0, 1, 9. They add to 20, and 20 divided by 5 games is 4. For Player B, the squares are 100, 25, 0, 25, 100, which add to 250, and 250 divided by 5 is 50. Player B's variance is more than ten times Player A's.

When the data are an entire **population** (every member of the group you care about), the variance is written $$\sigma^2$$ ("sigma squared") and the mean is written $$\mu$$ ("mu"):

$$
\sigma^2 = \frac{\sum (x - \mu)^2}{N}
$$

In words: subtract the mean from each value, square the results, add them up, and divide by $$N$$, the number of values. The sum on top has its own name, the **sum of squares**, and you will see it again in regression.

::::example[Population variance]
These five values are an entire population: 4, 8, 6, 5, 7. Find the population variance.

:::step
Find the mean. The values add to 30, and there are 5 of them, so $$\mu = 30 / 5 = 6$$.
:::

:::step
Find each deviation from 6: negative 2, 2, 0, negative 1, 1.
:::

:::step
Square each deviation: 4, 4, 0, 1, 1. The sum of squares is 10.
:::

:::step
Divide by $$N = 5$$: the variance is $$10 / 5 = 2$$.
:::

:::answer
The population variance is 2.
:::
::::

Notice the units. If the values are points, the squared deviations are in points squared, so the variance is in **squared units**. A variance of 50 "square points" is hard to picture. That is the reason for the next measure.

::check{id="variance-sd-02"}

## Standard deviation and why we take the square root

The **standard deviation** is the square root of the variance. Taking the square root undoes the squaring of the units, so the standard deviation is in the same units as the data.

$$
\sigma = \sqrt{\sigma^2}
$$

Player A's standard deviation is $$\sqrt{4} = 2$$ points. Player B's is $$\sqrt{50}$$, about 7.07 points. Now the numbers read naturally: Player A is typically about 2 points from the mean, and Player B about 7.

Three facts follow directly from the definition, and each one shows up as a test question:

1. A standard deviation is never negative, because it is a square root.
2. It equals zero only when every value is identical, since then every deviation is zero.
3. It is sensitive to outliers. A value far from the mean has a large deviation, and squaring makes it count heavily.

You can often compare spreads without computing anything. The set 48, 49, 50, 51, 52 clearly has a smaller standard deviation than 30, 50, 50, 50, 70, because its values hug the mean. When the comparison is close, think in squared deviations: two values 20 away contribute $$2 \times 20^2 = 800$$, while four values 15 away contribute $$4 \times 15^2 = 900$$. More moderate deviations can outweigh a wider range.

:::trap
**Common trap.** The dataset with the largest range does not always have the largest standard deviation. Range uses only the two extremes; standard deviation uses every value.
:::

::check{id="variance-sd-04"}

## Population versus sample [Competitive]

Most of the time you do not have the whole population. You have a **sample**, a subset of the population, and you use it to estimate the population's spread. For a sample, the formula changes in one place: you divide by $$n - 1$$ instead of $$n$$.

$$
s^2 = \frac{\sum (x - \bar{x})^2}{n - 1} \qquad s = \sqrt{s^2}
$$

Here $$s^2$$ is the **sample variance**, $$s$$ is the **sample standard deviation**, $$\bar{x}$$ is the sample mean, and $$n$$ is the sample size.

| | Population | Sample |
|---|---|---|
| Mean | $$\mu$$ | $$\bar{x}$$ |
| Size | $$N$$ | $$n$$ |
| Variance | $$\sigma^2$$, divide by $$N$$ | $$s^2$$, divide by $$n - 1$$ |
| Standard deviation | $$\sigma$$ | $$s$$ |

Why $$n - 1$$? The sample mean is calculated from the same values you are measuring, so the values sit a little closer to $$\bar{x}$$ than they do to the true population mean $$\mu$$. Dividing by $$n$$ would therefore underestimate the population's variance on average. Dividing by the slightly smaller number $$n - 1$$ corrects for this, and the fix is called **Bessel's correction**. Another way to see it: once you know the mean and $$n - 1$$ of the deviations, the last deviation is forced (they must sum to zero), so only $$n - 1$$ deviations are free to vary. That count is the **degrees of freedom**.

::::example[Sample standard deviation]
A random sample of five values is 2, 4, 4, 5, 10. Find the sample variance and the sample standard deviation. Then find the population versions for comparison.

:::step
Find the mean: $$\bar{x} = 25 / 5 = 5$$.
:::

:::step
Deviations: negative 3, negative 1, negative 1, 0, 5. Squares: 9, 1, 1, 0, 25. The sum of squares is 36.
:::

:::step
Sample variance: divide by $$n - 1 = 4$$, so $$s^2 = 36 / 4 = 9$$. Sample standard deviation: $$s = \sqrt{9} = 3$$.
:::

:::step
Population versions, if these five values were the whole population: $$36 / 5 = 7.2$$, and $$\sqrt{7.2} \approx 2.68$$.
:::

:::answer
The sample variance is 9 and the sample standard deviation is 3. Treated as a population, the variance would be 7.2 and the standard deviation about 2.68.
:::
::::

Notice that the sample version is always a little larger than the population version for the same numbers, and the gap shrinks as $$n$$ grows. With 1,000 values, dividing by 999 instead of 1,000 barely matters. With 5 values, it is the difference between 3 and 2.68, and test writers put both in the answer choices.

:::tip
**Test tip.** Read the stem for the signal. Words like "a sample of", "a random sample", or "estimate" mean sample: divide by $$n - 1$$. Words like "all", "every", or "the entire" group mean population: divide by $$N$$. Expect both answers among the options.
:::

For speed with the online calculator, there is a shortcut for the sum of squares that avoids computing every deviation:

$$
\sum (x - \bar{x})^2 = \sum x^2 - \frac{\left(\sum x\right)^2}{n}
$$

For 2, 4, 4, 5, 10: the squares are 4, 16, 16, 25, 100, which sum to 161. Then $$161 - 25^2 / 5 = 161 - 125 = 36$$, the same sum of squares as before. This shortcut also lets you answer questions that give you only $$n$$, $$\sum x$$, and $$\sum x^2$$.

::check{id="variance-sd-03"}

## How spread responds to shifting and scaling [Competitive]

Test writers love to ask what happens to spread when every value is changed the same way. Two rules cover every case.

**Adding a constant shifts the data but does not change the spread.** If every employee gets a $2,000 raise, every salary moves up by the same amount. The mean rises by $2,000, but every deviation from the mean stays exactly the same, so the variance and standard deviation do not change.

**Multiplying by a constant scales the spread.** If every value is multiplied by $$k$$, every deviation is multiplied by $$k$$. The standard deviation is multiplied by $$|k|$$ and the variance by $$k^2$$, because variance squares the deviations.

| Change to every value | Mean | Standard deviation | Variance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Add $$c$$ | adds $$c$$ | no change | no change |
| Multiply by $$k$$ | multiplied by $$k$$ | multiplied by $$\lvert k \rvert$$ | multiplied by $$k^2$$ |

::::example[Converting units]
Daily high temperatures have a mean of 20 degrees Celsius and a standard deviation of 5 degrees Celsius. Convert both to Fahrenheit using $$F = 1.8C + 32$$, and find the new variance.

:::step
The mean follows the whole formula: $$1.8 \times 20 + 32 = 68$$ degrees Fahrenheit.
:::

:::step
The standard deviation feels only the multiplication: $$1.8 \times 5 = 9$$ degrees Fahrenheit. Adding 32 shifts every value equally and changes no deviation.
:::

:::step
The variance was $$5^2 = 25$$. It is multiplied by $$1.8^2 = 3.24$$, giving 81, which is $$9^2$$ as it should be.
:::

:::answer
The mean is 68 degrees F, the standard deviation is 9 degrees F, and the variance is 81 square degrees F.
:::
::::

Two more situations appear often. **Removing an outlier** lowers the standard deviation, because it removes the largest squared deviation. **Adding a value exactly at the mean** also lowers it slightly: the new value adds a deviation of zero to the sum of squares, but the count you divide by grows by one.

:::trap
**Common trap.** "Everyone gets 5 extra points" changes the mean but not the standard deviation. Distractors often add the 5 to the standard deviation too.
:::

## Coefficient of variation [Competitive]

Is a standard deviation of 9.75 kilograms big or small? It depends on the scale. For adult weights averaging 65 kilograms it is substantial; for trucks averaging 15,000 kilograms it is tiny. The **coefficient of variation** (CV) puts spread on a common scale by expressing the standard deviation as a percentage of the mean:

$$
CV = \frac{s}{\bar{x}} \times 100\%
$$

Because the units cancel, the CV lets you compare variability between datasets measured in different units or on very different scales. In a study where heights have mean 170 centimeters and standard deviation 8.5 centimeters, the CV is 5%. If weights in the same study have mean 65 kilograms and standard deviation 9.75 kilograms, the CV is 15%. Weights vary three times as much relative to their mean, even though you could never compare 8.5 centimeters with 9.75 kilograms directly.

The CV only makes sense for data measured from a true zero with a positive mean, such as weights, times, prices, and counts. It is meaningless for temperatures in Celsius or Fahrenheit, where zero is arbitrary and the mean can be near zero or negative.

::check{id="coef-var-01"}

## Software defaults: NumPy versus pandas [Champion]

The population-versus-sample choice is built into every data tool, and the defaults differ. This is a favorite source of hard questions because the same data give different answers in different libraries.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

data = [2, 4, 4, 5, 10]
print(np.std(data))             # NumPy: population by default
print(pd.Series(data).std())    # pandas: sample by default
print(np.std(data, ddof=1))     # NumPy, asked for the sample version
```

```output
2.6832815729997477
3.0
3.0
```

The argument `ddof` means "delta degrees of freedom": the divisor is $$n$$ minus `ddof`. NumPy's `np.std` and `np.var` default to `ddof=0`, the population formula. pandas' `.std()` and `.var()` default to `ddof=1`, the sample formula. Python's built-in `statistics` module has separate functions, `pstdev` (population) and `stdev` (sample). In R, `sd()` and `var()` use $$n - 1$$. In Excel, `STDEV.P` is the population version and `STDEV.S` the sample version.

:::tip
**Test tip.** If a question shows NumPy and pandas code on the same data and asks whether the results match, they do not: NumPy divides by $$n$$ and pandas by $$n - 1$$ unless `ddof` is set.
:::

:::takeaways
- A deviation is $$x - \bar{x}$$. Deviations always sum to zero, so spread measures square them first.
- Variance is the average squared deviation, in squared units. Standard deviation is its square root, in the original units.
- Population: divide by $$N$$ (symbols $$\sigma^2$$, $$\sigma$$, $$\mu$$). Sample: divide by $$n - 1$$ (symbols $$s^2$$, $$s$$, $$\bar{x}$$). This is Bessel's correction.
- Standard deviation is never negative, is zero only when all values are equal, and is sensitive to outliers.
- Adding a constant to every value leaves spread unchanged. Multiplying by $$k$$ multiplies the standard deviation by $$|k|$$ and the variance by $$k^2$$.
- Coefficient of variation is $$s / \bar{x}$$ as a percentage. Use it to compare variability across different units, only for data with a true zero.
- Shortcut: sum of squares $$= \sum x^2 - (\sum x)^2 / n$$.
- NumPy defaults to the population formula (`ddof=0`); pandas, R's `sd()`, and Excel's `STDEV.S` use the sample formula.
:::

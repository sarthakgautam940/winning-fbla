title: Working with many variables

Real outcomes rarely depend on one thing. A home's price depends on its size, age, and location; whether a customer cancels depends on price, usage, and support calls. The objectives name the tools for this directly: dependence and interdependence methods, multiple linear regression, and logistic regression. Test questions ask you to classify a method, read a regression equation, interpret a coefficient, and say what PCA or clustering is for. This lesson covers each of them at the depth the test reaches.

## Univariate, bivariate, multivariate

Analyses are named by how many variables they study at once:

- **Univariate** analysis looks at one variable: its mean, its spread, its histogram.
- **Bivariate** analysis looks at the relationship between two variables: a scatterplot, a correlation, a two-way table.
- **Multivariate analysis** studies three or more variables together, to understand how they relate jointly. Predicting a home's price from its size, age, and location at the same time is multivariate.

Multivariate methods matter because looking at variables one or two at a time can mislead. Lesson 3.3 showed how a confounding variable or Simpson's paradox can distort a two-variable comparison. A multivariate method can measure the effect of one variable while **holding the others constant**, which is often the only way to untangle them.

## Dependence versus interdependence methods

Multivariate methods fall into two families, sorted by one question: **is one variable singled out as the outcome?**

| Family | The question it answers | Methods |
|---|---|---|
| **Dependence methods** | How do these variables predict or explain an outcome? | Multiple linear regression, logistic regression, discriminant analysis, MANOVA |
| **Interdependence methods** | How are these variables (or cases) related to one another? | Principal component analysis, factor analysis, cluster analysis, multidimensional scaling |

In a **dependence method**, one variable (or a set of them) is the **dependent variable**, and the others are used to predict or explain it:

- **Multiple linear regression** predicts a numeric outcome, such as price.
- **Logistic regression** predicts a categorical outcome, usually one of two classes, such as cancel or stay.
- **Discriminant analysis** uses a combination of predictors to assign cases to known groups, such as which of three product tiers a customer will choose.
- **MANOVA** (multivariate analysis of variance) compares group means on several outcome variables at once, such as testing whether three teaching methods differ in both math and reading scores.

In an **interdependence method**, no variable is the outcome. All variables are treated alike, and the goal is to find structure:

- **Principal component analysis** and **factor analysis** find a smaller set of underlying dimensions among the variables.
- **Cluster analysis** finds groups of similar cases.
- **Multidimensional scaling** draws a map in which similar items sit close together.

:::tip
**Test tip.** Look for an outcome. "Predict," "explain," or "estimate the effect on" an outcome means a dependence method. "Group," "segment," "reduce," or "find the structure" with no outcome means an interdependence method. The same split reappears in machine learning as supervised versus unsupervised learning (lesson 5.2).
:::

## Multiple linear regression and its coefficients

**Multiple linear regression** extends the regression line of lesson 3.3 to several predictors:

$$
\hat{y} = b_0 + b_1 x_1 + b_2 x_2 + \cdots + b_k x_k
$$

Here $$b_0$$ is the intercept and each $$b_i$$ is a **coefficient**, the weight on predictor $$x_i$$. Suppose a model for home prices, in thousands of dollars, is:

$$
\widehat{\text{price}} = 40 + 0.15 \times \text{size} - 2 \times \text{age}
$$

where size is in square feet and age is in years.

::::example[Predict and interpret]
Predict the price of an 1,800 square foot home that is 10 years old, and interpret the coefficient on age.

:::step
Substitute the values: 40 + 0.15 × 1,800 - 2 × 10 = 40 + 270 - 20 = 290.
:::

:::step
The price is in thousands of dollars, so the prediction is $290,000.
:::

:::step
The coefficient on age is -2: each additional year of age is associated with a predicted price $2,000 lower, holding size constant.
:::

:::answer
Predicted price: $290,000. Each extra year of age lowers the predicted price by $2,000 for homes of the same size.
:::
::::

The phrase **holding the other variables constant** is the heart of interpretation. The size coefficient of 0.15 means each additional square foot adds $150 to the predicted price *among homes of the same age*. Because of this, a predictor's coefficient in a multiple regression can differ from its slope in a simple one-predictor regression, and can even change sign, when the predictors are correlated. The intercept is the predicted value when every predictor is 0, which is often meaningless (no home has 0 square feet).

**Multicollinearity** occurs when predictors are strongly correlated with each other, such as size in square feet and number of rooms. The model cannot tell their effects apart, so their coefficients become unstable: they can swing wildly or flip sign when the data change slightly, and their standard errors grow. Predictions may still be accurate, but the coefficients cannot be trusted for interpretation. Analysts detect it with a correlation matrix or the **variance inflation factor** (VIF), and fix it by dropping or combining predictors or by regularization (lesson 5.6).

Linear regression rests on assumptions, often remembered as **LINE**: a **L**inear relationship, **I**ndependent errors, **N**ormally distributed errors, and **E**qual variance of the errors across all predicted values. Residual plots (lesson 3.3) are the standard check.

::check{id="multiple-regression-01"}

## R squared and adjusted R squared [Competitive]

**R squared** ($$R^2$$), the **coefficient of determination**, is the proportion of the variation in y that the model explains. It runs from 0 to 1. An $$R^2$$ of 0.81 means the model explains 81% of the variability in prices; the other 19% is left in the residuals. For a simple linear regression, $$R^2$$ is just the square of the correlation r.

$$R^2$$ has a weakness: adding any predictor never lowers it, even a useless one such as the house number. A model stuffed with predictors looks better and better while it overfits. **Adjusted R squared** corrects for this by penalizing each added predictor:

$$
R^2_{\text{adj}} = 1 - (1 - R^2)\,\frac{n - 1}{n - k - 1}
$$

where n is the number of observations and k is the number of predictors. With $$R^2 = 0.60$$, n = 25, and k = 3, adjusted $$R^2 = 1 - 0.40 \times \frac{24}{21} \approx 0.54$$. Adjusted $$R^2$$ rises only when a new predictor improves the fit more than chance would, and it falls when a predictor adds nothing, which makes it the better guide for comparing models with different numbers of predictors.

:::trap
**Common trap.** A high $$R^2$$ does not prove that the model is correct or that the predictors cause the outcome. A curved relationship forced into a straight line can still score a decent $$R^2$$, and a confounded relationship can score a high one.
:::

::check{id="multiple-regression-02"}

## Logistic regression: probabilities through the sigmoid

When the outcome is a category, such as spam or not spam, default or repay, straight-line regression fails: it can predict a "probability" of 1.4 or -0.2. **Logistic regression** fixes this. It computes a linear score from the predictors, then passes it through the **sigmoid** (or logistic) function, which squeezes any number into a probability between 0 and 1:

$$
z = b_0 + b_1 x_1 + \cdots + b_k x_k, \qquad p = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}
$$

```figure

fn: sigmoid
xRange: [-6, 6]
yRange: [0, 1]
xLabel: Linear score z
yLabel: Predicted probability p
hline: 0.5
marks:
  - { x: 0, label: "z = 0 gives p = 0.5" }
caption: The sigmoid curve. Large negative scores give probabilities near 0, large positive scores give probabilities near 1, and a score of 0 gives exactly 0.5, the usual threshold.
```

Despite its name, logistic regression is a **classification** method. To turn a probability into a class, compare it with a **threshold**, usually 0.5: predict the positive class when p is at least 0.5. Raising or lowering the threshold trades one kind of error for another (lesson 3.8).

A coefficient's sign tells you its direction. A positive coefficient means larger values of that predictor raise the predicted probability; a negative coefficient means they lower it.

::::example[Reading a churn model]
A model predicts whether a customer cancels a subscription, with z = -2 + 0.5 × (support calls in the past month). Find the predicted probability for a customer with 4 calls and for one with 8 calls.

:::step
For 4 calls, z = -2 + 0.5 × 4 = 0, and the sigmoid of 0 is exactly 0.5.
:::

:::step
For 8 calls, z = -2 + 0.5 × 8 = 2. Since $$e^{-2} \approx 0.135$$, p = 1 / (1 + 0.135), about 0.88.
:::

:::answer
The customer with 4 calls sits exactly at the 0.5 threshold; the customer with 8 calls has about an 88% predicted chance of canceling. The positive coefficient means more support calls go with a higher chance of canceling.
:::
::::

::check{id="logistic-regression-01"}

## Odds and log-odds [Champion]

Logistic regression is linear in the **log-odds**. Recall from lesson 2.7 that the **odds** of an event are p / (1 - p). The **log-odds**, or **logit**, is the natural logarithm of the odds, and logistic regression models it as a straight line:

$$
\ln\!\left(\frac{p}{1 - p}\right) = b_0 + b_1 x_1 + \cdots + b_k x_k
$$

So the score z is the log-odds. A few values to know: p = 0.5 gives odds of 1 and log-odds of 0; p = 0.75 gives odds of 3 and log-odds of ln 3, about 1.10; and p = 0.25 gives odds of 1/3 and log-odds of about -1.10.

This form explains how to read a coefficient. Increasing $$x_1$$ by one unit adds $$b_1$$ to the log-odds, which **multiplies the odds by $$e^{b_1}$$**, a number called the **odds ratio**. In the churn model, $$b_1 = 0.5$$, so each extra support call multiplies the odds of canceling by $$e^{0.5} \approx 1.65$$. A coefficient of about 0.693 doubles the odds, because $$e^{0.693} \approx 2$$, and a coefficient of 0 leaves them unchanged.

::check{id="logistic-regression-02"}

## PCA, factor analysis, and cluster analysis [Competitive]

The interdependence methods find structure without an outcome variable.

**Principal component analysis (PCA)** replaces many correlated variables with a few new ones called **principal components**. Each component is a weighted combination of the original variables. The first component points in the direction of the greatest variance in the data, the second captures the most of the remaining variance, and so on, with every component uncorrelated with the others. PCA is used to reduce dimensions, to visualize high-dimensional data in two dimensions, and to remove redundancy before modeling. Because it chases variance, variables measured in different units should be standardized first, or the variable with the biggest numbers will dominate.

Each component explains a share of the total variance:

```figure
type: bar
caption: Variance explained by each principal component of a 5-variable dataset. The first two components together explain 83% of the variance.
xLabel: Principal component
yLabel: Variance explained (%)
categories: [PC1, PC2, PC3, PC4, PC5]
series:
  - values: [62, 21, 9, 5, 3]
```

A chart like this is called a **scree plot**. Analysts keep enough components to explain most of the variance (often 80% to 90%), or stop at the "elbow," where the bars level off. Here two components keep 62% + 21% = 83% of the information in five variables.

**Factor analysis** assumes that the observed variables are driven by a smaller number of unobserved **latent factors**. If survey answers about enjoying parties, talking to strangers, and seeking attention all rise and fall together, factor analysis may reveal one latent factor behind them, such as extraversion. PCA summarizes variance; factor analysis models hidden causes.

**Cluster analysis** groups the cases (the rows) so that similar cases land in the same cluster, with no labels given in advance. k-means (lesson 3.7) is the best-known method. Clustering is different from **classification**: classification assigns cases to known, predefined classes using labeled examples, while clustering discovers the groups itself.

::check{id="interdependence-01"}

:::takeaways
- Univariate studies one variable, bivariate two, and multivariate three or more at once, which lets you hold other variables constant.
- Dependence methods predict or explain an outcome: multiple regression, logistic regression, discriminant analysis, MANOVA. Interdependence methods find structure with no outcome: PCA, factor analysis, cluster analysis, multidimensional scaling.
- In multiple regression, each coefficient is the change in predicted y for a one-unit change in that predictor, holding the others constant. Multicollinearity makes coefficients unstable.
- R squared is the share of variation in y explained. It never falls when predictors are added; adjusted R squared penalizes extra predictors.
- Logistic regression is a classification method: the sigmoid turns a linear score into a probability between 0 and 1, and a threshold (usually 0.5) assigns the class.
- The score is the log-odds. A score of 0 means p = 0.5, and each one-unit increase in a predictor multiplies the odds by e to the power of its coefficient.
- PCA builds uncorrelated components ordered by variance explained; standardize first. Factor analysis finds latent factors. Clustering discovers groups, while classification predicts known labels.
:::

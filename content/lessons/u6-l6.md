title: Capabilities and limitations

Two objectives ask you to discuss the capabilities and limitations of generative AI and the capabilities of large language models. Questions describe a task and ask whether an LLM handles it well, or describe a failure and ask what caused it. The key is to understand why: almost every strength and every weakness follows from how LLMs are built (lesson 6.4).

## What LLMs do well

Because they have absorbed patterns from enormous amounts of text, LLMs are strong at language tasks:

- **Writing and rewriting**: drafting emails, essays, and reports; changing tone, length, or reading level.
- **Summarizing**: condensing long documents into key points.
- **Translating** between many languages.
- **Explaining**: teaching a concept at different levels, with examples and analogies.
- **Coding**: writing, explaining, and debugging code in many programming languages.
- **Extracting and organizing**: pulling names, dates, and amounts out of messy text into tables.
- **Brainstorming**: generating ideas, titles, and options.
- **Conversation**: answering follow-up questions and following multi-step instructions in context.

Multimodal models extend these skills to images and audio (lesson 6.3), and in-context learning lets a model pick up a new format from a few examples (lesson 6.5).

There is one catch behind every item on that list: fluency is not accuracy. The same process that produces a polished paragraph can produce a polished error, so the more an answer matters, the more it needs checking.

## Hallucination, briefly

A **hallucination** is output from an AI model that sounds confident and plausible but is false, made up, or unsupported by its sources: an invented statistic, a quotation no one said, a citation to a book that does not exist. NIST's 2024 Generative AI Profile calls this **confabulation**, "confidently stated but erroneous or false content."

Hallucinations happen because an LLM predicts likely-sounding text rather than looking up verified facts. It has no built-in fact-checker, and where its training data is thin, it fills the gap with something that fits the pattern. Fabricated sources are a classic case: the pattern of a citation (author, title, journal, year) is easy to fill in with convincing inventions.

Organizations are responsible for what their AI tells people. In Moffatt v. Air Canada (2024), the airline's website chatbot wrongly told a customer, traveling after a grandparent's death, that a bereavement fare could be claimed retroactively within 90 days. British Columbia's Civil Resolution Tribunal held Air Canada liable, rejected its argument that the chatbot was "a separate legal entity," and ordered it to pay about 812 Canadian dollars. Lesson 8.6 covers why hallucinations happen and how to reduce them.

## Knowledge cutoffs and context limits

A model's knowledge comes from training data collected up to some date, its **knowledge cutoff**. It knows nothing about later events, such as a law passed last month or a game played this morning, unless the information is supplied, for example by retrieval-augmented generation or a search tool (lesson 6.5). Asked about recent events without help, it may answer confidently with outdated information.

The context window (lesson 6.4) sets a second limit: a document or conversation too long to fit cannot be considered all at once, and text that falls out of the window is lost to the model.

Two related facts surprise many users:

- **No memory across sessions.** By default, each new conversation starts with an empty context window, so the model does not remember earlier chats. Apps that offer memory save notes about the user and insert them into new prompts; the model itself is unchanged.
- **No learning from conversations.** Correcting a model during a chat changes only that chat's context, not its weights. Other users tomorrow get the same model, though developers may use feedback to improve future versions.

## Non-determinism and prompt sensitivity

LLM output is **non-deterministic**: with the temperature above 0, the model samples each token (lesson 6.5), so the same prompt can produce different answers, occasionally including a wrong one. That makes results hard to reproduce, test, and audit. One correct run proves little; reliable testing uses many prompts and repetitions, and tasks that need consistency use a low temperature.

LLMs are also **prompt sensitive**: small changes in wording, the order of examples, or formatting can change the answer, sometimes completely. A leading question, such as "Why is this false claim true?", can push a model to defend a false premise. Teams that deploy LLMs test many phrasings and ground answers in trusted sources.

::check{id="genai-limits-03"}

## Bias, cost, and energy [Competitive]

Several limitations are not about single answers but about the technology as a whole.

- **Bias.** LLMs learn from human-written text, so they can reproduce stereotypes and skewed viewpoints from that text, and they often perform worse in languages and for groups that are underrepresented in the data (lesson 8.2).
- **Cost.** Training a large model takes enormous computing resources, and running it costs money on every request. For a narrow task, a smaller model that is accurate enough is often the wiser choice.
- **Energy.** Training and serving large models consume large amounts of electricity in data centers. NIST's Generative AI Profile lists environmental impacts among the 12 risks of generative AI.
- **Copyright uncertainty.** Two questions are unsettled: whether training on copyrighted works without permission is lawful, and who, if anyone, owns AI-generated output. On the second, the U.S. Copyright Office's 2023 guidance says that copyright protects only material that is the product of human creativity, and that when an AI technology determines the expressive elements of its output, that material is not the product of human authorship. Applicants must disclose AI-generated content when registering a work. The Office has since published reports on copyrightability (January 2025) and on training (a pre-publication version in May 2025), and the EU AI Act gives providers of general-purpose AI models copyright duties.

## Tasks that need tools [Competitive]

Some tasks are unreliable for an LLM working alone, because of how it works:

| Task | Why an LLM alone is unreliable | Better approach |
|---|---|---|
| Exact arithmetic on long numbers or large datasets | It predicts tokens rather than computing | A calculator or code tool |
| Counting letters or words exactly | It sees tokens, not individual letters | A short program |
| Recent events, live prices, today's weather | Its knowledge stops at the cutoff | Search or retrieval tools |
| Exact quotations and citations | It can fabricate plausible ones | Retrieval, then checking every source |
| High-stakes decisions in medicine, law, or finance | Errors and bias can cause real harm | Human review and accountability |

The fix in each case is to let the model do what it does well, understanding the request and writing the answer, while tools, retrieval, and people supply what it cannot: exact computation, current facts, and judgment. The same thinking applies to code an LLM writes: treat it as a draft to read and test, not as finished work.

::check{id="llm-capabilities-04"}

:::takeaways
- LLMs do language tasks well: writing, rewriting, summarizing, translating, explaining, coding, extracting information into tables, brainstorming, and conversation. Fluency is not accuracy.
- A hallucination is confident, plausible output that is false or made up; NIST calls it confabulation. Companies answer for their chatbots (Moffatt v. Air Canada, 2024).
- The knowledge cutoff hides later events unless retrieval or search supplies them. There is no memory across sessions by default, and chats do not change the model's weights.
- Non-determinism: the same prompt can give different answers, so one test run proves little. Prompt sensitivity: small rewordings can change answers.
- Bias comes from training data; cost and energy grow with model size (NIST lists environmental impacts as a generative AI risk).
- Copyright: the U.S. Copyright Office (2023) protects only human creativity, not expression an AI determined, and requires disclosure of AI-generated content. Training on copyrighted works remains an open question.
- Use tools for exact math, counting, live facts, and citations, and use human review for high-stakes decisions.
:::

Most of the world's business data lives in relational databases: bank accounts, school records, store inventories. One objective asks you to describe the characteristics of relational databases, and the SQL lessons that follow build on this one. Expect questions on vocabulary, on identifying keys and relationships, on spotting a poorly designed table, on ACID transactions, on indexes, and on when a NoSQL database fits better. This lesson uses the course's sample **school** database, which you can view on the Reference page.

## Tables, rows, and columns

A **relational database** stores data in **tables** made of rows and columns, and links the tables to each other through shared key values. You ask it questions with **SQL** (Structured Query Language), the subject of the next three lessons. The relational model was introduced by E. F. Codd, a researcher at IBM, in a 1970 paper "A Relational Model of Data for Large Shared Data Banks."

Here is the school database's `students` table:

| student_id | name | grade | gpa | club_id |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ava | 10 | 3.6 | 1 |
| 2 | Ben | 11 | 2.9 | NULL |
| 3 | Chloe | 12 | 3.9 | 2 |
| 4 | Dev | 10 | 3.2 | 1 |
| 5 | Elena | 11 | 3.8 | 3 |
| 6 | Farid | 12 | 2.5 | NULL |
| 7 | Grace | 9 | 3.4 | 2 |
| 8 | Hiro | 11 | 3.9 | 1 |
| 9 | Isla | 10 | NULL | 3 |
| 10 | Jonah | 12 | 3.1 | 2 |

The vocabulary comes in pairs, because database people and the original theory use different words:

- A **table** (formally, a **relation**) holds one kind of thing, here students.
- A **row** (also a **record** or **tuple**) is one instance of that thing: one student.
- A **column** (also a **field** or **attribute**) is one property recorded for every row, with a fixed **data type** such as integer or text.
- Each cell holds a single value. **NULL** marks a missing value: Ben has no club, and Isla has no recorded GPA.

The **schema** is the database's structure: its tables, their columns and data types, the keys, and the relationships between tables. The schema describes the data; it is not the data itself.

Software that creates and manages relational databases is a **relational database management system** (RDBMS). Widely used examples are PostgreSQL, MySQL, SQLite, Oracle Database, and Microsoft SQL Server.

:::trap
**Common trap.** In database theory, a "relation" is a table, not a relationship between tables. The relational model is named for its tables, which is why a single table can be called a relation.
:::

## Primary keys and foreign keys

A **primary key** is a column, or a set of columns, whose value uniquely identifies each row. It must be **unique** (no two rows share it) and **never NULL**. In `students`, the primary key is `student_id`: two students could share a name, but never an ID.

A **foreign key** is a column in one table that refers to the primary key of another table, and it is how tables link together. In `students`, the column `club_id` is a foreign key that points to `clubs.club_id`:

| club_id | club_name | advisor |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Robotics | Ms. Park |
| 2 | Debate | Mr. Reyes |
| 3 | Data Science | Dr. Osei |
| 4 | Chess | Ms. Lin |

Ava's `club_id` of 1 means Ava is in Robotics. Instead of copying "Robotics" and "Ms. Park" into every student's row, the database stores them once and points to them.

**Referential integrity** is the rule that every foreign key value must match an existing primary key value (or be NULL, if the column allows it). A student with `club_id` 7 would break referential integrity, because no club 7 exists. Ben's NULL is fine: it simply means Ben is in no club.

A primary key made of two or more columns is a **composite key**. The `enrollments` table records which student takes which course, and its primary key is the pair (`student_id`, `course_id`): a student can appear many times and so can a course, but each pair appears only once.

Many tables use a **surrogate key**, an ID number invented just to identify rows, such as `student_id`, rather than a **natural key** taken from the real world, such as an email address.

::check{id="keys-01"}

## One-to-one, one-to-many, many-to-many

Keys express three kinds of **relationships** between tables:

- **One-to-many**: one row in a table relates to many rows in another. One club has many students, but each student belongs to at most one club. The foreign key goes on the "many" side: `students.club_id`.
- **Many-to-many**: rows on both sides relate to many rows on the other. A student takes many courses, and a course has many students. A relational database cannot store this with one foreign key, so it uses a third table, a **junction table** (also called a bridge or associative table), whose rows pair the two keys. In the school database, `enrollments` is the junction table: each row links one student to one course and records the score.
- **One-to-one**: each row matches at most one row in the other table, such as a student and an assigned locker. One-to-one tables are often merged, or kept separate to hold optional or sensitive details.

:::tip
**Test tip.** "Many-to-many" in a question almost always leads to a junction table. If each side can have many of the other, look for a third table holding two foreign keys.
:::

## Normalization to third normal form [Competitive]

Imagine storing club details inside the student table:

| student_id | name | club_id | club_name | advisor |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ava | 1 | Robotics | Ms. Park |
| 4 | Dev | 1 | Robotics | Ms. Park |
| 8 | Hiro | 1 | Robotics | Ms. Park |
| 3 | Chloe | 2 | Debate | Mr. Reyes |

The repetition causes three kinds of trouble, called **anomalies**:

- An **update anomaly**: if Robotics gets a new advisor, every Robotics row must change; miss one and the data contradicts itself.
- An **insertion anomaly**: a new club with no members yet cannot be recorded without inventing a student.
- A **deletion anomaly**: if the last Chess member leaves, deleting that row erases everything known about Chess.

**Normalization** organizes data into well-structured tables to reduce redundancy and prevent these anomalies, mainly by splitting tables apart and linking them with keys. Its steps are called **normal forms**:

- **First normal form (1NF)**: every cell holds a single, indivisible value. A cell listing "STAT1, CS1" breaks 1NF; the fix is one row per course.
- **Second normal form (2NF)**: 1NF, and every non-key column depends on the *whole* primary key. If `enrollments` also stored `course_title`, the title would depend only on `course_id`, half of the composite key. That is a **partial dependency**, and the fix is to keep titles in `courses`.
- **Third normal form (3NF)**: 2NF, and no non-key column depends on another non-key column. In the table above, `advisor` depends on `club_id`, which depends on the student. That chain is a **transitive dependency**, and the fix is to move club details into their own `clubs` table, exactly as the school database does.

A classic summary of 3NF: every non-key column must depend on "the key, the whole key, and nothing but the key."

Normalization is not always the goal. **Denormalization** deliberately combines tables or repeats data to make reading faster, because joins take time. It is common in data warehouses built for analysis, where data is read far more often than it is changed, and it accepts some risk of anomalies in exchange for speed.

## Constraints, transactions, and ACID [Competitive]

**Constraints** are rules the database enforces on every insert and update:

| Constraint | The rule it enforces |
|---|---|
| PRIMARY KEY | Values are unique and never NULL |
| FOREIGN KEY | Values must match a key in the referenced table |
| NOT NULL | A value is required |
| UNIQUE | No two rows share a value |
| CHECK | Each value must satisfy a condition, such as a range |
| DEFAULT | Supplies a value when none is given |

A version of the students table that enforced a grade range could be declared like this:

```sql
CREATE TABLE students (
  student_id INTEGER PRIMARY KEY,
  name       TEXT NOT NULL,
  grade      INTEGER NOT NULL CHECK (grade BETWEEN 9 AND 12),
  gpa        REAL,
  club_id    INTEGER REFERENCES clubs(club_id)
);
```

A **transaction** is a group of SQL statements treated as a single, all-or-nothing unit of work. Moving $100 between bank accounts takes two statements, a withdrawal and a deposit, and they must succeed or fail together. **COMMIT** makes a transaction's changes permanent; **ROLLBACK** undoes all of them.

Reliable transactions have four properties, remembered as **ACID**:

- **Atomicity**: all of the transaction happens, or none of it does. If the system crashes after the withdrawal but before the deposit, the withdrawal is undone.
- **Consistency**: a transaction moves the database from one valid state to another, so every constraint still holds afterward.
- **Isolation**: transactions running at the same time do not interfere; the result is as if they ran one after another. Two buyers cannot both purchase the last concert ticket.
- **Durability**: once a transaction is committed, it survives power failures and crashes.

## Indexes [Competitive]

Finding every student in grade 11 without help means reading every row, a **full table scan**. An **index** is a separate data structure, usually a balanced tree, that lets the database jump straight to the rows with a given value, the way a book's index points you to a page:

```sql
CREATE INDEX idx_students_grade ON students (grade);
```

Indexes speed up searches, joins, and sorts on the indexed columns. They have costs: they take extra storage, and every INSERT, UPDATE, or DELETE must also update each index, so writes get slower. Primary keys are usually indexed automatically. The usual advice is to index the columns that appear often in WHERE clauses and joins, not every column.

:::key
**Key idea.** An index trades slower writes and extra storage for faster reads.
:::

::check{id="db-integrity-02"}

## SQL versus NoSQL

**NoSQL** databases, read as "not only SQL," store data in models other than linked tables. There are four main types:

| Type | How it stores data | Example | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Document | Flexible, JSON-like documents of fields and values | MongoDB | Product catalogs, user profiles |
| Key-value | A value stored under a unique key, like a dictionary | Redis | Caching, session data |
| Wide-column | Rows whose columns can vary, partitioned across many servers | Apache Cassandra | Huge write-heavy logs and sensor data |
| Graph | Nodes, relationships, and their properties | Neo4j | Social networks, recommendations, fraud rings |

The trade-offs, in general terms:

- **Schema**: relational databases have a fixed schema defined in advance; NoSQL databases offer flexible schemas, so records can differ in their fields.
- **Scaling**: relational databases have traditionally scaled **vertically** (a bigger server), while NoSQL databases are designed to scale **horizontally** (more servers).
- **Relationships and consistency**: relational databases excel at joining related tables under strict ACID guarantees, which is why banks and inventory systems rely on them.

Choose relational for structured data with many relationships and strict consistency needs. Choose NoSQL for rapidly changing or varied data, enormous scale, or data that is naturally a graph or a set of documents.

:::trap
**Common trap.** NoSQL does not mean "no transactions" or "no structure." MongoDB, a NoSQL document database, supports multi-document ACID transactions, and many NoSQL systems hold highly structured data.
:::

:::takeaways
- A relational database stores data in tables of rows (records) and columns (attributes) linked by keys, and is queried with SQL. E. F. Codd introduced the model at IBM in 1970.
- The schema is the structure (tables, columns, types, keys, relationships). RDBMS examples: PostgreSQL, MySQL, SQLite, Oracle, SQL Server.
- A primary key is unique and never NULL; a foreign key refers to another table's primary key; a composite key uses several columns. Referential integrity requires every foreign key to match an existing key.
- One-to-many puts the foreign key on the "many" side. Many-to-many needs a junction table, like enrollments.
- Normalization prevents update, insertion, and deletion anomalies. 1NF: single values per cell. 2NF: no partial dependencies. 3NF: no transitive dependencies. Denormalization trades redundancy for faster reads.
- Constraints: PRIMARY KEY, FOREIGN KEY, NOT NULL, UNIQUE, CHECK, DEFAULT. Transactions are all-or-nothing: COMMIT saves, ROLLBACK undoes. ACID: atomicity, consistency, isolation, durability.
- An index speeds reads at the cost of storage and slower writes.
- NoSQL ("not only SQL") types: document, key-value, wide-column, graph. NoSQL favors flexible schemas and horizontal scaling; relational favors relationships and strict consistency.
:::

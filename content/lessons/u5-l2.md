title: Supervised and unsupervised learning
---

Machine learning methods are sorted first by what kind of data they learn from: labeled examples, unlabeled data, or rewards. One objective asks you to characterize supervised, unsupervised, and reinforcement learning, and another asks you to pick the right one for a problem (lesson 5.4). This lesson covers the first two families, then the other learning paradigms that appear in test options: semi-supervised, self-supervised, transfer, online, and batch learning, plus the champion-level distinction between parametric and non-parametric models. Reinforcement learning gets its own lesson next.

## Supervised learning: labeled examples

In **supervised learning**, a model learns from **labeled** examples: each training example comes with the correct answer, as if a teacher were supervising. The model learns the relationship between the features x and the label y, then predicts y for new examples whose labels are unknown.

Examples are everywhere:

- predicting whether an email is spam, from emails already marked spam or not spam;
- predicting a house's price, from past sales with known prices;
- diagnosing a disease from scans, using scans labeled by doctors.

Because the right answers are known, supervised models are easy to evaluate: compare their predictions with the true labels on test data that the model never saw during training (lesson 5.5).

Common supervised algorithms include linear and logistic regression, decision trees and random forests, k-nearest neighbors, support vector machines, naive Bayes, and neural networks.

::check{id="supervised-01"}

## Classification versus regression

Supervised learning splits into two tasks according to what is predicted:

- **Classification** predicts a **category**: spam or not spam, which animal is in a photo, which digit someone wrote.
- **Regression** predicts a **number** on a continuous scale: a price, a temperature, a wait time.

| Problem | Output | Task |
|---|---|---|
| Will this customer cancel? | Yes or no | Classification |
| Which of 10 digits is this? | One of 10 classes | Classification |
| What will this house sell for? | A dollar amount | Regression |
| How many minutes until the bus arrives? | A number | Regression |

Classification comes in three forms. **Binary** classification has two classes (fraud or legitimate). **Multiclass** classification has three or more classes, with one label per example (cat, dog, or bird). **Multilabel** classification gives each example any number of labels at once, such as tagging a photo with "dog," "ball," and "grass."

:::trap
**Common trap.** Decide by the output, not by the subject. Predicting *whether* a customer will spend more than $100 is classification (yes or no), even though it involves money. Predicting *how much* the customer will spend is regression. And logistic regression, despite its name, is a classification method.
:::

::check{id="supervised-02"}

## Unsupervised learning: structure without labels

In **unsupervised learning**, the data has **no labels**. Nobody tells the model the right answers; it looks for structure on its own. The main tasks:

- **Clustering** groups similar examples, as k-means does when it finds customer segments (lesson 3.7).
- **Dimensionality reduction** compresses many features into fewer, as PCA does (lesson 3.6).
- **Association rules** find items that occur together, as in market basket analysis.
- **Anomaly detection** flags examples that do not fit the usual pattern, such as unusual sensor readings, often without any labeled examples of failures.

Unsupervised learning is harder to evaluate, because there are no correct answers to compare against. Analysts judge it with internal measures, such as the silhouette score or the within-cluster sum of squares (lesson 3.7), and, above all, by whether the patterns are meaningful and useful.

:::key
**Key idea.** Labels decide the family. Labeled examples mean supervised learning; no labels mean unsupervised learning.
:::

## Semi-supervised and self-supervised learning [Competitive]

Labels are often expensive: a doctor must read every scan, a person must tag every photo. Two paradigms reduce the need for them.

**Semi-supervised learning** combines a small labeled dataset with a large unlabeled one. A hospital might have 200 scans labeled by radiologists and 20,000 unlabeled scans. The model learns from the labeled examples and uses the structure of the unlabeled ones to improve, for instance by labeling the unlabeled examples it is most confident about and training on those too, a technique called **pseudo-labeling**.

**Self-supervised learning** creates its own labels from the data itself, by hiding part of each example and learning to predict the hidden part. A language model that reads billions of sentences and learns to predict each next word needs no human labels: the next word in the text *is* the label. This is how large language models are pretrained (lesson 6.4), and similar tricks, such as predicting missing patches of images, train vision models.

::check{id="other-paradigms-01"}

## Transfer learning, online learning, and batch learning [Competitive]

**Transfer learning** reuses a model trained on one task as the starting point for a related task. An image model pretrained on millions of everyday photos has already learned to detect edges, textures, and shapes; fine-tuning it on 800 labeled X-rays can produce a strong medical model far faster, and with far less data, than training from scratch.

Models also differ in how they take in data over time:

- **Batch learning** (also called offline learning) trains on the whole dataset at once. To include new data, the model is retrained, often on a schedule such as every month.
- **Online learning** updates the model a little with each new example, or each small group of examples, as they arrive. It suits fast-changing streams, such as news recommendations or market prices. Its risk is that a burst of bad data can quickly degrade the model, so it needs monitoring.

:::trap
**Common trap.** "Batch learning" (training on all the data at once) is a different idea from the "mini-batches" used in gradient descent (lesson 5.7), which split one training run into small groups of examples.
:::

::check{id="other-paradigms-02"}

## Parametric versus non-parametric models [Champion]

A **parametric** model has a fixed number of parameters no matter how much data it sees, because it assumes a particular form for the relationship. Linear regression assumes a straight line, so it always has one coefficient per feature plus an intercept. Logistic regression and naive Bayes are also parametric. Parametric models train fast and need less data, but if the assumed form is wrong, they underfit.

A **non-parametric** model makes few assumptions about the form, and its complexity grows with the data. k-nearest neighbors keeps the entire training set; a decision tree can keep adding splits. Non-parametric models are flexible enough to fit complicated patterns, but they need more data and can overfit.

A related distinction is when the work happens. A **lazy learner**, such as k-nearest neighbors, does almost nothing during training (it simply stores the examples) and does its work at prediction time, by searching for the nearest neighbors. An **eager learner**, such as a decision tree or a regression, builds its model during training, so predictions are fast.

::check{id="other-paradigms-03"}

:::takeaways
- Supervised learning learns from labeled examples and predicts labels for new data; it is evaluated against known answers.
- Classification predicts categories (binary, multiclass, or multilabel); regression predicts numbers. Decide by the output, not the subject.
- Unsupervised learning finds structure without labels: clustering, dimensionality reduction, association rules, anomaly detection. It is harder to evaluate.
- Semi-supervised learning uses a few labels plus many unlabeled examples. Self-supervised learning makes labels from the data itself, such as predicting the next word.
- Transfer learning reuses a pretrained model for a related task. Batch learning trains on all the data at once; online learning updates continuously as data arrives.
- Parametric models have a fixed number of parameters and assume a form (linear and logistic regression, naive Bayes). Non-parametric models grow with the data (k-NN, decision trees).
- Lazy learners such as k-NN do their work at prediction time; eager learners build the model during training.
:::

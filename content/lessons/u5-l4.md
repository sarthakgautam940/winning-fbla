title: Choosing an approach for a problem
---

One objective asks you to select an appropriate machine learning approach for a problem: supervised, unsupervised, or reinforcement learning. These are scenario questions, and they reward a fixed routine more than memorization. This lesson gives you three questions to ask of every scenario, a decision chart that combines them, the constraints that decide between algorithms, and a set of practice scenarios. With the routine in hand, most of these items take under 20 seconds.

## Question one: are there labels?

Start with the data. Does each example come with the correct answer, the outcome you want to predict?

- **Yes, there are labels:** supervised learning. A hospital's X-rays labeled "pneumonia" or "no pneumonia," transactions marked "fraud" or "legitimate," and past sales with known prices are all labeled.
- **No labels:** unsupervised learning, or reinforcement learning if an agent will learn by acting (question three).

Labels can hide in plain sight. "Past customers, and whether each one canceled" is labeled data, even if the word "label" never appears. On the other hand, "we want to find groups" or "discover patterns" with no known outcome means there are no labels.

:::tip
**Test tip.** Look for a recorded outcome for each past example: "marked as," "known," "diagnosed," "whether each." If you find one, think supervised.
:::

::check{id="choose-approach-01"}

## Question two: what is being predicted?

If the problem is supervised, the output decides the task:

- a **category** (spam or not, which disease, which digit) means **classification**;
- a **number** (price, minutes, temperature, units sold) means **regression**.

If the problem is unsupervised, the goal decides the task:

- finding **groups** means **clustering**;
- compressing many features into fewer means **dimensionality reduction**;
- finding **unusual cases** means **anomaly detection**;
- finding **items that occur together** means **association rules**.

::check{id="choose-approach-02"}

## Question three: is there feedback over time?

Some problems are not about predicting from a fixed dataset at all. If an **agent** must choose **actions**, the actions change what happens next, and success is measured by **rewards** collected over time, the problem calls for **reinforcement learning**. Game-playing, robot control, and systems that tune their own settings through trial and error all fit this description.

## Picking an algorithm, and when interpretability matters [Competitive]

Once the task is clear, the constraints of the situation narrow the choice of algorithm:

| Constraint | Good choices | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Decisions must be explained to people | Logistic regression, linear regression, decision trees | Each prediction can be traced to coefficients or rules |
| Highest accuracy on tables of data | Random forests, gradient boosting | Ensembles of trees capture complex patterns (lesson 5.8) |
| Images, audio, or text | Deep learning: convolutional networks, transformers | They learn features from raw data (lesson 5.10) |
| Few labeled examples | Simple models, or transfer learning from a pretrained model | Complex models overfit small datasets |
| Groups in unlabeled numeric data | k-means (scaled features); DBSCAN for odd shapes and noise | Distance-based grouping (lessons 3.7 and 5.8) |

**Interpretability** matters most where people are affected by the decisions and may need reasons, as in lending, hiring, health care, and criminal justice. A bank that must tell an applicant why a loan was denied can do so with a decision tree ("income below the threshold and debt above 40% of income"). It cannot easily do so with a deep neural network, which is often called a **black box** because its millions of weights do not translate into reasons. Explaining black-box models is an active field (lesson 8.7), but when a simpler model performs nearly as well, it is often the responsible choice.

:::trap
**Common trap.** The most powerful model is not automatically the right answer. If the stem stresses explanations, a small dataset, or limited computing power, the best option is usually a simpler model.
:::

## Scenario practice

::::example[Name the approach]
Classify each scenario by learning type and task: (1) predict how many minutes a food delivery will take, using past deliveries with recorded times; (2) find natural groups among a city's neighborhoods from census data; (3) teach a drone to fly through obstacles, rewarding fast and safe flights; (4) decide whether a credit card transaction is fraud, using past transactions marked as fraud or not.

:::step
Scenario 1 has labels (recorded times), and the output is a number, so it is supervised regression.
:::

:::step
Scenario 2 has no labels and the goal is groups, so it is unsupervised clustering.
:::

:::step
Scenario 3 has an agent acting and earning rewards, so it is reinforcement learning.
:::

:::step
Scenario 4 has labels (fraud or not), and the output is a category, so it is supervised classification.
:::

:::answer
Supervised regression, unsupervised clustering, reinforcement learning, supervised classification.
:::
::::

Two more patterns are worth knowing. A **recommendation** problem ("suggest products similar customers bought") is usually solved with collaborative filtering, which learns from patterns of past behavior rather than from one labeled outcome. And a problem that *sounds* supervised may lack labels: if a company wants to predict which customers will cancel but has never recorded who canceled, it must first collect those outcomes before any supervised model can be trained.

::check{id="choose-approach-05"}

:::takeaways
- Ask three questions: Are there labels? What is being predicted? Is an agent acting and earning rewards over time?
- Labels mean supervised learning: a category is classification and a number is regression.
- No labels and no agent mean unsupervised learning: clustering, dimensionality reduction, anomaly detection, or association rules.
- An agent choosing actions for rewards over time means reinforcement learning.
- Constraints pick the algorithm: interpretable models (linear and logistic regression, decision trees) when decisions need reasons; ensembles for top accuracy on tables; deep learning for images, audio, and text; simple models or transfer learning when data is scarce.
- Deep networks are often black boxes. When people need explanations and a simpler model performs nearly as well, prefer the simpler model.
:::

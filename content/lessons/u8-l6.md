title: Credibility, hallucinations, and misinformation

A language model sounds just as sure of itself when it is wrong as when it is right, and generative AI has made convincing fakes cheap to produce. The last objective of the event asks about exactly these credibility concerns. Building on lesson 6.6, this lesson explains why models make things up, how to reduce and catch it, how misinformation differs from disinformation, and the habits that keep you from being fooled.

## What a hallucination is

A **hallucination** is output from an AI model that sounds confident and plausible but is false, made up, or unsupported by its sources. NIST's term for it is **confabulation**: confidently stated but erroneous or false content. Common forms include invented statistics, quotations no one said, wrong details about real people, citations to sources that do not exist, and, in code, references to software libraries that do not exist.

The most famous fabricated citations reached a federal court. In Mata v. Avianca (2023), two lawyers submitted a filing that relied on judicial opinions ChatGPT had invented, complete with fake quotations and citations. The judge found that the lawyers had acted in bad faith, imposed a $5,000 penalty, and ordered them to send the ruling to their client and to each real judge falsely named as the author of the six fake opinions.

A hallucination is not a lie. The model has no intention to deceive; it produced text that fit a pattern and happened to be false. That difference matters when you classify false information later in this lesson.

## Why hallucinations happen

A language model is trained to predict likely next tokens (lesson 6.4), not to look up verified facts. Text can be likely without being true. Where the training data are thin, or a question falls after the model's knowledge cutoff, the model fills the gap with statistically plausible text rather than admitting it does not know. Citations are especially easy to fabricate because they follow a predictable pattern, author, title, journal, year, that a model can fill with convincing inventions.

Incentives make it worse. In 2025, researchers at OpenAI (Adam Tauman Kalai and colleagues) argued that language models hallucinate because their training and evaluation procedures reward guessing over acknowledging uncertainty. Most benchmarks score an answer as simply right or wrong, so "I don't know" earns nothing, and guessing when uncertain improves test performance. You know this incentive well: this event has no penalty for wrong answers, which is exactly why you never leave a question blank. A model graded the same way learns to guess too. The researchers' proposed fix is to change how benchmarks are scored, so that confident errors stop being rewarded over honest uncertainty.

:::key
**Key idea.** A language model produces text that is likely, and likely is not the same as true. A confident, fluent tone is not evidence that an answer is correct.
:::

::check{id="hallucination-02"}

## Reducing hallucinations

No technique eliminates hallucinations, but several reduce them or catch them before they cause harm. OWASP's guidance on LLM misinformation recommends:

- **Grounding with retrieval.** Retrieval-augmented generation (lesson 6.5) gives the model trusted documents to answer from, instead of relying on memory alone.
- **Cross-verification and human oversight.** People check important outputs against trusted sources, and reviewers are trained not to over-rely on the model.
- **Automatic validation.** Tools check key outputs, for example confirming that a cited case appears in a legal database or that a suggested software package really exists.
- **Risk communication and interface design.** Label AI-generated content and state what the tool should and should not be used for.
- **Training and education.** Teach users the limits of LLMs and the need for independent verification.

As a user, you can also ask for sources and then actually open them, give the model permission to say it does not know, and use tools rather than the model's memory for exact facts, math, and recent events.

:::trap
**Common trap.** Retrieval reduces hallucinations; it does not end them. A model can still misread or misstate the documents it retrieves, and the documents themselves can be wrong or even poisoned (lesson 8.4).
:::

::::example[Checking a citation a chatbot gave you]
A chatbot supports a claim with this citation: Rivera, L. (2021). "Neural Scoring of Student Essays." *Journal of Learning Systems*, 8(2), 44 to 61. (The citation is made up for this example.) How do you decide whether to use it?

:::step
Search for the exact title in a library database or search engine. A real article will turn up in an index or on the publisher's site.
:::

:::step
If it exists, confirm the details: the authors, journal, year, volume, and pages should all match.
:::

:::step
Open the article and find the passage that supports the claim. Real sources are often cited for things they never said.
:::

:::answer
Treat the citation as unverified until you have read the source yourself. A citation you cannot find is most likely fabricated, and you should not use it.
:::
::::

## Misinformation, disinformation, and deepfakes

False information comes in kinds, and what separates them is intent:

- **Misinformation** is false or misleading information spread without an intent to deceive, through honest error.
- **Disinformation** is false or misleading information spread deliberately to deceive.
- **Malinformation** is genuine information shared in order to cause harm, such as leaking someone's private records.

AI feeds both of the first two. A chatbot's hallucinations create misinformation that no one intended, and misinformation appears on OWASP's Top 10 for LLM Applications. Generative AI also lowers the cost of disinformation: NIST's information integrity risk warns that it can be used for large-scale disinformation and misinformation campaigns.

Deepfakes are the sharpest tool of disinformation. In lesson 8.1 you met the Arup employee who sent about US$25 million after a video call with deepfaked colleagues. One response is **content provenance**, a record of where a piece of content came from and how it was edited. The Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity (C2PA), founded in 2021 by Adobe, Arm, the BBC, Intel, Microsoft, and Truepic, develops an open standard for this record; the signed record attached to a file is called **Content Credentials**, which C2PA likens to a nutrition label for digital content. The EU AI Act adds a legal duty: deepfakes must be labeled.

:::trap
**Common trap.** Provenance is not fact-checking. Content Credentials can show that a photo came from a certain camera and was edited in a certain app, but a genuine photo can still carry a false caption. And a missing credential does not prove a file is fake: credentials can be separated from a file when its metadata is stripped or corrupted, which is why the standard also supports invisible watermarks and fingerprints to find them again.
:::

## Automation bias and overreliance [Competitive]

Hallucinations do the most damage when people trust them. **Automation bias** is the tendency to automatically rely, or over-rely, on the output of an automated system; that is the EU AI Act's own wording. OWASP describes the same problem for chatbots as **overreliance**: users place excessive trust in LLM-generated content and act on it without checking. NIST's Generative AI Profile lists both under its human-AI configuration risk.

The lawyers in Mata v. Avianca filed cases they had never read. Police who treat a face-recognition match as proof can arrest the wrong person, which is why Detroit's 2024 settlement in the Robert Williams case bars arrests based solely on facial-recognition results.

The defenses work on the people as much as the model. The EU AI Act requires that the humans overseeing a high-risk AI system be able to remain aware of automation bias and to disregard, override, or reverse the system's output. Good designs show uncertainty, require independent evidence for high-stakes decisions, and train reviewers to disagree with the machine when the evidence says so.

:::tip
**Test tip.** A "human in the loop" who approves everything adds no protection. If a question asks what makes human oversight meaningful, look for the option in which the person can, and does, independently check and override the system.
:::

## Verifying what you read

A few habits catch most false content, whether a person or a model produced it:

| Habit | What it catches |
|---|---|
| Read laterally: leave the page and see what other sources say about it and its publisher | Unreliable sites that look polished |
| Trace a claim to its original source | Statistics quoted wrongly or stripped of context |
| Check that citations exist and say what is claimed | Fabricated or misquoted sources |
| Check the date and the context of images and quotes | Old or unrelated material presented as new |
| Look for provenance, such as Content Credentials | Altered or AI-generated media |
| Pause before sharing anything that provokes a strong reaction | Content built to spread before anyone checks it |

**Lateral reading** comes from observing professional fact checkers: the best way to learn about a website is to leave it and see what other sources say about it, rather than reading the site itself more carefully from top to bottom. Use an AI system as a starting point, never as the final word, and verify independently whenever the stakes are high, as in health, law, money, or anything you will publish.

:::takeaways
- A hallucination (NIST: confabulation) is confident, plausible output that is false or unsupported: invented facts, citations, quotes, or software packages. In Mata v. Avianca (2023), lawyers were sanctioned for filing six fake opinions invented by ChatGPT.
- Models hallucinate because they generate likely text, not verified facts, and because training and benchmarks reward guessing over admitting uncertainty (Kalai and colleagues, 2025), just as a test with no wrong-answer penalty rewards guessing.
- Reduce hallucinations with retrieval grounding, cross-verification and human oversight, automatic validation, clear labels, and user training. Retrieval reduces errors but does not eliminate them.
- Misinformation is false and spread without intent to deceive; disinformation is false and spread deliberately; malinformation is true but shared to cause harm. Hallucinations are misinformation.
- Content provenance (C2PA's Content Credentials) records origin and edit history, not truth, and a missing credential does not prove a fake. The EU AI Act requires deepfakes to be labeled.
- Automation bias is the tendency to over-rely on automated output; overreliance on LLMs turns hallucinations into harm. Meaningful oversight means people can and do override the system.
- Verify by reading laterally, tracing claims to original sources, checking that citations exist, and checking dates, context, and provenance.
:::

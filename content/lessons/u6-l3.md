Within a few years, generative AI went from research labs to tools that millions of people use every day for writing, images, code, and more. Two objectives ask you to discuss the nature of generative AI and to list its uses in fields such as healthcare, research, and digital art. Expect questions that separate generative from predictive AI, match a description to a model family, and match a use to a sector.

## Generative versus discriminative models

**Generative AI** is artificial intelligence that creates new content, such as text, images, audio, video, or code, by learning the patterns in its training data and producing new examples that follow those patterns. Ask a generative model for "a lighthouse at sunset, painted in watercolor," and it produces an image that never existed before.

Most of the machine learning in earlier units was **discriminative**, also called **predictive**: the model takes an existing example and predicts a label or a number. Is this email spam? What will this house sell for? A discriminative model learns only what it needs to tell classes apart, such as the boundary between spam and not spam. A **generative model** learns the patterns of the data itself, what typical examples look like, so it can sample new ones.

| | Discriminative (predictive) model | Generative model |
|---|---|---|
| Learns | The boundary between classes, or how inputs map to an output | The patterns of the data itself |
| Produces | A label or a number for an existing example | New examples: text, images, audio, code |
| Example | Spam filter; price predictor; fraud detector | Chatbot; image generator; music generator |

How does a generative model learn those patterns? It trains on enormous datasets, mostly without human labels, by solving a task the data itself supplies: predict the next word, remove noise that was added to an image, or rebuild an input from a compressed code. To do these well, the model must absorb the statistical structure of language or images. Its outputs are new combinations of learned patterns, not stored copies, although models can occasionally reproduce memorized training data (lesson 8.5).

:::trap
**Common trap.** In technical machine learning, "generative" can also describe a classifier that models how data is produced within each class. Naive Bayes (lesson 5.8) is the classic generative classifier, and logistic regression is its discriminative partner. "Generative AI" in everyday use means models that create new content.
:::

## What generative AI can produce

Generative models now create almost every kind of digital content:

- **Text**: essays, summaries, emails, translations, chat replies.
- **Code**: whole functions, completions, explanations, tests.
- **Images**: illustrations and photorealistic pictures from a text prompt (text-to-image).
- **Audio**: speech in a chosen voice, sound effects, music.
- **Video and 3D**: short clips, animations, and objects for games.
- **Science**: designs for new molecules and proteins.
- **Synthetic data**: artificial datasets that mimic real ones, used to train other models.

Many recent models are **multimodal**, meaning they work across more than one kind of data. A multimodal model can take a photo and a typed question and answer in text, or turn a sentence into an image.

Most of these tools are built on **foundation models**. Stanford researchers coined the term in 2021 for models trained on broad data at scale that can be adapted to a wide range of downstream tasks; their examples included BERT, GPT-3, and the image generator DALL-E. One foundation model can be fine-tuned or prompted for translation, tutoring, coding, and much more. The flip side is that a flaw in a foundation model, such as a bias, carries into every application built on it.

## GANs [Competitive]

A **generative adversarial network** (GAN), introduced by Ian Goodfellow and colleagues in 2014, trains two networks against each other in a two-player game (lesson 5.10). The **generator** takes a vector of random numbers and turns it into a sample, such as an image. The **discriminator** sees a mix of real training images and generated ones and tries to label each as real or fake. The generator is rewarded for fooling the discriminator, and the discriminator for catching it, so both improve. Training succeeds when the discriminator can do no better than guessing.

GANs produce sharp, realistic images, and once trained, the generator makes each image in a single fast pass. Their training is notoriously unstable, though. A common failure is **mode collapse**: the generator finds a few outputs that fool the discriminator and keeps producing them, so it covers only a small part of the variety in real data. GANs have been used for realistic faces, turning sketches into photos, sharpening low-resolution images, and, notoriously, deepfakes (lesson 8.6).

::check{id="genai-models-01"}

## VAEs [Competitive]

A **variational autoencoder** (VAE), introduced by Diederik Kingma and Max Welling in a December 2013 paper, builds on the autoencoder of lesson 5.10. An **encoder** compresses each input into a compact representation, and a **decoder** rebuilds the input from it. The space of these compressed representations is called the **latent space**: a space of learned features in which similar items sit close together, so nearby points decode into similar outputs.

A plain autoencoder can rebuild its inputs but is poor at inventing new ones, because its latent space has gaps that decode into nonsense. A VAE fixes this by encoding each input as a small region, described by a center and a spread, and training the whole latent space to stay smooth and organized. Then almost any point you sample decodes into a realistic new example, and moving gradually from one point to another blends their features, morphing one face into another, for instance.

VAEs train stably and give a well-organized latent space, but their images tend to be blurrier than those of GANs or diffusion models.

::check{id="genai-models-04"}

## Diffusion models

A **diffusion model** learns to create images by learning to remove noise. During training, random noise is added to real images in many small steps, until nothing is left but static, and the model learns to undo one step at a time: given a noisy image, predict the noise so it can be subtracted. To generate, the model starts from pure random noise and repeats the denoising step many times. A text prompt guides each step, which is how text-to-image tools steer the picture toward "a lighthouse at sunset."

```figure
type: flow
steps: [Pure random noise, Partly denoised, Mostly denoised, Finished image]
caption: Generation in a diffusion model. Each step removes a little noise, guided by the prompt. Training runs the opposite way, adding noise to real images and teaching the model to undo it.
```

Diffusion models were introduced in 2015 by Jascha Sohl-Dickstein and colleagues, and a 2020 paper on denoising diffusion probabilistic models by Jonathan Ho, Ajay Jain, and Pieter Abbeel showed that they could produce high-quality images. They are now a leading approach to image generation. Their outputs are high in quality and variety, and their training is more stable than a GAN's, but generation is slower, because the network runs once for every denoising step instead of once per image.

::check{id="genai-models-02"}

## Autoregressive transformers

Chatbots and other text generators use **autoregressive** models: they produce a sequence one piece at a time, and each new piece is predicted from everything generated so far. A language model reads the prompt, predicts the most suitable next **token** (a word or part of a word, lesson 6.4), appends it, and repeats until the answer is done. Each output becomes part of the input for the next step.

The networks doing this are transformers (lesson 5.10), which is where GPT gets its name: **generative pre-trained transformer**. Scale turned out to matter enormously: GPT-3, described in 2020, had 175 billion parameters. The same next-token approach also generates code, and with images or sound broken into tokens, it can create those too.

| Family | How it creates | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| GAN | A generator turns noise into a sample, trained against a discriminator | Sharp images, fast generation | Unstable training; mode collapse |
| VAE | A decoder turns a point from a smooth latent space into a sample | Stable training; organized latent space | Blurrier images |
| Diffusion model | Starts from noise and removes it step by step | High quality and variety | Slow, many steps per image |
| Autoregressive transformer | Predicts the next token, appends it, repeats | Text and code; very flexible | One token at a time; can't revise earlier tokens |

:::tip
**Test tip.** Match the keyword to the family: "generator and discriminator" means a GAN; "latent space," "encoder and decoder" means a VAE; "adding and removing noise" means diffusion; "next token" means an autoregressive transformer.
:::

## Uses by sector

The guidelines name healthcare, research, and digital art as examples; the test may ask you to match any use to its sector.

- **Healthcare and drug discovery.** Drafting visit summaries and clinical notes for doctors to review; generating new candidate molecules and protein designs for chemists to make and test; creating synthetic medical images and records to train other models without exposing patients' data. Candidates designed by AI still go through laboratory work and clinical trials.
- **Research.** Summarizing large bodies of literature, drafting and debugging analysis code, and suggesting hypotheses. The 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry went half to David Baker for computational protein design and half to Demis Hassabis and John Jumper of Google DeepMind for AlphaFold2, which predicted the structures of virtually all of the roughly 200 million known proteins. (AlphaFold2 predicts structures; design tools generate new ones.)
- **Digital art and media.** Images from text prompts, concept art, music, video, game assets, and voice. These raise questions of authorship and copyright (lesson 8.1).
- **Education.** Tutoring, practice questions, explanations at different levels.
- **Business.** Marketing copy, customer-service chatbots, reports, translation.
- **Software.** Code completion, generation, and explanation, which developers must still review and test.

**Synthetic data** deserves special attention. It is artificial data generated to mimic the statistical patterns of real data, used when real data is scarce, sensitive, or missing rare cases: privacy-safe patient records, or extra examples of rare fraud. It has limits. A generator can only imitate what it learned, so synthetic data inherits the gaps and biases of its source and must be checked against real data before anyone relies on it.

::check{id="genai-uses-02"}

:::takeaways
- Generative AI creates new content (text, images, audio, video, code) from patterns learned in training data. Discriminative (predictive) models label or score existing examples.
- Generative models learn mostly without human labels, by predicting the next word, removing noise, or rebuilding inputs. They produce new combinations of learned patterns, not stored copies.
- Foundation models (a 2021 term from Stanford) are trained on broad data at scale and adapted to many tasks; their flaws carry into everything built on them. Multimodal models work across several kinds of data.
- GAN: a generator turns noise into samples and a discriminator judges real versus fake. Sharp and fast, but unstable training and mode collapse.
- VAE: an encoder maps inputs into a smooth latent space and a decoder turns sampled points into new examples. Stable but blurrier.
- Diffusion: training adds noise, and generation starts from noise and removes it step by step. High quality, slower generation.
- Autoregressive transformers predict the next token, append it, and repeat; GPT means generative pre-trained transformer.
- Uses: healthcare and drug discovery, research, digital art and media, education, business, software, and synthetic data, which inherits its source's gaps and biases.
:::

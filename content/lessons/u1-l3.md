title: Numeric and categorical data
---

Every column in a dataset holds one kind of variable, and the kind decides everything that follows: which summaries make sense, which charts to draw, and how a model must encode it. "Identify numeric and categorical data points" is an official objective, and test writers love the cases that look numeric but are not. This lesson gives you a two-second test for each classification and walks through every trick case.

## Numeric versus categorical: the do-math test

**Numeric data** (also called quantitative data) are numbers that measure or count something, so arithmetic on them makes sense. Heights, test scores, prices, and the number of messages sent per day are numeric.

**Categorical data** (also called qualitative data) are labels that place each observation into a group. Eye color, favorite sport, state of residence, and "yes" or "no" answers are categorical.

To classify a variable in seconds, ask: **would an average of these values mean anything?**

| Variable | Average meaningful? | Kind |
|---|---|---|
| Height in centimeters | Yes, average height | Numeric |
| Number of siblings | Yes, average family size | Numeric |
| Blood type | No | Categorical |
| Favorite app | No | Categorical |

:::key
**Key idea.** Numeric data measure or count, so arithmetic is meaningful. Categorical data name groups, so it is not. The question is never "is it written with digits?" but "does doing math on it make sense?"
:::

::check{id="var-num-cat-01"}

## Trick cases: numbers that are really labels

Many categorical variables are written with digits. They are still labels, because the digits identify something rather than measure it.

- **ZIP codes.** 90210 and 10001 name places. The "average ZIP code" is meaningless, so ZIP codes are categorical. (They also lose leading zeros if stored as numbers, as you saw in lesson 1.2.)
- **Phone numbers, student IDs, account numbers, Social Security numbers.** These are identifiers: categorical.
- **Jersey numbers.** Number 23 is not "more" than number 7. Categorical.
- **Ratings on a scale.** "Rate your experience from 1 (poor) to 5 (excellent)" gives ordered categories. The categories have an order, but the distance between "poor" and "fair" need not equal the distance between "good" and "excellent". Treat ratings and other **Likert scale** responses (strongly disagree to strongly agree) as ordinal categorical data.
- **Codes for yes and no.** A column with 1 for "subscribed" and 0 for "not subscribed" is still categorical: two groups. One useful fact: the mean of a 0/1 column is the proportion of 1s, so a mean of 0.35 means 35 percent subscribed.

Some numbers really are numeric even when they look like labels. A **calendar year** such as 2027 measures time: the difference between two years is meaningful. Ages and counts are numeric too.

:::trap
**Common trap.** "It is made of digits, so it is numeric." Test writers pick ZIP codes, IDs, and jersey numbers precisely because they look numeric. Apply the do-math test every time.
:::

::check{id="var-num-cat-02"}

## Discrete versus continuous numbers

Numeric data split further, into two kinds.

**Discrete** data take separate, countable values, usually whole numbers: the number of students in a class, goals in a match, clicks on an ad, or cars in a lot. There is no such thing as 2.5 students.

**Continuous** data can take any value within a range, limited only by how precisely you measure: height, weight, time, distance, and temperature. A runner's time could be 21.4 minutes, 21.43, or 21.4317, depending on the stopwatch.

The fastest test: **did the value come from counting or from measuring?** Counting gives discrete data; measuring gives continuous data.

| Counting (discrete) | Measuring (continuous) |
|---|---|
| Number of laps run | Time to run a lap |
| Number of rainy days | Millimeters of rain |
| Number of customers | Amount each customer spent |

Two subtleties show up on hard questions:

- **Rounding does not make data discrete.** Rainfall recorded to the nearest 0.1 millimeter is still continuous; the rounding comes from the instrument, not from the quantity.
- **Averages of discrete data can be fractions.** A store that averages 12.4 customers per hour is still counting customers. The variable is discrete; only its mean is not a whole number.

## Nominal and ordinal categories

Categorical data split into two kinds as well.

**Nominal** data are categories with no natural order: eye color, blood type, country, sport, or type of pet. You can count how many fall in each group, but no category is "more" than another.

**Ordinal** data are categories with a meaningful order, but with gaps that are not necessarily equal or even known: education level (high school, bachelor's, master's), letter grades, small, medium, and large, finishing place in a race, or a satisfaction rating from "very unsatisfied" to "very satisfied".

The race example shows why the gaps matter. First place might beat second by a tenth of a second while second beats third by a full minute. The order is real; the spacing is not captured.

::check{id="var-levels-02"}

## Levels of measurement [Competitive]

In 1946 the psychologist S. S. Stevens organized variables into four **levels of measurement**, each adding a property to the one before. Remember them in order with the word **NOIR**:

| Level | Adds this property | Valid operations | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Nominal** | Categories | Equal or not equal; count | Blood type, country, jersey number |
| **Ordinal** | Order | Greater or less than; rank | Letter grades, ratings, finishing place |
| **Interval** | Equal spacing | Add and subtract differences | Temperature in Celsius or Fahrenheit, calendar year |
| **Ratio** | A true zero | Multiply and divide; ratios | Height, weight, income, time taken, counts, Kelvin |

The key distinction at the top is the **true zero**. On a ratio scale, zero means "none of the quantity": zero kilograms means no weight, so 20 kilograms really is twice 10 kilograms. On an interval scale, zero is an arbitrary point. Zero degrees Celsius is just the freezing point of water, not "no temperature", so 20 degrees Celsius is **not** twice as hot as 10 degrees Celsius. Differences still work on an interval scale (a 10-degree rise is the same size anywhere on the scale), but ratios do not.

::::example[Classify by level]
Classify each variable: (1) T-shirt size (S, M, L, XL), (2) temperature in Fahrenheit, (3) number of app downloads, (4) favorite color.

:::step
T-shirt sizes are categories with an order, but the gaps are not measured: ordinal.
:::

:::step
Fahrenheit has equal spacing, but 0 degrees Fahrenheit does not mean "no heat": interval.
:::

:::step
A download count has a true zero (no downloads) and 200 really is twice 100: ratio.
:::

:::step
Colors have no order: nominal.
:::

:::answer
Ordinal, interval, ratio, nominal.
:::
::::

:::tip
**Test tip.** To separate interval from ratio, ask: "Does zero mean none of it?" If yes, ratio. Temperature in Celsius or Fahrenheit and calendar years are the classic interval examples; almost every other measurement is ratio.
:::

## Which summaries fit which level [Champion]

Because each level adds a property, each one also unlocks more summaries. Stevens listed which statistics are permissible at each level, and the list is cumulative: anything allowed at a lower level is allowed at a higher one.

| Level | Center | Spread and other statistics |
|---|---|---|
| Nominal | Mode | Counts and proportions |
| Ordinal | Median (and mode) | Percentiles, range of ranks |
| Interval | Mean (and median, mode) | Standard deviation |
| Ratio | All of these | Plus ratios and the coefficient of variation |

So the average jersey number is meaningless (nominal), the median satisfaction rating is sensible (ordinal), the mean temperature is fine (interval), and the coefficient of variation from lesson 2.3 needs a true zero (ratio). Statisticians debate how strictly to apply these rules (people often average Likert ratings in practice), so treat the table as the standard guideline that test questions follow.

The level also guides later steps. Nominal and ordinal variables are shown with bar charts rather than histograms, and they must be encoded as numbers before most models can use them: one-hot encoding for nominal data and ordered label encoding for ordinal data, both covered in lesson 1.7.

::check{id="var-levels-06"}

:::takeaways
- Numeric data measure or count (arithmetic makes sense); categorical data name groups. Use the do-math test: would an average mean anything?
- ZIP codes, phone numbers, IDs, and jersey numbers are categorical. Ratings and Likert responses are ordinal categorical. A 0/1 yes-or-no code is categorical, and its mean is the proportion of 1s.
- Discrete data come from counting; continuous data come from measuring. Rounding does not make data discrete, and a discrete variable can have a fractional mean.
- Nominal categories have no order; ordinal categories have an order with unmeasured gaps.
- Levels of measurement (Stevens, 1946), NOIR: nominal, ordinal, interval (equal spacing, arbitrary zero), ratio (true zero, so "twice as much" is meaningful).
- Celsius, Fahrenheit, and calendar years are interval. Height, weight, income, time taken, counts, and Kelvin are ratio.
- Mode for nominal, median for ordinal, mean and standard deviation for interval, and ratios and the coefficient of variation only for ratio data.
:::

title: Scatterplots, correlation, and causation

A scatterplot is the first thing to draw when you suspect two numeric variables are related, and "does this prove that one causes the other?" is one of the most common questions in all of data science. The test asks you to describe scatterplots, read a fitted line and its residuals, spot points that distort a correlation, and, above all, to recognize when a causal claim is not justified. This lesson covers all of it, ending with Simpson's paradox, a classic champion-level trap.

## Direction, form, strength, and outliers

A **scatterplot** shows the relationship between two numeric variables measured on the same individuals, with one dot per individual. Put the **explanatory variable** (the suspected influence) on the horizontal x-axis and the **response variable** (the outcome) on the vertical y-axis.

```figure
type: scatter
caption: Hours studied and exam scores for 20 students. The relationship is positive, linear, and strong (r = 0.97). The line is the least-squares regression line.
xLabel: Hours studied
yLabel: Exam score
series:
  - points: [[6.5, 80], [6.6, 87], [4.4, 72], [2.6, 59], [0.9, 55], [3.4, 71], [3.6, 60], [0.8, 51], [0.9, 48], [8.0, 91], [5.4, 71], [2.3, 61], [3.8, 69], [7.8, 89], [7.2, 82], [6.8, 83], [3.4, 61], [4.2, 64], [5.6, 80], [1.0, 48]]
line: { slope: 5.53, intercept: 45.56, label: fitted line }
```

Describe any scatterplot with four features:

- **Direction**: positive (as x increases, y tends to increase) or negative (y tends to decrease).
- **Form**: linear (the points follow a straight line), curved, or clustered into groups.
- **Strength**: how tightly the points follow the form. Tight means strong; a loose cloud means weak.
- **Unusual points**: outliers far from the overall pattern.

For a linear relationship, the correlation coefficient r (lesson 2.4) puts direction and strength into one number from -1 to 1. A tight upward band like the one above has r close to 1, a loose upward cloud has r around 0.5, and a shapeless cloud has r near 0.

Form matters because r measures only *linear* association. The plot below shows a strong relationship that r nearly misses: electricity use is high on cold days (heating) and on hot days (cooling) and low in between.

```figure
type: scatter
caption: Daily temperature and household electricity use. The U-shaped relationship is strong, yet r is only 0.18 because the pattern is not a straight line.
xLabel: Daily high temperature (degrees F)
yLabel: Electricity use (kWh)
series:
  - points: [[56, 38], [84, 43], [39, 41], [46, 36], [84, 40], [61, 30], [61, 34], [39, 38], [21, 61], [95, 54], [27, 54], [88, 45], [49, 32], [96, 57], [52, 32], [95, 52], [64, 33], [39, 35]]
```

An **outlier** in a scatterplot is a point that falls far from the overall pattern. An **influential point** is one whose removal would change the fitted line or r a lot. Points far out in the x-direction, away from the rest of the data, are the usual suspects.

```figure
type: scatter
caption: Ten points with almost no relationship (r = 0.18), plus one point far to the right. With that single point included, r jumps to 0.87.
xLabel: x
yLabel: y
series:
  - points: [[2, 15], [3, 15], [3.5, 24], [4, 19], [4.5, 16], [5, 15], [5.5, 22], [6, 16], [6.5, 15], [7, 22], [16, 45]]
highlight: [[16, 45]]
```

:::trap
**Common trap.** A correlation near 0 does not mean "no relationship." It means no *linear* relationship. Always look at the plot: a strong curve or a single influential point can make r badly misleading.
:::

## Lines of best fit and residuals [Competitive]

The line in the first plot is the **least-squares regression line**, the line that makes the sum of the squared vertical distances from the points to the line as small as possible. Rounded, its equation is:

$$
\hat{y} = 45.6 + 5.5x
$$

Here $$\hat{y}$$ ("y-hat") is the predicted exam score and x is the hours studied. The slope says that each additional hour of study is associated with about 5.5 more points, on average. Lesson 3.7 covers interpreting slopes and intercepts in depth.

A **residual** is the vertical gap between a point and the line:

$$
\text{residual} = \text{actual } y - \text{predicted } \hat{y}
$$

::::example[Compute a residual]
A student studied 5.6 hours and scored 80. Find the residual and say whether the line overpredicted or underpredicted.

:::step
Predict with the line: 45.6 + 5.5 × 5.6 = 45.6 + 30.8 = 76.4.
:::

:::step
Subtract the prediction from the actual score: 80 - 76.4 = 3.6.
:::

:::answer
The residual is 3.6. It is positive, so the point lies above the line, and the line underpredicted this student's score by 3.6 points.
:::
::::

The sign rule is worth memorizing: a point **above** the line has a **positive** residual (the model underpredicted), and a point **below** the line has a **negative** residual (the model overpredicted). A student who studied 3.6 hours and scored 60 has a predicted score of 45.6 + 5.5 × 3.6 = 65.4, so the residual is 60 - 65.4 = -5.4.

A **residual plot** graphs the residuals against x. If a straight line fits well, the residuals scatter randomly around 0 with no pattern. A curved pattern in the residuals means a straight line is the wrong form for the data.

## Correlation is not causation

Two variables can be strongly associated without either one causing the other. When a study finds that A and B move together, there are at least three explanations besides "A causes B":

1. **Confounding**: a third variable drives both. More ice cream is sold on days with more drownings because hot weather increases both swimming and ice cream sales.
2. **Reverse causation**: B causes A. Cities with more police officers tend to have more crime, but high crime may be what leads a city to hire more officers.
3. **Coincidence**: the association arose by chance. Search through enough pairs of variables and some will line up purely by luck.

The standard way to establish cause and effect is a **randomized experiment**: subjects are randomly assigned to a treatment group or a control group, and the outcomes are compared. Random assignment balances confounding variables between the groups on average, so a difference in outcomes can be credited to the treatment. An **observational study** (lesson 1.6), which only records what happens, can show association but cannot by itself establish causation.

:::key
**Key idea.** Association plus random assignment supports a causal conclusion. Association alone supports only "is associated with," never "causes."
:::

:::tip
**Test tip.** When a stem says "a study found that people who do X have more Y" and asks which conclusion is justified, the right answer almost always says "associated with" or names a possible confounder, unless the stem describes random assignment.
:::

::check{id="causation-01"}

## Confounding and lurking variables

A **confounding variable** is related to both the explanatory variable and the response, so its effect is tangled up with the effect you are trying to measure. A **lurking variable** is a variable that was not measured or included in the analysis but affects the relationship; the two terms are often used for the same idea.

Practice spotting them:

- Children with larger shoe sizes score higher on reading tests. The lurking variable is **age**: older children have bigger feet and read better.
- People who carry lighters are more likely to develop lung disease. The confounder is **smoking**, which explains both.
- Students who use a tutoring service earn lower grades than students who do not. The confounder may be **prior achievement**: students who are struggling are the ones who sign up for tutoring.

Analysts handle confounders in three main ways: by running a randomized experiment, by comparing like with like (analyzing each group of the confounder separately), or by including the confounder as another variable in a multiple regression model (lesson 3.6).

::check{id="causation-02"}

## Simpson's paradox [Champion]

**Simpson's paradox** occurs when a trend that holds in every group reverses when the groups are combined. It happens when a lurking variable is spread unevenly across the groups being compared.

Consider two hospitals (hypothetical numbers). Patients arrive in mild or severe condition:

| | Hospital A survived | Hospital A rate | Hospital B survived | Hospital B rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mild cases | 90 of 100 | 90% | 340 of 400 | 85% |
| Severe cases | 300 of 400 | 75% | 60 of 100 | 60% |
| All cases | 390 of 500 | 78% | 400 of 500 | 80% |

Hospital A has the higher survival rate for mild cases (90% versus 85%) **and** for severe cases (75% versus 60%), yet the lower rate overall (78% versus 80%). The reason is the lurking variable, severity: 400 of Hospital A's 500 patients were severe cases, while 400 of Hospital B's were mild. Hospital A's overall rate is dragged down by its harder caseload, not by worse care.

The lesson is to compare like with like. When groups differ on a variable that affects the outcome, the combined numbers can point the wrong way, and the group-by-group comparison tells the truer story.

::check{id="causation-03"}

:::takeaways
- In a scatterplot, put the explanatory variable on x and the response on y. Describe direction, form, strength, and unusual points.
- r measures only linear association. A strong curve can have r near 0, and one influential point can make r large or small.
- The least-squares line minimizes the sum of squared residuals. Residual = actual - predicted.
- A point above the line has a positive residual (underpredicted); a point below has a negative residual (overpredicted). A pattern in a residual plot means a straight line is the wrong form.
- Correlation is not causation: consider confounding, reverse causation, and coincidence.
- A randomized experiment is the standard way to establish causation; observational studies show association.
- A confounding (or lurking) variable is linked to both variables, such as age for shoe size and reading.
- Simpson's paradox: a trend in every group can reverse when the groups are combined, because a lurking variable is spread unevenly.
:::

title: Samples, the central limit theorem, and inference
---

Data scientists almost never measure a whole population. They survey 1,000 voters to learn about millions, or test a model on a sample of users to predict how everyone will respond. This lesson explains why that works: why sample averages behave predictably, how sample size controls precision, and what confidence intervals, p-values, and statistical power mean. It also explains why the normal distribution from lesson 2.5 is so important. Test questions here are mostly about meaning and direction (what grows, what shrinks, what a statement really says), with a few quick calculations.

## Population versus sample, parameter versus statistic

A **population** is the entire group you want to learn about: every student at a school, every customer of a store. A **sample** is the part of the population you actually measure.

The same split applies to the numbers you compute:

| | Describes | Examples | Symbols |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Parameter** | The population | True mean, true SD, true proportion | $$\mu$$, $$\sigma$$, $$p$$ |
| **Statistic** | A sample | Sample mean, sample SD, sample proportion | $$\bar{x}$$, $$s$$, $$\hat{p}$$ |

A memory aid: **p**arameter goes with **p**opulation, **s**tatistic goes with **s**ample. Usually the parameter is unknown, and the statistic is your estimate of it.

For a statistic to be a good estimate, the sample must be **representative**, which is why samples are drawn at **random**. Lesson 3.4 shows what goes wrong when they are not.

## Sampling distributions

Take a random sample of 50 students and compute their mean height. Take another random sample of 50 and you will get a slightly different mean. This sample-to-sample variation is called **sampling variability**.

Now imagine repeating that thousands of times and plotting all the sample means. That distribution of a statistic over many samples is the **sampling distribution**. For the sample mean it has two crucial properties:

- It is **centered on the population mean** $$\mu$$. On average, the sample mean hits the target; it is an unbiased estimate.
- It is **narrower than the population**. Averages of 50 heights vary much less than individual heights, because tall and short individuals tend to cancel out within each sample.

::check{id="clt-02"}

## The central limit theorem

The **central limit theorem** (CLT) says that for a large enough sample size, the sampling distribution of the sample mean is **approximately normal, whatever the shape of the population**. The population can be skewed, lumpy, or flat; the means of large samples still pile up in a bell curve, centered on $$\mu$$.

A common rule of thumb calls a sample "large enough" when $$n \ge 30$$. Treat that as a guideline, not a law: an extremely skewed population may need a much larger sample, while a population that is already normal gives normal sample means at any sample size.

Do not confuse the CLT with the **law of large numbers**. The law of large numbers says that as the sample grows, the sample mean gets closer to the population mean. The CLT says what **shape** the distribution of sample means takes. One is about closeness, the other about shape.

The CLT is why normal-based methods work so widely: confidence intervals, A/B tests, and quality control all lean on it, even when the raw data are not normal.

:::trap
**Common trap.** The CLT does not say that large samples make the **data** normal. A sample of 1,000 incomes is still skewed. It is the distribution of the **sample mean**, across many samples, that becomes normal.
:::

::check{id="clt-03"}

## Standard error and sample size [Competitive]

The standard deviation of the sampling distribution has its own name, the **standard error** (SE). For the sample mean:

$$
SE = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}
$$

When $$\sigma$$ is unknown, the sample standard deviation $$s$$ stands in for it. The standard error measures how much the sample mean typically varies from sample to sample, which is to say how precise it is as an estimate.

The square root controls everything:

- If $$\sigma = 20$$ and $$n = 100$$, then $$SE = 20 / \sqrt{100} = 2$$.
- Quadruple the sample to $$n = 400$$, and $$SE = 20 / 20 = 1$$: four times the data cuts the error only in **half**.
- To cut the standard error in half, you need **four times** the sample size.

:::tip
**Test tip.** Whenever a question changes the sample size, think square root. Multiply n by 4, divide the SE (and the margin of error) by 2. Multiply n by 9, divide by 3.
:::

::check{id="clt-04"}

## Confidence intervals and margin of error [Competitive]

A single number, such as a sample mean of 72, gives no sense of its own precision. A **confidence interval** reports a range instead:

$$
\text{estimate} \pm \text{margin of error}, \qquad \text{margin of error} = z^* \times SE
$$

For 95% confidence, $$z^* = 1.96$$ (lesson 2.5). With a sample mean of 72 and SE of 2, the margin of error is $$1.96 \times 2 = 3.92$$, and the 95% confidence interval runs from 68.08 to 75.92.

What does "95% confident" mean? It describes the **method**: if you took many random samples and built an interval from each one in this way, about 95% of those intervals would contain the true population mean. It does not mean that 95% of the data fall inside the interval.

Two levers move the width:

- **Higher confidence means a wider interval.** To be 99% confident, use $$z^* = 2.576$$ instead of 1.96. To be more sure of catching the true value, you need a wider net.
- **Larger samples mean a narrower interval**, because the SE shrinks with $$\sqrt{n}$$.

The margin of error in election polls works the same way. For a result near 50%, the 95% margin is about $$1.96 \times \sqrt{0.25 / n}$$, which is about 3.1 percentage points for a poll of 1,000 people.

## p-values, significance, errors, and power [Champion]

A **hypothesis test** asks whether data provide convincing evidence of an effect. It starts with a **null hypothesis** ($$H_0$$), usually "no effect" or "no difference," and an **alternative hypothesis** ($$H_a$$), the effect you are looking for.

The **p-value** is the probability of getting results at least as extreme as the ones observed, **assuming the null hypothesis is true**. A small p-value means the data would be surprising if there were no effect. If the p-value falls below a chosen **significance level** $$\alpha$$ (often 0.05), the result is called **statistically significant** and the null hypothesis is rejected.

:::trap
**Common trap.** A p-value is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true, and it is not the probability that the result happened "by chance" in a general sense. It is computed by assuming the null hypothesis is true.
:::

Any test can be wrong in two ways:

| | The null hypothesis is true | The null hypothesis is false |
|---|---|---|
| **Reject it** | **Type I error** (a false positive) | Correct |
| **Do not reject it** | Correct | **Type II error** (a false negative) |

The probability of a Type I error is $$\alpha$$. A drug study that declares a useless drug effective has made a Type I error; one that misses a drug that really works has made a Type II error. These are exactly the false positives and false negatives you will meet in the confusion matrix (lesson 3.8).

**Power** is the probability that a test detects an effect that really exists, which is 1 minus the probability of a Type II error. Power increases with a **larger sample size**, a **larger true effect**, less variability in the data, and a larger $$\alpha$$ (at the cost of more Type I errors).

Finally, **statistical significance is not practical significance**. With millions of observations, a difference far too small to matter can still produce a tiny p-value. Always ask how big the effect is, not only whether it is significant.

:::takeaways
- Parameters describe populations ($$\mu$$, $$\sigma$$, p); statistics describe samples ($$\bar{x}$$, s, $$\hat{p}$$). Random samples make statistics good estimates.
- The sampling distribution of the mean is centered on $$\mu$$ and narrower than the population.
- CLT: for large n (rule of thumb n of 30 or more; more for very skewed populations), sample means are approximately normal whatever the population's shape. The law of large numbers is about the sample mean getting close to $$\mu$$.
- Standard error of the mean = $$\sigma / \sqrt{n}$$. Four times the sample halves the SE.
- Confidence interval = estimate plus or minus $$z^*$$ x SE. 95% uses 1.96. More confidence means wider; more data means narrower. "95% confident" describes the method's success rate.
- p-value: probability of results at least this extreme if $$H_0$$ is true. Below $$\alpha$$ (often 0.05) is statistically significant.
- Type I error = false positive (rejecting a true $$H_0$$, probability $$\alpha$$); Type II error = false negative. Power = 1 minus P(Type II) and rises with sample size and effect size.
- Statistical significance is not the same as practical importance.
:::

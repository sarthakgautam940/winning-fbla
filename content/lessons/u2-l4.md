title: Covariance and correlation

So far every statistic has described one variable. Data science is usually about two or more at once: does studying more go with higher scores, does a higher price go with fewer sales? Covariance and correlation measure how two numeric variables move together. The official objective names covariance alongside variance and standard deviation, and correlation appears throughout the test, in charts, regression, and machine learning. This lesson teaches both, the reason correlation is the one you usually report, and the two things correlation cannot see.

## Covariance: moving together or apart

Take two variables measured on the same subjects, such as hours studied (x) and quiz score (y) for five students. **Covariance** measures whether the two tend to be above or below their means at the same time.

- **Positive covariance**: when x is above its mean, y tends to be above its mean too. The variables move together.
- **Negative covariance**: when x is above its mean, y tends to be below its mean. The variables move in opposite directions.
- **Covariance near zero**: no linear tendency either way.

The formula multiplies each subject's two deviations and averages the products. For a sample:

$$
\text{cov}(x, y) = \frac{\sum (x - \bar{x})(y - \bar{y})}{n - 1}
$$

When both deviations have the same sign, their product is positive; when the signs differ, it is negative. The sum tells you which kind of pairing dominates. (For a whole population, divide by N instead.)

::::example[Computing a covariance]
Five students' hours studied and quiz scores are (1, 60), (2, 65), (3, 70), (4, 80), (5, 85). Find the sample covariance.

:::step
Means: hours average 3 and scores average 72.
:::

:::step
Deviations of hours: negative 2, negative 1, 0, 1, 2. Deviations of scores: negative 12, negative 7, negative 2, 8, 13.
:::

:::step
Multiply each pair: 24, 7, 0, 8, 26. The sum is 65.
:::

:::step
Divide by n - 1 = 4: 65 / 4 = 16.25.
:::

:::answer
The sample covariance is 16.25 hour-points. It is positive, so more study time goes with higher scores.
:::
::::

::check{id="covariance-01"}

## Why covariance is hard to compare [Competitive]

Look at the units of that answer: **hour-points**. Covariance is measured in the product of the two variables' units, which has no everyday meaning. Worse, its size depends on the scale. Record study time in minutes instead of hours and every x-deviation is 60 times larger, so the covariance becomes 975, even though the relationship has not changed at all.

That means the size of a covariance cannot tell you how strong a relationship is. A covariance of 975 might be weak and one of 2 might be very strong; it depends entirely on the units. Covariance tells you the **direction** reliably; for **strength**, you need correlation.

Two more facts appear at the champion level:

- The covariance of a variable with itself is its **variance**: $$\text{cov}(x, x) = s_x^2$$.
- The variance of a sum depends on covariance: $$\text{Var}(X + Y) = \text{Var}(X) + \text{Var}(Y) + 2\,\text{Cov}(X, Y)$$. When two quantities move together, their total is more variable than the parts alone suggest.

::check{id="covariance-02"}

## Correlation r: from -1 to 1

The **correlation coefficient** r (Pearson's r) fixes covariance's scale problem by dividing by both standard deviations:

$$
r = \frac{\text{cov}(x, y)}{s_x \, s_y}
$$

Dividing cancels the units, so r is a pure number, always between negative 1 and 1:

| Value of r | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 1 | Perfect positive linear relationship: the points lie exactly on an upward line |
| Close to 1 | Strong positive linear relationship |
| Close to 0 | Little or no linear relationship |
| Close to -1 | Strong negative linear relationship |
| -1 | Perfect negative linear relationship |

The sign gives the **direction** and the size gives the **strength** of the linear relationship. As a rough guide, |r| above 0.8 is strong, 0.5 to 0.8 moderate, and below 0.3 weak, though fields differ. A correlation of negative 0.85 is stronger than one of 0.7: strength is about the size, not the sign.

For the study data, the standard deviations are about 1.58 hours and 10.37 points, so r = 16.25 / (1.58 x 10.37), about 0.99: a very strong positive relationship. Software agrees:

```python run
import numpy as np

hours = [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]
score = [60, 65, 70, 80, 85]
print(np.cov(hours, score)[0, 1])
print(round(np.corrcoef(hours, score)[0, 1], 3))
```

```output
16.25
0.991
```

Two properties make r convenient. It is **unit-free**, so converting hours to minutes or Celsius to Fahrenheit leaves r unchanged (multiplying one variable by a negative number would flip its sign). And it is **symmetric**: the correlation of x with y equals the correlation of y with x.

::check{id="correlation-01"}

## Reading r from a scatterplot

Test questions often show a scatterplot and ask which r is most likely. Judge two things: the **direction** of the trend (upward is positive, downward is negative) and how **tightly** the points hug a straight line (tighter means a larger |r|).

:::tip
**Test tip.** The slope of the trend does not set r. A shallow line of points that hug it perfectly has r = 1; a steep but scattered cloud has a smaller r. Ask "how close to a line?", not "how steep?"
:::

## r squared [Competitive]

Squaring r gives $$r^2$$, which in simple linear regression is called the **coefficient of determination**: the proportion of the variation in y that is explained by its linear relationship with x. If the correlation between study hours and scores is 0.8, then $$r^2 = 0.64$$, so 64% of the variation in scores is explained by study hours, and 36% comes from everything else.

Because squaring removes the sign, r = 0.8 and r = negative 0.8 both give $$r^2 = 0.64$$. $$r^2$$ tells you how much is explained, not the direction. You will meet $$r^2$$ again in regression (Unit 3).

::check{id="correlation-03"}

## What r cannot see: curves and outliers [Competitive]

Correlation has two blind spots, and test writers target both.

**r only measures linear relationships.** The points below follow the curve $$y = x^2$$ perfectly, a completely predictable relationship, yet r is exactly 0, because the left half falls while the right half rises.

```figure
type: scatter
caption: A perfect U-shaped relationship with r = 0. Correlation misses any pattern that is not a straight line.
xLabel: x
yLabel: y
series:
  - points: [[-3, 9], [-2, 4], [-1, 1], [0, 0], [1, 1], [2, 4], [3, 9]]
```

So r = 0 means "no **linear** relationship," not "no relationship."

**r is sensitive to outliers.** A single point far from the rest can raise or lower r dramatically. In one textbook example, a dataset's correlation is 0.66 with an outlier and 0.91 after removing it. The rule that follows: always look at the scatterplot before trusting r.

And as you will see in Unit 3, even a very strong correlation does not show that one variable causes the other.

::check{id="correlation-04"}

:::takeaways
- Covariance shows direction: positive means the variables move together, negative means opposite, near 0 means no linear tendency. Sample formula: sum of (x - mean)(y - mean), divided by n - 1.
- Covariance's units are the product of the two units, and its size depends on scale, so it cannot measure strength.
- cov(x, x) = variance. Var(X + Y) = Var X + Var Y + 2 Cov(X, Y).
- r = cov / (sx sy) is unit-free and runs from -1 to 1. The sign is direction; the size is the strength of the linear relationship.
- r is unchanged by changing units and is symmetric in x and y. Judge r from a scatterplot by tightness to a line, not steepness.
- r squared is the share of the variation in y explained by the linear relationship. It ignores the sign.
- r misses curves (a perfect U can give r = 0) and is pulled by outliers. Always look at the plot.
:::

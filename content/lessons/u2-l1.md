The mean, median, mode, and range are the first numbers anyone computes about a dataset, and the official objective says it directly: "Calculate the mean, median, mode, and range of a dataset." The calculations are simple, which is exactly why test writers load them with traps: an unsorted list, a frequency table, a weighted grade, a missing value, an outlier. This lesson makes each calculation fast and shows you every trap before the test does.

## The mean, and what it balances

The **mean** (the arithmetic average) is the sum of the values divided by how many there are:

$$
\bar{x} = \frac{\sum x}{n}
$$

Here $$\sum x$$ means "add up all the values" and $$n$$ is how many there are. For the values 4, 8, 6, 5, 7, the sum is 30 and n is 5, so the mean is 6. The mean of a whole population is written $$\mu$$; the mean of a sample is written $$\bar{x}$$.

The mean is the **balance point** of the data: if the values were weights on a ruler, the ruler would balance at the mean. That is why the deviations from the mean always sum to zero (you will use this fact in lesson 2.3).

Two shortcuts save time on the test:

- **Evenly spaced values**: the mean is the average of the first and last. The mean of 12, 15, 18, 21, 24 is (12 + 24) / 2 = 18.
- **Adding or removing one value**: work with totals. Nine values with mean 50 total 450. Add a value of 70, and the new mean is (450 + 70) / 10 = 52.

## Means from frequency tables and weighted means

Data often arrive as a **frequency table**: each value with a count of how many times it occurs. Multiply each value by its frequency, add those products, and divide by the total count.

::::example[Mean from a frequency table]
Twenty students took a 10-point quiz. Find the mean score.

| Score | 6 | 7 | 8 | 9 | 10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Students | 2 | 5 | 8 | 4 | 1 |

:::step
Multiply each score by its count: 6 x 2 = 12, 7 x 5 = 35, 8 x 8 = 64, 9 x 4 = 36, 10 x 1 = 10.
:::

:::step
Add the products: 12 + 35 + 64 + 36 + 10 = 157. That is the sum of all 20 scores.
:::

:::step
Divide by the number of students, 2 + 5 + 8 + 4 + 1 = 20: 157 / 20 = 7.85.
:::

:::answer
The mean score is 7.85.
:::
::::

A **weighted mean** gives some values more influence than others:

$$
\bar{x}_w = \frac{\sum w x}{\sum w}
$$

If homework counts 20%, tests 50%, and the final 30%, and a student scores 90, 80, and 70, the grade is $$0.2 \times 90 + 0.5 \times 80 + 0.3 \times 70 = 18 + 40 + 21 = 79$$. The weights already add to 1, so there is nothing more to divide by. The plain average of 90, 80, and 70 would be 80, which is wrong here.

The same idea combines two groups. If Class A has 20 students averaging 80 and Class B has 30 students averaging 70, the combined mean is

$$
\frac{20 \times 80 + 30 \times 70}{20 + 30} = \frac{3700}{50} = 74
$$

:::trap
**Common trap.** Never average two averages when the groups differ in size. The mean of 80 and 70 is 75, but the larger, lower-scoring class pulls the true combined mean down to 74.
:::

::check{id="mean-02"}

## Working backward: a missing value from the mean [Competitive]

If you know the mean and the count, you know the **total**: total = n x mean. That turns "missing value" questions into subtraction.

::::example[Finding a missing value]
The mean of five numbers is 12. Four of them are 10, 15, 8, and 14. What is the fifth?

:::step
The five numbers must total 5 x 12 = 60.
:::

:::step
The four known numbers total 10 + 15 + 8 + 14 = 47.
:::

:::answer
The fifth number is 60 - 47 = 13.
:::
::::

The same move answers "what do I need" questions. A student averaging 85 on four tests wants an 88 average after the fifth. The five tests must total 5 x 88 = 440, the four so far total 4 x 85 = 340, so the fifth test needs 100.

::check{id="mean-03"}

## The median: sort first

The **median** is the middle value of the data **once it is sorted**. Half the values lie at or below it and half at or above.

- With an **odd** number of values, the median is the single middle value, at position $$(n + 1) / 2$$.
- With an **even** number of values, the median is the average of the two middle values.

For 7, 2, 9, 4, 5: sorted, the data are 2, 4, 5, 7, 9, so the median is the third value, 5. For 3, 8, 1, 6, 10, 4: sorted, the data are 1, 3, 4, 6, 8, 10, so the median is (4 + 6) / 2 = 5.

From a frequency table, count your way to the middle position. In the quiz table above there are 20 scores, so the median is the average of the 10th and 11th. Running totals: 2 students scored 6, 7 scored 7 or less, and 15 scored 8 or less. Both the 10th and 11th scores are 8, so the median is 8.

The median ignores how extreme the extreme values are. In 4, 7, 9, 12, 15, the median is 9; change the 15 to 150 and the median is still 9. Statistics that barely move when an extreme value changes are called **resistant**.

:::trap
**Common trap.** The middle of an **unsorted** list is not the median. In 7, 2, 9, 4, 5 the middle number as written is 9, which is a distractor, not the median. Sort first, every time.
:::

## The mode and the range

The **mode** is the value that appears most often. A dataset can have one mode, two (**bimodal**), several (**multimodal**), or none at all if every value appears equally often. In 2, 4, 4, 6, 8, 8, 10, both 4 and 8 appear twice, so the data are bimodal with modes 4 and 8. In 5, 8, 12, 15, 21, there is no mode.

The mode is the only measure of center that works for categorical data. If a survey finds 14 people prefer dogs, 11 cats, and 3 fish, the mode is "dog"; there is no mean or median of pet types.

The **range** is the simplest measure of spread: maximum minus minimum. For 15, 4, 22, 9, 18, the range is 22 - 4 = 18. Because it uses only the two most extreme values, a single outlier can change it dramatically, which makes the range a crude measure. Lesson 2.2 introduces the interquartile range, which fixes that weakness.

## Outliers and skew: which center to report [Competitive]

An **outlier** is a value far from the rest of the data. Watch what one does to each statistic. Five salaries at a small firm are 40, 42, 45, 48, and 50 thousand dollars, so the mean and median are both 45. Add the owner's salary of 900 thousand:

| Statistic | Before | After adding 900 |
|---|---|---|
| Mean | 45 | 187.5 |
| Median | 45 | 46.5 |
| Range | 10 | 860 |

The mean jumps by more than 140 thousand and no longer describes anyone; the median barely moves. The mean and the range are **not resistant**; the median is.

The same pull shows up in the shape of a distribution. When a few very large values stretch out a long right tail, the distribution is **skewed right**, and the mean is pulled above the median. A long left tail (**skewed left**) pulls the mean below the median. In a **symmetric** distribution the mean and median are about equal.

```figure

caption: Right-skewed household incomes. The long tail of high incomes pulls the mean (about 60 thousand dollars) above the median, which falls in the 40 to 60 thousand bin.
xLabel: Household income (thousands of dollars)
yLabel: Households

  - { from: 20, to: 40, count: 12 }
  - { from: 40, to: 60, count: 18 }
  - { from: 60, to: 80, count: 10 }
  - { from: 80, to: 100, count: 5 }
  - { from: 100, to: 120, count: 3 }
  - { from: 120, to: 140, count: 1 }
  - { from: 140, to: 160, count: 1 }
```

In a right-skewed distribution with a single peak, the usual order is mode, then median, then mean, from left to right, with the mean farthest out toward the tail. In a left-skewed one the order reverses.

So which center should you report? For roughly symmetric data without outliers, the mean, because it uses every value. For skewed data or data with outliers, such as incomes, home prices, or response times, the median, because it describes a typical value. This is why news reports cite **median** household income.

:::tip
**Test tip.** You can read skew from two numbers. Mean well above median: skewed right. Mean well below median: skewed left. A mean of 52 with a median of 61 means a long left tail.
:::

::check{id="center-choice-01"}

Try it: build a small dataset, then drag one value far to the right and watch the mean chase it while the median stays put.

:::takeaways
- Mean = sum / n, the balance point. Evenly spaced values: (first + last) / 2.
- Frequency table: multiply each value by its count, add, and divide by the total count. Weighted mean = sum of (weight x value) / sum of weights.
- Combine groups by totals: (n1 x mean1 + n2 x mean2) / (n1 + n2). Never average unequal groups' averages.
- Missing value: total = n x mean, then subtract the known values.
- Median: sort first. Odd n gives the middle value at position (n + 1) / 2; even n gives the average of the two middle values.
- Mode: most frequent value; there can be none, one, or several. It is the only center for categorical data.
- Range = max - min, and it is very sensitive to outliers.
- Outliers pull the mean, not the median. Skewed right: mean > median. Skewed left: mean < median. Report the median for skewed data.
:::

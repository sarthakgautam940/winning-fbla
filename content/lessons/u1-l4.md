Every number, letter, pixel, and sound a computer stores comes down to bits: 0s and 1s. Data scientists meet binary in file sizes and encodings, and hexadecimal in color codes, memory addresses, and IDs. The official objective says it plainly: "Convert among common data representations (e.g., binary, hexadecimal, decimal)." Expect several quick conversions on the test, each with wrong answers built from reading bits backward, mixing up a place value, or misjudging a hex digit. This lesson makes every direction fast and reliable.

## Place value in any base

You already know one number system completely: **decimal**, or base 10. It uses ten digits (0 to 9), and each place is worth ten times the place to its right:

$$
4307 = 4 \times 1000 + 3 \times 100 + 0 \times 10 + 7 \times 1
$$

Every number system works the same way. In **base** $$b$$ (also called the **radix**), there are $$b$$ digits, from 0 to $$b - 1$$, and the places from right to left are worth $$b^0 = 1$$, $$b^1$$, $$b^2$$, and so on.

| System | Base | Digits | Place values (right to left) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Binary | 2 | 0, 1 | 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, ... |
| Octal | 8 | 0 to 7 | 1, 8, 64, 512, ... |
| Decimal | 10 | 0 to 9 | 1, 10, 100, 1000, ... |
| Hexadecimal | 16 | 0 to 9, A to F | 1, 16, 256, 4096, ... |

To show which base a number is in, write the base as a subscript, as in $$1011_2$$ or $$2F_{16}$$. Programming languages such as Python use prefixes instead: `0b1011` for binary, `0o17` for octal, and `0x2F` for hexadecimal.

Binary place values are powers of two, and you should know them on sight:

| Power | $$2^0$$ | $$2^1$$ | $$2^2$$ | $$2^3$$ | $$2^4$$ | $$2^5$$ | $$2^6$$ | $$2^7$$ | $$2^8$$ | $$2^9$$ | $$2^{10}$$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Value | 1 | 2 | 4 | 8 | 16 | 32 | 64 | 128 | 256 | 512 | 1,024 |

Two more worth memorizing: $$2^{16} = 65{,}536$$ and $$2^{20} = 1{,}048{,}576$$ (about a million).

## Binary to decimal

To convert binary to decimal, write the place values under the bits and add the place values that sit under a 1.

::::example[Binary to decimal]
Convert $$1011001_2$$ to decimal.

:::step
There are 7 bits, so the place values from left to right are 64, 32, 16, 8, 4, 2, 1.
:::

:::step
Line them up: the bits 1, 0, 1, 1, 0, 0, 1 sit under 64, 32, 16, 8, 4, 2, 1.
:::

:::step
Keep the place values under each 1: 64, 16, 8, and 1.
:::

:::step
Add them: 64 + 16 + 8 + 1 = 89.
:::

:::answer
$$1011001_2 = 89$$.
:::
::::

There is a second method that needs no table, useful for long numbers. **Doubling**: start at 0, and for each bit from left to right, double your running total and add the bit. For $$1011001_2$$: 0, then 1, 2, 5, 11, 22, 44, 89. Same answer.

Three patterns make many questions instant:

- **The last bit decides odd or even.** The rightmost place is worth 1 and every other place is even, so a binary number ending in 1 is odd and one ending in 0 is even.
- **A 1 followed by n zeros is $$2^n$$.** $$1000000_2$$ is $$2^6 = 64$$.
- **n ones in a row is $$2^n - 1$$.** $$1111_2 = 15$$ and $$11111111_2 = 255$$, one less than the next power of two.

:::trap
**Common trap.** Place values grow from the **right**. If you start the 1s place on the left, you read the number backward: $$110101_2$$ becomes 43 instead of 53, and both appear among the choices.
:::

## Decimal to binary: two methods

**Method 1: subtract powers of two.** Find the largest power of two that fits, write a 1 in that place, subtract, and repeat with what is left. Every power you skip gets a 0.

::::example[Decimal to binary by subtraction]
Convert 77 to binary.

:::step
The largest power of two that fits in 77 is 64. Write a 1 in the 64s place; 77 minus 64 leaves 13.
:::

:::step
32 and 16 do not fit in 13, so those places get 0s.
:::

:::step
8 fits: write a 1 and 13 minus 8 leaves 5. Then 4 fits: write a 1 and 5 minus 4 leaves 1.
:::

:::step
2 does not fit in 1, so write a 0. Then 1 fits: write a 1, leaving 0.
:::

:::answer
Reading the places 64, 32, 16, 8, 4, 2, 1 gives $$1001101_2$$. Check: 64 + 8 + 4 + 1 = 77.
:::
::::

**Method 2: repeated division by 2.** Divide by 2, write down the remainder, and keep dividing the quotient until you reach 0. The remainders, read from the **last one to the first**, are the binary digits.

::::example[Decimal to binary by division]
Convert 77 to binary by repeated division.

:::step
77 / 2 = 38 remainder 1. 38 / 2 = 19 remainder 0. 19 / 2 = 9 remainder 1.
:::

:::step
9 / 2 = 4 remainder 1. 4 / 2 = 2 remainder 0. 2 / 2 = 1 remainder 0. 1 / 2 = 0 remainder 1.
:::

:::step
The remainders in the order found are 1, 0, 1, 1, 0, 0, 1. Read them bottom to top: 1, 0, 0, 1, 1, 0, 1.
:::

:::answer
$$77 = 1001101_2$$, matching Method 1.
:::
::::

:::trap
**Common trap.** The first remainder is the 1s place, the **last** digit. Writing the remainders in the order you find them reverses the number, and that reversed number is a favorite distractor.
:::

Counting in binary works like an odometer with only two digits: 0, 1, 10, 11, 100, 101, 110, 111, 1000. Adding 1 to a number ending in a run of 1s turns those 1s into 0s and carries: $$10111_2 + 1 = 11000_2$$.

## Hexadecimal: digits A to F, and the nibble trick

Hexadecimal (hex) is base 16, so it needs sixteen digits. After 0 to 9 come six letters:

| Hex digit | A | B | C | D | E | F |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Decimal value | 10 | 11 | 12 | 13 | 14 | 15 |

**Hex to decimal** uses place values 1, 16, 256, 4,096. For $$2F_{16}$$: $$2 \times 16 + 15 \times 1 = 47$$. For $$1A3_{16}$$: $$1 \times 256 + 10 \times 16 + 3 = 419$$.

**Decimal to hex** works like decimal to binary, with 16 in place of 2: divide by 16 and read the remainders bottom to top, writing remainders 10 to 15 as letters. For 200: 200 / 16 = 12 remainder 8, and 12 / 16 = 0 remainder 12, so reading bottom to top gives C, 8, that is $$C8_{16}$$.

The real power of hex is its link to binary. Since $$16 = 2^4$$, **each hex digit stands for exactly four bits** (four bits are called a **nibble**). That makes conversion between hex and binary a matter of swapping digits for nibbles:

- **Hex to binary:** replace each hex digit with its 4-bit pattern. $$3C_{16}$$: 3 is 0011 and C is 1100, so $$3C_{16} = 00111100_2 = 111100_2$$.
- **Binary to hex:** group the bits in fours **starting from the right**, padding the leftmost group with zeros, then convert each group. $$1011010_2$$ becomes 0101 1010, which is 5 and A: $$5A_{16}$$.

::::example[Binary to hex with the nibble trick]
Convert $$110111110_2$$ to hexadecimal.

:::step
Group from the right in fours: 1 1011 1110. The leftmost group has one bit, so pad it: 0001 1011 1110.
:::

:::step
Convert each nibble: 0001 is 1, 1011 is 11 (B), and 1110 is 14 (E).
:::

:::answer
$$110111110_2 = 1BE_{16}$$, which is 446 in decimal.
:::
::::

This is why programmers write binary in hex: a byte (8 bits) is exactly two hex digits, so $$11111111_2$$ is simply FF, which is 255. The largest value with n hex digits is $$16^n - 1$$: FF is 255 and FFF is 4,095. You will see hex in web colors such as #FF8000, in memory addresses, and in long IDs.

:::trap
**Common trap.** Grouping bits in fours from the **left** breaks the conversion whenever the length is not a multiple of four. $$1011010_2$$ grouped from the left gives 1011 010, which suggests B2, but the right answer is 5A. Always group from the right.
:::

## Octal and the three-bit trick [Competitive]

Octal is base 8, with digits 0 to 7 and place values 1, 8, 64, 512. For $$157_8$$: $$1 \times 64 + 5 \times 8 + 7 = 111$$.

Since $$8 = 2^3$$, **each octal digit stands for exactly three bits**, and the trick mirrors hex: group bits in threes from the right. $$1101011_2$$ becomes 001 101 011, which is 1, 5, 3, so $$1101011_2 = 153_8$$. In the other direction, $$52_8$$ becomes 101 010, so $$52_8 = 101010_2$$.

Octal is less common than hex today, but you still see it in Unix file permissions, where a setting such as 755 packs three 3-bit groups of read, write, and execute flags.

## Invalid digits and comparing across bases [Champion]

A digit must always be smaller than its base. Binary allows only 0 and 1, so 1021 cannot be a binary number. Octal stops at 7, so 1078 is not valid octal. Hex stops at F, so a "G" can never appear.

The same digits mean different values in different bases. The numeral 10 is two in binary, eight in octal, ten in decimal, and sixteen in hex: in every base, 10 means "one of the base, and nothing more".

To compare values written in different bases, convert each to decimal. Which is largest: $$11111111_2$$, $$377_8$$, $$FE_{16}$$, or 256? They are 255, 255, 254, and 256, so 256 wins, and the first two are equal.

The method works for any base, even one you have never practiced. In base 5, the places are 1, 5, 25, 125, so $$243_5 = 2 \times 25 + 4 \times 5 + 3 = 73$$.

:::tip
**Test tip.** For any conversion question, check your answer by converting back. It takes five seconds and catches nearly every reversed or misplaced digit.
:::

:::takeaways
- In base b, digits run from 0 to b - 1 and places are powers of b from the right. Subscripts ($$1011_2$$) or prefixes (0b, 0o, 0x) name the base.
- Binary to decimal: add the place values under each 1, or double-and-add from the left.
- Decimal to binary: subtract the largest powers of two, or divide by 2 and read the remainders from the last to the first.
- Last bit 1 means odd. A 1 followed by n zeros is $$2^n$$; n ones in a row is $$2^n - 1$$.
- Hex digits: A = 10, B = 11, C = 12, D = 13, E = 14, F = 15. Places are 1, 16, 256, 4,096. FF = 255, and n hex digits reach $$16^n - 1$$.
- One hex digit is four bits (a nibble); one octal digit is three bits. Group bits from the right.
- A digit must be less than its base. Compare across bases by converting to decimal, and check answers by converting back.
:::

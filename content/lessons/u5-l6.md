title: Overfitting, underfitting, and regularization

Every model sits somewhere between too simple and too complex, and finding the right spot is the central balancing act of machine learning. Test questions give you a model's training and validation errors and ask what is wrong, ask which change would help, or ask how a regularization technique works. This lesson teaches you to diagnose a model from its errors in seconds and to match each problem with its fix.

## Underfitting and overfitting

**Underfitting** happens when a model is too simple to capture the real pattern in the data. A straight line drawn through clearly curved data misses the curve everywhere, so its errors are high on the training data and on new data alike.

**Overfitting** happens when a model fits the training data too closely, learning its noise and quirks along with the real pattern. It performs superbly on the examples it trained on and poorly on new ones, because it has memorized rather than learned. A student who memorizes the answers to last year's test, without understanding the material, has overfit.

A good model captures the pattern and ignores the noise, which is what lets it **generalize** to new data.

Try it: raise the degree of the curve fitted to the 10 training points. A low degree misses the shape (underfitting); a very high degree threads every training point and swings wildly between them, so its error on the hollow test points shoots up (overfitting).

## Reading training versus validation error

You diagnose a model by comparing its error on the training data with its error on held-out validation data (lesson 5.5):

| Training error | Validation error | Diagnosis |
|---|---|---|
| High | High, and close to the training error | Underfitting |
| Low | Much higher than the training error | Overfitting |
| Low | Low, and close to the training error | A good fit |

The key signal for overfitting is the **gap**: a model that does far better on the data it trained on than on new data is memorizing. The key signal for underfitting is that **both** errors are high, well above what a good model achieves.

As a model is made more complex, its training error keeps falling, because a more flexible model can always fit its training data more closely. Its validation error falls at first, while the model is learning real patterns, and then rises, once it starts fitting noise. Plotting both errors against complexity gives the **model complexity curve**:

The same shape appears over training time in neural networks: as epochs go by, training loss keeps falling while validation loss eventually starts to climb. That turning point is where **early stopping** halts training.

## The bias-variance tradeoff [Competitive]

Prediction error comes from two sources a modeler can control, plus noise that no model can remove:

- **Bias** is error from assumptions that are too simple. A high-bias model misses the real pattern in a consistent way, whatever data it is trained on. High bias causes underfitting.
- **Variance** is error from being too sensitive to the particular training data. A high-variance model changes a lot when it is trained on a different sample, because it has fit that sample's noise. High variance causes overfitting.

Simple models (a straight line, a shallow tree) tend to have **high bias and low variance**. Complex models (a very deep tree, a huge network) tend to have **low bias and high variance**. Making a model more complex trades one for the other, which is why the problem is called the **bias-variance tradeoff**. The goal is the complexity at which their combined error is smallest, the bottom of the validation curve.

This mirrors the darts picture from lesson 3.4: bias is how far the throws land from the bullseye on average, and variance is how widely they scatter.

::check{id="fit-tradeoff-03"}

## Fixes for each problem

Each problem has its own fixes, and the fix for one usually makes the other worse.

**To fix underfitting (high bias):**

- use a more flexible model (higher degree, deeper tree, larger network);
- add better or more informative features;
- reduce the amount of regularization;
- train longer, if training stopped too early.

**To fix overfitting (high variance):**

- collect more training data;
- simplify the model (fewer features, a shallower or pruned tree, a smaller network);
- add regularization, dropout, or early stopping (next section);
- use ensembles such as random forests, which average many models to reduce variance (lesson 5.8);
- for images, use **data augmentation**: train on flipped, rotated, or cropped copies of the images so the model sees more variety.

:::trap
**Common trap.** More data is a strong cure for overfitting but does little for underfitting: a straight line stays a straight line no matter how many points it sees. If both errors are high, make the model more capable; if only validation error is high, give it more data or less freedom.
:::

## Regularization: L1, L2, dropout, early stopping [Competitive]

**Regularization** is any technique that discourages a model from becoming too complex, so it overfits less. The most common form adds a **penalty** for large weights to the loss the model minimizes:

$$
\text{total loss} = \text{error} + \lambda \times \text{penalty}
$$

The hyperparameter $$\lambda$$ (lambda) sets the regularization strength. With $$\lambda = 0$$ there is no regularization. Raising $$\lambda$$ pushes the weights toward zero, making the model simpler, with more bias and less variance; raising it too far makes the model underfit.

Two penalties are standard:

- **L2 regularization** (used in **ridge regression**) penalizes the sum of the **squared** weights. It shrinks all weights smoothly toward zero but rarely makes any exactly zero.
- **L1 regularization** (used in **lasso regression**) penalizes the sum of the **absolute values** of the weights. It can shrink some weights to exactly zero, which removes those features from the model, a built-in form of **feature selection**.

::::example[Computing the penalties]
A model has weights 3, -1, and 2, and $$\lambda = 0.1$$. Compute the L1 and L2 penalty terms.

:::step
L1 adds the absolute values: 3 + 1 + 2 = 6. Times λ: 0.1 × 6 = 0.6.
:::

:::step
L2 adds the squares: 9 + 1 + 4 = 14. Times λ: 0.1 × 14 = 1.4.
:::

:::answer
The L1 penalty term is 0.6 and the L2 penalty term is 1.4. Both are added to the model's error during training, so large weights cost more.
:::
::::

Neural networks add two more tools:

- **Dropout**, introduced by Geoffrey Hinton and colleagues in 2012, randomly switches off a fraction of the neurons (for example, half of them) in each training step. The network cannot rely on any single neuron, so it learns patterns spread across many neurons, which hold up better on new data. Dropout is used only during training; at prediction time, every neuron is active.
- **Early stopping** ends training when the validation error stops improving, before the model has time to memorize the training data.

::check{id="regularization-01"}

:::takeaways
- Underfitting: too simple; high error on both training and validation data. Overfitting: too complex; low training error but much higher validation error.
- Diagnose by comparing errors: both high means underfitting; a large gap means overfitting; both low and close means a good fit.
- As complexity grows, training error keeps falling while validation error falls and then rises. The best model is at the bottom of the validation curve.
- Bias is error from oversimplified assumptions (underfitting); variance is error from sensitivity to the training sample (overfitting). Simple models: high bias, low variance. Complex models: the reverse.
- Fix underfitting with a more flexible model, better features, or less regularization. Fix overfitting with more data, a simpler model, regularization, dropout, early stopping, ensembles, or data augmentation.
- Regularization adds λ × penalty to the loss. L2 (ridge) uses squared weights and shrinks them smoothly; L1 (lasso) uses absolute weights and can set some to zero, selecting features. Larger λ means a simpler model.
- Dropout randomly turns off neurons during training only; early stopping halts training when validation error stops improving.
:::

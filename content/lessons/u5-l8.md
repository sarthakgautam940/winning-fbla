One objective asks how machine learning algorithms behave, and this lesson opens up the classic ones: how a decision tree picks its questions, why a forest of trees beats a single tree, how k-nearest neighbors, support vector machines, and naive Bayes decide, and how the main clustering methods group data. Expect calculation questions on Gini impurity and information gain, quick classification with k-NN, and many "which algorithm behaves this way?" items. Lesson 3.7 showed how to use trees and k-means; this lesson shows how they learn.

## How a decision tree chooses a split

A decision tree is built from the top down. At the root, the algorithm considers many possible questions, each a feature and a threshold, such as "income below $40,000?" or "has a cosigner?" It scores each one by how well it separates the classes, and it keeps the question that makes the two resulting groups, the **child nodes**, as **pure** as possible, meaning each child is as close as possible to containing a single class. Then it repeats the same search inside each child, and so on down the tree.

Growth stops when a node is pure, when a maximum depth is reached, or when a node has too few examples to split further. The search is **greedy**: at each node it takes the best split available right now, without looking ahead to see whether a different choice would lead to a better tree later.

Regression trees work the same way, but they choose splits that make the target values within each child as similar as possible, and each leaf predicts the average of its training examples.

## Gini impurity and entropy [Competitive]

Two measures score how mixed a node is. For a node where the classes appear in proportions $$p_1, p_2, \ldots$$:

$$
\text{Gini} = 1 - \sum_k p_k^2, \qquad \text{Entropy} = -\sum_k p_k \log_2 p_k
$$

Both are 0 for a pure node. With two classes, both reach their maximum at a 50/50 mix: Gini is 0.5 and entropy is 1 bit.

::::example[Impurity of one node]
A node holds 10 examples: 8 "yes" and 2 "no." Find its Gini impurity and its entropy.

:::step
The proportions are 8/10 = 0.8 and 2/10 = 0.2.
:::

:::step
Gini = 1 - 0.8² - 0.2² = 1 - 0.64 - 0.04 = 0.32.
:::

:::step
Entropy = -(0.8 × log₂ 0.8 + 0.2 × log₂ 0.2) = -(0.8 × -0.322 + 0.2 × -2.322) = 0.258 + 0.464, about 0.72.
:::

:::answer
Gini impurity is 0.32 and entropy is about 0.72 bits. Both are well below their 50/50 maximums, so the node is fairly pure.
:::
::::

The two measures nearly always agree on the best split. Gini is a little faster to compute, which is one reason it is a common default.

::check{id="decision-trees-02"}

## Information gain [Champion]

A split is scored by how much it **reduces** impurity. Compute the impurity of each child, take the average weighted by the share of examples in each child, and subtract it from the parent's impurity. With entropy, this reduction is called **information gain**:

$$
\text{gain} = \text{impurity(parent)} - \sum_{\text{children}} \frac{n_{\text{child}}}{n_{\text{parent}}} \times \text{impurity(child)}
$$

Take a parent with 10 "yes" and 10 "no" (Gini 0.5, entropy 1). A split sends 8 yes and 2 no to the left child and 2 yes and 8 no to the right child. Each child has Gini 0.32 and entropy about 0.72, and each holds half the examples, so the weighted Gini is 0.32 and the weighted entropy is about 0.72. The Gini decrease is 0.5 - 0.32 = 0.18, and the information gain is about 1 - 0.72 = 0.28. The tree compares this gain with every other candidate split and chooses the largest.

Try it: change the class counts in each child. A split that leaves both children at 50/50 gains nothing; a split that produces pure children gains the most.

## Pruning and depth

A tree allowed to grow without limits keeps splitting until every leaf is pure, which usually means memorizing the training data: perfect training accuracy, poor validation accuracy (lesson 5.6). A tree that is too shallow underfits. Depth is controlled in two ways:

- **Pre-pruning** stops growth early, with hyperparameters such as the maximum depth, the minimum number of examples in a leaf, and the minimum impurity decrease a split must achieve.
- **Post-pruning** grows a full tree, then cuts away branches that do not improve performance on validation data.

Shallow trees are also easier to read, which preserves the interpretability that makes trees attractive in the first place (lesson 5.4).

## Bagging, random forests, and boosting [Competitive]

An **ensemble** combines many models into one, and it usually beats any single member. Two strategies dominate:

**Bagging** (bootstrap aggregating) trains many models independently, each on a **bootstrap sample**: a random sample of the training data drawn **with replacement**, the same size as the original, so some examples repeat and others are left out. The models then vote (for classification) or average (for regression). If 5 of 7 trees say "spam" and 2 say "not spam," the ensemble says "spam." Averaging many models that each overfit in different ways cancels much of their noise, so bagging mainly **reduces variance**.

A **random forest** is bagging applied to decision trees, with a second source of randomness: at each split, every tree considers only a **random subset of the features**. That keeps the trees from all making the same choices, so their errors are less correlated and the average is more accurate.

**Boosting** trains models **one after another**, and each new model concentrates on the mistakes of the models before it, either by giving misclassified examples more weight (as in AdaBoost) or by fitting the remaining errors (as in **gradient boosting**, including popular libraries such as XGBoost and LightGBM). The final prediction combines all of them. Boosting mainly **reduces bias**, and gradient-boosted trees are often the most accurate models for tables of data, though too many rounds can overfit.

| | Bagging and random forests | Boosting |
|---|---|---|
| How models are trained | Independently, in parallel | One after another |
| Each model learns from | A bootstrap sample | The errors of earlier models |
| Mainly reduces | Variance | Bias |

## k-nearest neighbors

**k-nearest neighbors** (k-NN) classifies a new example by finding the k training examples closest to it, usually by straight-line distance, and taking a majority vote of their labels. For regression, it averages their values instead.

::::example[Classify with k-NN]
A new point sits at (2, 3). The training points are (1, 3) in class A, (3, 4) in class A, (4, 3) in class B, (2, 6) in class B, and (6, 6) in class B. Classify the new point with k = 3.

:::step
Distances: to (1, 3) is 1; to (3, 4) is $$\sqrt{1 + 1} \approx 1.41$$; to (4, 3) is 2; to (2, 6) is 3; to (6, 6) is 5.
:::

:::step
The 3 nearest are the points at distances 1 (A), 1.41 (A), and 2 (B).
:::

:::answer
Two of the three neighbors are class A, so the new point is classified as A. With k = 5, the vote would be 2 A to 3 B, and the answer would flip to B.
:::
::::

The choice of k matters. A very small k, such as 1, follows every quirk of the training data and is sensitive to noise (high variance). A very large k smooths over real local patterns (high bias). An odd k avoids ties between two classes.

Because k-NN works with distances, features must be **scaled**: a feature measured in thousands, such as income, would swamp one measured in single digits. And because it is a lazy learner (lesson 5.2), every prediction searches the training data, which is slow for very large datasets.

::check{id="knn-svm-nb-01"}

## Support vector machines and naive Bayes [Competitive]

A **support vector machine** (SVM) separates two classes with the boundary that leaves the **widest margin**, the largest gap between the boundary and the nearest training points of each class. Those nearest points are the **support vectors**: they alone determine where the boundary goes, and moving any other point would not change it. When the classes overlap, a **soft margin** tolerates some mistakes, with a hyperparameter controlling the trade-off.

When no straight boundary can separate the classes, the **kernel trick** lets an SVM work as if the data had been mapped into a higher-dimensional space, where a straight boundary does separate them, without ever computing that mapping directly. In the original space, the result is a curved boundary.

**Naive Bayes** classifies with Bayes' theorem (lesson 2.7): it computes the probability of each class given the features and picks the most probable. It is "naive" because it assumes the features are **conditionally independent** given the class, so it can simply multiply each feature's likelihood. For a spam filter, it assumes the words in an email appear independently once you know whether the email is spam. The assumption is rarely true, yet naive Bayes is fast, needs little data, and works well on text.

## Clustering and dimensionality reduction algorithms

Unsupervised learning (lesson 5.2) has its own family of algorithms:

- **k-means** (lesson 3.7) repeats two steps: assign each point to its nearest centroid, then move each centroid to the mean of its points.
- **Hierarchical clustering**, in its common bottom-up form, starts with every point as its own cluster and repeatedly merges the two closest clusters. The history of merges is drawn as a **dendrogram**, a tree diagram, and cutting the dendrogram at a chosen level gives the clusters, so the number of clusters need not be chosen in advance.
- **DBSCAN** (density-based clustering) defines clusters as dense regions of points. It finds clusters of any shape, and it labels points in sparse regions as **noise**, which makes it useful for spotting outliers. It needs no k, but it does need a distance threshold and a minimum number of neighbors.
- **PCA** (lesson 3.6) reduces dimensions with components ordered by the variance they explain. For pictures of high-dimensional data, **t-SNE** and **UMAP** produce two-dimensional maps that keep similar points close together.

:::tip
**Test tip.** "Clusters of any shape" or "labels outliers as noise" points to DBSCAN. "Dendrogram" or "no need to choose k" points to hierarchical clustering. "Choose k, assign, update centroids" is k-means.
:::

:::takeaways
- A decision tree greedily picks, at each node, the split that makes the child nodes purest, then repeats in each child.
- Gini = 1 - sum of squared proportions; entropy = -sum of p log₂ p. Both are 0 when pure; for two classes, maximums are 0.5 (Gini) and 1 (entropy) at 50/50.
- Information gain = parent impurity - weighted average child impurity. Choose the split with the largest gain.
- Unlimited depth overfits. Pre-pruning (max depth, minimum leaf size) and post-pruning keep trees general.
- Bagging trains models in parallel on bootstrap samples and votes, reducing variance. Random forests add a random feature subset at each split. Boosting trains models in sequence on earlier errors, reducing bias.
- k-NN votes among the k nearest training points. Small k overfits, large k underfits; scale features first.
- SVMs maximize the margin; support vectors define the boundary; the kernel trick gives curved boundaries. Naive Bayes applies Bayes' theorem assuming features are independent given the class.
- Hierarchical clustering builds a dendrogram; DBSCAN finds dense clusters of any shape and labels noise; k-means needs k; PCA, t-SNE, and UMAP reduce dimensions.
:::

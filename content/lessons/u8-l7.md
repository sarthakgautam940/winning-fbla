The test does not expect you to be a lawyer, but it does expect you to match a privacy law to a scenario, place an AI system in the right EU risk tier, name the NIST framework's four functions, and tell transparency from explainability. Laws and their timelines change, so this lesson states each one's status as of late 2026; check the Reference page for dates before your competition.

## Privacy laws: GDPR, CCPA and CPRA, HIPAA, COPPA, FERPA

**The GDPR.** The European Union's General Data Protection Regulation was adopted in April 2016 and has applied since 25 May 2018. It sets seven principles for handling personal data: lawfulness, fairness, and transparency; purpose limitation; data minimisation; accuracy; storage limitation; integrity and confidentiality; and accountability. It gives people rights over their data, including:

- the **right of access** (see the data an organization holds about you),
- the **right to rectification** (correct it),
- the **right to erasure**, often called the right to be forgotten (have it deleted),
- the **right to data portability** (take it to another service),
- the **right to object** to some processing, and
- under **Article 22**, the right not to be subject to a decision based *solely* on automated processing, including profiling, that has legal or similarly significant effects. Where such decisions are allowed because of a contract or the person's explicit consent, the person must at least be able to obtain human intervention, give their view, and contest the decision.

Organizations must report a personal data breach to the regulator without undue delay and, where feasible, within 72 hours of becoming aware of it. Fines reach up to 20 million euros or 4 percent of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher, for breaking the core principles.

**U.S. laws** are narrower, each covering one sector or group:

| Law | What it covers | Key rule |
|---|---|---|
| CCPA, as amended by the CPRA (California) | Businesses handling California consumers' personal information | Rights to know, delete, correct, and opt out of the sale or sharing of data; the CPRA (approved by voters in 2020) amended the CCPA and created the California Privacy Protection Agency, now publicly called CalPrivacy |
| HIPAA (1996) | Health plans, health care clearinghouses, health care providers that conduct certain transactions electronically, and their business associates | Rules for using and disclosing protected health information |
| COPPA (1998) | Websites and online services directed to children under 13, or that knowingly collect their data | Verifiable parental consent before collecting children's personal information |
| FERPA (1974) | Education records at schools that receive U.S. Department of Education funds | Parents can inspect records, request corrections, and must consent to most disclosures; the rights pass to the student at 18 or on entering college |
| GLBA (Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act) | Financial institutions, such as lenders, investment advisers, and insurers | Explain information-sharing practices to customers, let them opt out of some sharing, and safeguard their data |

:::trap
**Common traps.** COPPA covers children under 13, not under 16 or 18. FERPA is about education records; COPPA is about online collection from young children. HIPAA is spelled with one P and two As, and the P stands for portability. The CPRA amended the CCPA; it did not replace it. And GDPR dates come in pairs: adopted in 2016, applying since 2018.
:::

## The EU AI Act's risk tiers [Competitive]

The **EU AI Act**, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, entered into force on 1 August 2024, and its rules apply in stages. It sorts AI systems into four levels of risk:

```figure
type: pyramid
caption: The EU AI Act's four risk levels. The higher the risk, the stricter the rules; most AI systems sit at the bottom.
levels: ["Unacceptable risk: banned", "High risk: strict obligations", "Transparency risk: disclosure duties", "Minimal risk: no new rules"]
```

- **Unacceptable risk** is banned. The Act lists nine prohibited practices, including social scoring, untargeted scraping of faces to build recognition databases, emotion recognition in workplaces and schools, real-time remote biometric identification by police in public spaces (apart from narrow exceptions), and, added in 2026, systems that generate non-consensual sexually explicit content.
- **High risk** systems face strict obligations. Examples include software that sorts CVs for recruitment and credit scoring that can deny someone a loan.
- **Transparency risk**, often called limited risk, brings disclosure duties: a chatbot must tell people they are talking to a machine, and deepfakes must be labeled.
- **Minimal or no risk** systems, such as spam filters and AI in video games, face no new rules.

General-purpose AI models, such as large language models, follow a separate track rather than a fifth tier: their providers have transparency and copyright duties, and providers of models with systemic risk must also assess and reduce those risks.

The timeline is a classic trap, because entering into force is not the same as applying. The bans have applied since 2 February 2025 (the ninth from 2 December 2026), duties for general-purpose AI models since 2 August 2025, and most of the Act, including the transparency rules, from 2 August 2026. A 2026 amendment, the Digital Omnibus, pushed the high-risk requirements back to 2 December 2027 for uses such as hiring, education, and credit scoring, and to 2 August 2028 for AI built into regulated products. The top fines, for prohibited practices, reach 35 million euros or 7 percent of worldwide annual turnover, higher than the GDPR's 4 percent.

::::example[Placing systems in the EU AI Act's tiers]
Classify each system: (1) a city system that scores residents' trustworthiness from their social behavior and limits services for low scorers; (2) software that ranks job applicants' CVs; (3) a customer-service chatbot; (4) an email spam filter.

:::step
System 1 is social scoring, a prohibited practice, so it is unacceptable risk.
:::

:::step
System 2 sorts CVs for recruitment, a listed high-risk use.
:::

:::step
System 3 talks with people, so it carries a transparency duty: it must disclose that it is a machine.
:::

:::step
System 4 poses minimal risk and faces no new rules.
:::

:::answer
Unacceptable, high, transparency, and minimal risk.
:::
::::

## The NIST AI Risk Management Framework [Competitive]

In the United States, NIST, the federal standards agency, published the **NIST AI Risk Management Framework** (AI RMF 1.0, publication NIST AI 100-1) on 26 January 2023. It is voluntary guidance, not a law and not a certification. (Contrast ISO/IEC 42001:2023, an international standard for an AI management system that organizations can be independently audited and certified against.) As of late 2026, AI RMF 1.0 is still the current version, and NIST says it is being revised.

The framework's core has four functions:

| Function | What it does |
|---|---|
| GOVERN | Cultivates and implements a culture of risk management; it applies across all the other functions |
| MAP | Establishes the context to frame the risks of a particular AI system |
| MEASURE | Uses quantitative, qualitative, or mixed methods to analyze, assess, benchmark, and monitor AI risk |
| MANAGE | Allocates risk resources to the risks that have been mapped and measured |

NIST also lists seven characteristics of trustworthy AI, three of which come in tested pairs: valid and reliable; safe; secure and resilient; accountable and transparent; explainable and interpretable; privacy-enhanced; and fair with harmful bias managed. Its Generative AI Profile (NIST AI 600-1, 2024) adds 12 risks specific to generative AI, from confabulation to environmental impacts, which you have met throughout this unit.

:::tip
**Test tip.** Remember the order the work usually flows, with GOVERN over all of it: map the context, measure the risks, manage them. A question that describes building an organization's policies and culture is GOVERN.
:::

## Transparency and explainability: LIME and SHAP [Competitive]

These two words are tested as a pair, so learn NIST's distinction:

- **Transparency** reflects the extent to which information about an AI system and its outputs is available to the people interacting with it: that AI is being used at all, what data it was trained on, what it is for, and its known limits.
- **Explainability** refers to a representation of the mechanisms underlying an AI system's operation: how and why it produced a particular output. NIST adds **interpretability**, the meaning of a system's output in the context of its intended purpose.

Transparency answers "what is this system, and what does it do?" Explainability answers "why did it decide this?" A company can be transparent about a model yet unable to explain an individual decision.

Two documentation tools serve transparency. A **model card** (Mitchell and colleagues, 2019) is a short document that accompanies a trained model and reports its intended use and its performance across different groups and conditions. A **datasheet for a dataset** (Gebru and colleagues) describes a dataset's motivation, composition, collection process, preprocessing, recommended uses, distribution, and maintenance.

Two techniques serve explainability for complex models:

- **LIME** (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations; Ribeiro, Singh, and Guestrin, 2016) explains one prediction of any black-box classifier by fitting a simple, interpretable model that approximates the black box near that prediction. It is local (one prediction at a time) and model-agnostic (it works with any model).
- **SHAP** (SHapley Additive exPlanations; Lundberg and Lee, 2017) assigns each feature an importance value for a particular prediction using Shapley values from cooperative game theory. The contributions are additive: a base value plus the feature contributions equals the prediction.

::::example[Reading a SHAP explanation]
A model predicts the chance that a loan applicant will miss a payment. For one applicant, SHAP reports a base value of 0.40 (the model's average prediction). Savings lower the prediction by 0.10, credit card debt raises it by 0.05, and two recent missed payments raise it by 0.25. What is the model's prediction for this applicant, and which feature mattered most?

:::step
Add the increases: 0.05 + 0.25 = 0.30.
:::

:::step
Subtract the decrease: 0.30 minus 0.10 = a net change of 0.20.
:::

:::step
Add the net change to the base value: 0.40 + 0.20 = 0.60.
:::

:::answer
The prediction is 0.60, and recent missed payments, which raised it by 0.25, contributed the most.
:::
::::

## Accountability and human oversight

**Accountability** means that identifiable people and organizations answer for an AI system's decisions and harms (lesson 8.1). NIST puts it bluntly: accountability presupposes transparency, because no one can be held responsible for a system nobody can see into. Laws build accountability in several ways:

- **Human oversight.** GDPR Article 22 limits decisions made *solely* by automated processing, and the EU AI Act requires that people overseeing high-risk systems be able to recognize automation bias and to override or reverse the system's output (lesson 8.6). A decision with meaningful human review, by someone who weighs other evidence and can decide differently, is not "solely automated."
- **Audits for disparate impact.** Fairness can be checked against rules of thumb such as the four-fifths rule (lesson 8.3), and some laws now require audits. New York City's Local Law 144, enforced since 5 July 2023, bars employers from using an automated employment decision tool unless it has had a bias audit within the past year, information about the audit is public, and candidates have been notified in advance.
- **Documentation and responsibility.** Model cards, datasheets, and decision logs let others check a system's work. And organizations answer for what their AI tells people: in Moffatt v. Air Canada (lesson 6.6), a tribunal held the airline responsible for its website chatbot's wrong advice.

## Professional codes: ACM and IEEE

**Ethics guidelines** are documents setting out principles that people and organizations developing AI agree to follow. Unlike laws, they usually have no legal force, but they guide professional practice and organizations' own policies. A leading example is the EU's Ethics Guidelines for Trustworthy AI, presented on 8 April 2019 by the High-Level Expert Group on AI. They say trustworthy AI should be lawful, ethical, and sound from both a technical and a social point of view, and they list seven key requirements: human agency and oversight; technical reliability and safety; privacy and data governance; transparency; diversity, non-discrimination and fairness; societal and environmental well-being; and accountability.

Professional codes apply such principles to individual practitioners:

| Code | From | Core content |
|---|---|---|
| ACM Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct | Association for Computing Machinery, adopted 22 June 2018 (replacing the 1992 code) | Seven General Ethical Principles: contribute to society and human well-being; avoid harm; be honest and trustworthy; be fair and take action not to discriminate; respect the work required to produce new ideas and creative works; respect privacy; honor confidentiality |
| Ethically Aligned Design, First Edition | IEEE, April 2019 | Eight General Principles: human rights, well-being, data agency, effectiveness, transparency, accountability, awareness of misuse, and competence |
| IEEE 7000-2021 | IEEE, September 2021 | A standard process for identifying stakeholder values and addressing ethical concerns during system design |

::check{id="responsible-ai-05"}

:::takeaways
- GDPR (applies since 2018): seven principles including data minimisation and accountability; rights of access, rectification, erasure, portability, and objection; Article 22 limits solely automated decisions; breaches reported within 72 hours where feasible; fines up to 20 million euros or 4 percent of turnover.
- U.S. laws by sector: CCPA as amended by the CPRA (California consumers), HIPAA (health information at covered entities), COPPA (children under 13 online), FERPA (education records), GLBA (financial institutions).
- EU AI Act (in force August 2024, applying in stages): unacceptable risk is banned (such as social scoring), high risk has strict duties (such as CV sorting and credit scoring), transparency risk needs disclosure (chatbots, deepfakes), minimal risk has none. Top fines: 35 million euros or 7 percent.
- NIST AI RMF 1.0 (2023) is voluntary; its functions are GOVERN, MAP, MEASURE, and MANAGE. ISO/IEC 42001 is certifiable.
- Transparency: information about a system is available. Explainability: how and why it produced an output. Model cards and datasheets document models and data; LIME fits a local simple model around one prediction; SHAP's Shapley values add up from a base value to the prediction.
- Accountability presupposes transparency. Oversight must be meaningful, and audits such as those required by NYC Local Law 144 check for disparate impact.
- Ethics guidelines are voluntary principles, such as the EU's 2019 Trustworthy AI guidelines (lawful, ethical, and technically and socially sound; seven requirements). The ACM Code (2018) has seven General Ethical Principles; IEEE's Ethically Aligned Design (2019) has eight.
:::

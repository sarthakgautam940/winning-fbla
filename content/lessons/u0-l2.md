title: Pacing, elimination, and smart guessing
---

Knowing the material gets you most of the way. The last stretch comes from how you spend 50 minutes: never stuck, never rushed at the end, never leaving free points on the table. This lesson gives you a pacing plan, a method for eliminating wrong answers, and rules for guessing well.

## The 30-second average

Fifty minutes for 100 questions is 30 seconds each, but questions are not equal. A definition you know cold takes 8 seconds. A standard-deviation calculation might take 60. The goal is not 30 seconds per question; it is **finishing all 100 with a few minutes to spare**.

Think of time as a budget:

- **Fast questions** (recognize a term, pick a chart, classify a variable): 10 to 20 seconds. Most of the test.
- **Working questions** (a short calculation, reading a SQL query or a code snippet): 30 to 60 seconds.
- **Hard questions** (multi-step reasoning, subtle distinctions): up to 90 seconds, but only after everything else is safe.

Fast questions pay for slow ones. If you answer the easy majority quickly and accurately, you bank the minutes the hard ones need.

## The two-pass method

The best way to protect your time is to never let one question hold you hostage.

**First pass.** Go through all 100 in order.

1. If you know it, answer and move on.
2. If you can work it out in under about 45 seconds, do it.
3. If not, **pick your best guess anyway**, write the question number on your scratch paper, and move on.

**Second pass.** Return to the numbers on your list with the time you saved. Work each one properly and change your answer only if you find a concrete reason.

Because you always enter a guess on the first pass, every question has an answer even if time runs out. The second pass can only improve your score.

:::key
**Key idea.** Never skip a question with no answer. Guess, write down the number, and move on. A blank is worth zero; a guess among four choices is worth a quarter point on average, and much more once you have eliminated one or two choices.
:::

## Checkpoints

Glance at the timer at three points:

| After question | Time left should be about |
|---|---|
| 25 | 37 minutes |
| 50 | 25 minutes |
| 75 | 13 minutes |

If you are behind, speed up on the fast questions and send more to your second-pass list. If you are ahead, you have earned a careful second pass. Either way, aim to finish the first pass with **at least 5 minutes left**.

:::tip
**Test tip.** In the last 2 minutes, stop working problems. Make sure every question has an answer, then submit. Remember the third tiebreaker: a faster finish wins a tie, so do not sit on a finished test.
:::

## Elimination: how wrong answers are built

Test writers do not invent wrong answers at random. Each one is built from a specific mistake, and once you know the patterns you can cross them out quickly. These are the most common:

- **The neighbor concept.** A correct description of a different idea. A question about precision offers the definition of recall; a question about reinforcement learning offers a correct description of unsupervised learning.
- **The reversed relationship.** "Increasing the sample size increases the standard error." Right variables, wrong direction.
- **The partial truth.** A statement that is true in part but adds a false condition, such as "always" or "only".
- **The calculation slip.** For a computed answer, the wrong options are the results of common errors: dividing by n instead of n minus 1, forgetting to square or to take the square root, reading bits in the wrong order.
- **True but not responsive.** A true statement that does not answer the question that was asked.

When you are unsure, look for the answer that is **precisely** right rather than roughly right. Two options often differ by one word; that word is usually the point of the question.

## Reading the stem carefully

Most avoidable errors come from misreading the question, not from not knowing the content.

- **NOT and EXCEPT** reverse the question. When you see them, the right answer is the one false statement among three true ones. Restate the question to yourself: "Three of these are true; which one is false?"
- **BEST and MOST LIKELY** mean more than one option may be partly right. Choose the one an expert would defend first.
- **Units and rounding** in the stem ("in thousands", "to the nearest tenth") are there for a reason. Check them before you choose a number.
- **Sample or population?** For any spread question, the stem's wording decides whether you divide by n or by n minus 1.

:::trap
**Common trap.** On a NOT question, students find a true statement, feel the familiar pull of a correct fact, and pick it. Mark the word NOT on your mental checklist before you read the options.
:::

## Smart guessing

When you must guess, guess with a reason:

1. **Eliminate first.** Each choice you remove raises your odds from 25 percent to 33 percent to 50 percent.
2. **Prefer the precise and the specific.** Vague, sweeping answers ("AI always outperforms humans") are rarely right.
3. **Distrust absolutes.** Options with "always", "never", "only" or "guarantees" are usually wrong, unless the fact really is absolute (a standard deviation is never negative).
4. **Do not hunt for letter patterns.** Well-made tests balance their answer letters. Picking "C" because the last three answers were not C is superstition.
5. **Do not change answers on a feeling.** Change only when you can name the mistake in your first choice.

:::takeaways
- Budget time: fast questions pay for slow ones. Finish the first pass with at least 5 minutes left.
- Two passes: answer or guess every question on the first pass, write down the ones to revisit, and return with saved time.
- Checkpoints: about 37 minutes left after question 25, 25 after question 50, 13 after question 75.
- Wrong answers come from patterns: neighbor concepts, reversed relationships, partial truths, calculation slips, and true-but-irrelevant statements.
- Read NOT, EXCEPT, BEST, units, and "sample or population" before reading the choices.
- Never leave a blank. Eliminate, prefer precise answers, distrust absolutes, ignore letter patterns, and submit once your review is done.
:::

AI is not one technology but a family of them. Test questions describe an application and ask which subfield it belongs to, or name a task and ask what it does: drawing boxes around pedestrians, labeling reviews as positive or negative, turning speech into text, recommending a movie. This lesson maps the major subfields, the tasks inside each, and the vocabulary that tells them apart.

## Computer vision and its tasks

**Computer vision** is the subfield of AI that enables computers to interpret and understand images and video. To a computer, an image is a grid of numbers. In a grayscale image, each **pixel** holds one number, typically from 0 (black) to 255 (white); a color image stores three numbers per pixel, one each for red, green, and blue (lesson 1.5). A 1,920 × 1,080 color photo is therefore about 6.2 million numbers, and a vision model learns to find patterns in them.

The main vision tasks, from simplest to most detailed:

| Task | Question it answers | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Image classification** | What is in this image? One label for the whole image | "Cat" or "no cat"; "pneumonia" or "normal" on an X-ray |
| **Object detection** | What objects are here, and where? A labeled box around each one | Boxes around every pedestrian and car for a self-driving car |
| **Image segmentation** | Which pixels belong to what? A label for every pixel | Mapping roads, buildings, and water in satellite images |
| **Facial recognition** | Whose face is this? | Unlocking a phone; tagging people in photos |
| **Optical character recognition** (OCR) | What text appears in the image? | Turning scanned pages into searchable text |

Segmentation comes in two kinds. **Semantic segmentation** gives every pixel a class, so all the cars in a street scene share one label. **Instance segmentation** also separates individual objects, so each car gets its own outline. Other tasks include **pose estimation**, which locates the joints of a body, and image generation, which creates new images (lesson 6.3).

The standard models are the convolutional neural networks of lesson 5.10. Computer vision shows up in medical imaging, self-driving cars, factory inspection, crop monitoring, security cameras, and checkout systems that recognize products.

:::tip
**Test tip.** Classification says *what*; detection says *what and where*, with boxes; segmentation says *what* for *every pixel*.
:::

## Natural language processing and its tasks

**Natural language processing** (NLP) is the subfield of AI that enables computers to understand, interpret, and generate human language, written or spoken. It works in two directions. **Natural language understanding** (NLU) extracts meaning and intent from language, such as working out that "it arrived broken, I want to send it back" is a return request. **Natural language generation** (NLG) produces language: the reply, a summary, or a report.

Common NLP tasks:

- **Sentiment analysis**: classify the opinion or emotional tone of text, such as positive, negative, or neutral reviews.
- **Machine translation**: convert text from one language to another.
- **Named entity recognition** (NER): find and label the names of people, organizations, places, dates, and amounts.
- **Text classification**: sort documents into categories, as a spam filter does.
- **Summarization**: condense a long text into its main points.
- **Question answering**: find or generate the answer to a question.
- **Conversational agents**: chatbots and assistants that hold a dialogue.

Approaches range from simple to sophisticated. ELIZA (lesson 6.1) matched keywords and filled in templates; later systems counted words statistically; today's systems use deep networks, especially transformers, that learn language from enormous amounts of text (lesson 6.4).

## How machines process language: ambiguity, parsing, and text features [Competitive]

Human language is full of **ambiguity**, which people resolve without noticing and computers find hard. Three kinds come up on tests:

- **Lexical ambiguity**: a single word has more than one meaning. When the meanings are related, as with "head" (a body part, or the leader of a club), it is called **polysemy**.
- **Reference ambiguity**: it is unclear which noun a pronoun points to. In "Maria told Ana that she won," who won?
- **Attachment (syntactic) ambiguity**: it is unclear how the parts of a sentence connect. In "I saw the man with the telescope," the telescope could belong to the seeing or to the man.

People resolve ambiguity with context and **commonsense knowledge** about how the world works. **Winograd sentences** test exactly this. Compare "The trophy did not fit in the suitcase because it was too big" with "...because it was too small." Changing one word flips what "it" refers to, first the trophy and then the suitcase, and only knowledge of the world (big things do not fit in small spaces) tells you which.

To analyze grammar, NLP systems use **part-of-speech (POS) tagging**, which labels each word as a noun, verb, adjective, and so on, and **parsing**, which builds a **parse tree** grouping words into phrases: a **noun phrase** (NP) such as "the old dog," a **verb phrase** (VP) such as "chased the ball," and a **prepositional phrase** (PP) such as "with the telescope." An attachment ambiguity is a sentence with two valid parse trees.

```figure

caption: A parse tree for "The dog chased the ball." The sentence (S) splits into a noun phrase (NP) and a verb phrase (VP), and each phrase splits into words tagged with their parts of speech (Det marks a determiner such as "the").
root:
  label: S
  children:
    - label: NP
      children:
        - { label: "Det: the" }
        - { label: "Noun: dog" }
    - label: VP
      children:
        - { label: "Verb: chased" }
        - label: NP
          children:
            - { label: "Det: the" }
            - { label: "Noun: ball" }
```

Before a classic model can learn from text, the text must become numbers. The common steps and features:

- **Tokenization**: split text into units called tokens, such as words (lesson 6.4 shows how LLMs tokenize).
- **Stop-word removal**: drop very common words such as "the," "is," and "of," which carry little meaning alone.
- **Stemming**: cut words down to a shared root, so "connected," "connecting," and "connects" all count as "connect." A related step, **lemmatization**, uses a dictionary to find each word's base form, so "ran" becomes "run."
- **Bag-of-words**: represent a document by how many times each word appears, ignoring order. "Dog bites man" and "man bites dog" get identical bags.
- **N-grams**: count sequences of n neighboring words to keep some order. "The cat sat" has the bigrams (2-grams) "the cat" and "cat sat"; a text of n words has n - 1 bigrams.
- **TF-IDF** (term frequency times inverse document frequency): weight each word by how often it appears in this document and how rare it is across the whole collection. A word that is frequent here but rare elsewhere scores high, so it is a good clue to what the document is about, while words that appear everywhere score near zero.

## Speech recognition and synthesis

Speech technology runs in two directions:

- **Speech recognition**, also called automatic speech recognition (ASR) or speech-to-text, converts spoken audio into written words. It must cope with accents, background noise, fast or mumbled speech, and words that sound alike, such as "their" and "there."
- **Speech synthesis**, also called text-to-speech (TTS), converts written text into spoken audio, ideally with natural rhythm and tone.

A voice assistant chains the pieces together. Speech recognition turns your question into text, natural language understanding works out what you want, the system finds the answer and generates a reply, and speech synthesis speaks it.

## Robotics

**Robotics** is the field that designs and builds robots: machines that sense the physical world, decide what to do, and act on it, often with some independence. AI supplies the deciding and much of the sensing. Robots repeat a **sense, plan, act** cycle:

1. **Sense.** **Sensors** gather information: cameras, **lidar** (which measures distance with laser pulses), ultrasonic range finders, GPS, accelerometers, and touch sensors.
2. **Plan.** The robot interprets what it sensed and decides what to do, such as choosing a path around an obstacle.
3. **Act.** **Actuators**, such as motors, wheels, and grippers, carry out the decision. Then the robot senses again to see what changed.

Robots range from factory arms that repeat precise motions to warehouse robots, robot vacuums, drones, surgical systems guided by a surgeon, and self-driving cars, which are robots on wheels (lesson 8.1 covers their levels of automation). A **teleoperated** robot is controlled remotely by a person; an **autonomous** robot makes its own decisions. The physical world is hard for AI: sensors are noisy, situations change in real time, and mistakes can hurt people, so safety is central to robotics.

## Human-AI interaction

**Human-AI interaction** studies how people and AI systems communicate and work together, and how to design that relationship well. Its goals include:

- **Natural interaction**: let people use speech, text, gestures, or touch instead of special commands.
- **Appropriate trust**: show confidence levels and explanations, so users rely on the system when it is right and question it when it is not, rather than trusting it blindly.
- **Human control**: keep a person in the loop for important decisions, with easy ways to correct or override the AI.
- **Accessibility**: open technology to more people, through live captions, screen readers, and voice control for people with disabilities.

**Affective computing** is the branch that recognizes and responds to human emotions, using cues such as facial expressions, tone of voice, gestures, and word choice. A tutoring system might notice frustration and offer a hint, and a customer-service agent might slow its speech for an upset caller. Emotion recognition is also contested: the EU AI Act lists emotion recognition in workplaces and education institutions among its banned practices.

## Expert systems, planning, and recommender systems [Competitive]

Three more subfields round out the map.

**Expert systems** capture the knowledge of human specialists as rules. A **knowledge base** holds facts and if-then rules, and an **inference engine** applies the rules to the facts of a case to reach conclusions. MYCIN, begun at Stanford in 1972, advised doctors on antibiotics, and XCON configured computer orders at Digital Equipment Corporation (lesson 6.1). Lesson 7.5 shows how inference engines chain rules together.

**Automated planning** finds a sequence of actions that leads from a starting state to a goal, such as scheduling deliveries, ordering a robot's tasks, or guiding a game character. Planners rely on the search methods of lesson 7.8.

**Recommender systems** suggest items a user is likely to want: movies, songs, products, posts. There are two main approaches:

| Approach | How it works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Content-based filtering** | Recommends items whose features resemble what this user already liked | More mystery novels for a mystery reader |
| **Collaborative filtering** | Recommends what similar users liked or bought | "Customers who bought this also bought..." |

Many services combine the two in a **hybrid** recommender. Collaborative filtering struggles with the **cold-start problem**: a new user or a new item has no history to match on, so the system falls back on item features, popular items, or a few questions about the user's tastes.

:::tip
**Test tip.** For "which subfield?" questions, find the input and the output. Pixels in, labels out: computer vision. Words in, meaning or words out: NLP. Sound in, text out: speech recognition. Sensors in, physical action out: robotics. Experts' rules applied to a case: an expert system. User history in, suggestions out: a recommender system.
:::

:::takeaways
- Computer vision interprets images, which are grids of pixel values (0 to 255, with three channels for color). Classification labels the whole image, detection boxes each object, segmentation labels every pixel (semantic by class, instance by individual object), and OCR reads text. CNNs are the standard models.
- NLP understands (NLU) and generates (NLG) human language. Tasks: sentiment analysis, translation, named entity recognition, classification, summarization, question answering, conversation.
- Ambiguity: lexical, including polysemy (one word, related meanings); reference (which noun a pronoun means); attachment (how phrases connect). Winograd sentences need commonsense knowledge.
- POS tagging labels words, and a parse tree groups them into NP, VP, and PP. Text features: tokens, stop-word removal, stemming, bag-of-words (no order), n-grams (n - 1 bigrams for n words), and TF-IDF (frequent here, rare elsewhere).
- Speech recognition turns speech into text; speech synthesis turns text into speech.
- Robotics: sense (sensors such as lidar), plan, act (actuators such as motors and grippers).
- Human-AI interaction aims for natural interaction, appropriate trust, human control, and accessibility; affective computing recognizes emotions.
- Expert systems: a knowledge base plus an inference engine. Planning: action sequences that reach a goal. Recommenders: content-based (item features) or collaborative (similar users), with a cold-start problem for newcomers.
:::

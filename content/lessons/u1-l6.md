title: Where data comes from
---

Data scientists rarely create data from nothing. They gather it from somewhere, and where it comes from decides almost everything else: its structure, how fast it arrives, how trustworthy it is, and what conclusions it can support. The official objective asks you to "describe the types of data that could be gathered from various sources." Test questions match sources to the data they produce, separate primary from secondary data and experiments from observational studies, name the Vs of big data, and define pipelines and governance.

## Common sources and the data each produces

A **data source** is any origin of data: a device, a system, an organization, or a process that produces or holds it. The table shows the sources you should know and what each typically yields.

| Source | Example | Typical data | Structure | Speed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Surveys and questionnaires | A customer satisfaction survey | Ratings, choices, written comments | Structured answers plus unstructured comments | Collected once or periodically |
| Sensors and the Internet of Things (IoT) | Smart thermostats, fitness trackers, factory machines | Time-stamped readings | Structured or semi-structured | Continuous streams |
| Transactions | Store checkouts, online orders, bank transfers | Items, amounts, times, customer IDs | Structured | Constant flow |
| Logs | Web server logs, app events, clickstreams | What users did and when | Semi-structured | High volume, continuous |
| Social media | Posts, comments, likes, shares | Text, images, video, connections | Mostly unstructured | Very fast |
| Public records and open data | Census tables, government portals such as data.gov | Population, economic, and geographic data | Structured | Released periodically |
| Internal databases | Customer records, inventory systems | Business records | Structured | Updated continuously |
| Interviews and focus groups | Recorded conversations | Opinions and explanations in words | Unstructured | Small, slow |

The **Internet of Things** means everyday physical objects fitted with sensors and network connections, so they report data on their own. That is why sensor data arrives as a fast, never-ending stream rather than as a finished file.

:::tip
**Test tip.** When a question describes a goal and asks for the best source, match the data to the question. "What do customers buy together?" calls for transaction records. "How do people feel about our brand?" calls for social media or surveys. "How hot does the machine run each minute?" calls for sensors.
:::

## APIs, web scraping, and open data [Competitive]

Two ways to collect data from the web come up constantly.

An **API** (application programming interface) is a defined way for one program to request data or services from another. A weather service's API, for example, answers a request such as "current conditions for Austin" with structured data, usually JSON. APIs are the preferred route: the data is clean and documented, access is permitted (often with a key and a limit on how many requests you can make), and the format does not break when the website's design changes.

**Web scraping** means writing a program that downloads web pages and pulls data out of their HTML, for example with Python's Requests and BeautifulSoup libraries. It works when no API exists, but it is fragile, because a redesign can break the scraper, and it raises ethical and legal questions. Before scraping, check the site's terms of service and its robots.txt file (which says what automated programs may access), avoid collecting personal data, and keep your request rate low so you do not burden the site. If an API exists, use it instead.

**Open data** is data published for anyone to use, such as government portals, research datasets, and public collections like Kaggle. It is free and often well documented, but check its license, its documentation, and how current it is.

## Primary and secondary data

**Primary data** is collected firsthand, by you, for your own question: your own survey, your own experiment, sensors you installed. It fits your question exactly and you know how it was gathered, but it costs time and money.

**Secondary data** was collected by someone else for another purpose, and you reuse it: census data, a published dataset, or another department's records. It is fast and cheap and often large, but it may not fit your question exactly, may be out of date, and you cannot control how it was collected.

Businesses also sort data by who collected it:

- **First-party data** is collected directly from your own customers or users, such as your store's purchase history.
- **Second-party data** is another organization's first-party data shared with you through a partnership.
- **Third-party data** is bought from companies that gather it from many sources, without a direct relationship with the people involved.

:::trap
**Common trap.** "Secondary" does not mean "less accurate." Census data is secondary for nearly everyone who uses it, and it is among the most carefully collected data there is. The terms describe who collected the data and why, not its quality.
:::

## Experiments versus observational studies

How data was collected decides what you may conclude from it.

In an **observational study**, researchers record what happens without changing anything. A survey of people's sleep and grades, or a record of which shoppers used coupons, is observational.

In an **experiment**, researchers deliberately apply a **treatment** to some subjects and compare them with a **control group** that does not get it. When subjects are assigned to the groups **at random**, the groups are alike in every way except the treatment, so a difference in outcomes can be credited to the treatment. That makes a randomized experiment the standard way to establish cause and effect. An **A/B test**, which randomly shows two versions of a web page to different visitors, is a randomized experiment.

Observational data can show that two things are related but not that one causes the other. People who drink green tea might have fewer heart attacks because they also exercise more or eat differently; a factor like that, which affects both variables, is called a **confounding variable**. You will study confounding in depth in Unit 3.

Two more terms: a **census** collects data from every member of a population, while a **sample** collects data from a subset and uses it to draw conclusions about the whole.

:::key
**Key idea.** Only a randomized experiment supports a cause-and-effect claim. Observational data, no matter how large, shows association.
:::

## Cross-sectional, longitudinal, and time series data [Competitive]

Data also differs in how it covers time:

- **Cross-sectional data** measures many subjects at a single point in time, like a snapshot: a survey of 2,000 voters during one week in October.
- **Longitudinal data** (also called **panel data**) measures the same subjects repeatedly over time: the grades of the same 500 students, recorded every year from ninth grade to twelfth.
- **Time series data** records one quantity at regular intervals: a stock's daily closing price, or the temperature every hour.

Longitudinal data is powerful because it shows how individuals change, not just how a group looks at one moment. Cross-sectional data is faster and cheaper to collect.

::check{id="study-types-03"}

## Big data and the Vs

**Big data** means data so large, so fast, or so varied that ordinary tools such as a single computer or a spreadsheet cannot store or process it. In 2001, analyst Doug Laney described three challenges of rapidly growing data, and these "three Vs" became the standard way to define big data:

- **Volume**: the sheer amount of data, often terabytes or more.
- **Velocity**: the speed at which data arrives and must be processed, as with sensor streams or card transactions.
- **Variety**: the many types and formats, from tables to text, images, and video.

Later writers added more Vs. The most common are **veracity**, the accuracy and trustworthiness of the data (IBM promoted it as a fourth V), and **value**, whether the data can actually be turned into something useful.

Speed also shapes how data is processed. **Batch processing** handles data that has been collected and stored, in large chunks on a schedule, such as a nightly sales report. **Stream processing** handles each piece of data as it arrives, which is necessary when an answer is needed immediately, such as flagging a fraudulent card swipe before it is approved.

Because big data is too large for one machine, tools such as **Hadoop** and **Apache Spark** spread both storage and computation across many computers working in parallel. You only need to recognize these names and what they are for.

::::example[Name the V]
A hospital combines lab-result tables, doctors' typed notes, X-ray images, and live heart-monitor readings into one system. Which V is the main challenge, and which second V does the heart-monitor feed add?

:::step
Tables, free text, images, and readings are four different kinds of data, so the main challenge is variety.
:::

:::step
The heart monitors send readings continuously, which must be processed as they arrive, so they add a velocity challenge.
:::

:::answer
Variety, with velocity from the live monitor feed.
:::
::::

## Pipelines, ETL, governance, and lineage [Competitive]

Once data is flowing in, organizations need ways to move it and rules for managing it.

A **data pipeline** is an automated series of steps that moves data from its sources, through processing, to a destination such as a warehouse, a dashboard, or a model. The classic pipeline pattern is **ETL**:

1. **Extract** data from the sources.
2. **Transform** it: clean it, fix formats, combine sources, and calculate new fields.
3. **Load** it into the destination, usually a data warehouse.

A newer pattern, **ELT**, loads the raw data first and transforms it later inside the destination. Cheap cloud storage made ELT popular, because it keeps the raw data and lets analysts transform it in different ways later.

**Data governance** is the set of policies, roles, and processes that keep an organization's data accurate, secure, available to the right people, and used legally and ethically. It covers who owns each dataset (often a data steward), who may access it, which quality standards it must meet, how long it is kept, and how privacy laws are followed.

**Data lineage** (also called provenance) is the record of where data came from and every transformation it went through on the way to its current form. When a dashboard shows a wrong number, lineage lets you trace the error back to the source or step that caused it. A **data catalog** is a searchable inventory of an organization's datasets with their metadata, so people can find data and understand it.

:::takeaways
- Sources and their data: surveys (ratings and comments), sensors and IoT (fast time-stamped streams), transactions (structured purchases), logs and clickstreams (user actions), social media (mostly unstructured), public records and open data (structured tables).
- An API is the documented, permitted way to request data, usually as JSON. Scraping parses HTML; it is fragile and requires checking terms of service and robots.txt.
- Primary data is collected by you for your question; secondary data is reused from others. First-party is your own customers' data, second-party is a partner's shared data, third-party is bought.
- Observational studies show association; only randomized experiments establish cause and effect. Watch for confounding variables.
- Cross-sectional data is many subjects at one time; longitudinal (panel) data follows the same subjects over time; a time series records one quantity at regular intervals.
- Big data: volume, velocity, variety (Laney, 2001), with veracity and value added later. Batch processing works on stored data; stream processing works as data arrives.
- Pipelines move data automatically. ETL transforms before loading; ELT loads raw data and transforms later.
- Governance sets the rules for data quality, access, privacy, and retention. Lineage traces where data came from and how it changed.
:::

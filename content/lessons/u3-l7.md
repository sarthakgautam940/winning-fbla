The objectives name three algorithms you must be able to apply to real problems: linear regression, decision trees, and k-means. Test questions hand you a fitted model and ask for a prediction or an interpretation, show you a tree and ask how it classifies a case, walk you through k-means, or describe a business problem and ask which kind of algorithm fits. This lesson teaches each one at the level of use; Unit 5 returns to how they learn.

## Linear regression: slope, intercept, prediction

**Linear regression** models a numeric response as a straight-line function of a predictor:

$$
\hat{y} = a + bx
$$

where a is the **intercept** and b is the **slope**. Businesses use it to predict sales from advertising spend, home prices from size, delivery times from distance, and demand from price.

::::example[A taxi fare model]
A city's taxi fares fit the model predicted fare = 3.50 + 2.25 × (miles). Predict the fare for an 8-mile ride and interpret both numbers in the model.

:::step
Substitute 8 for miles: 3.50 + 2.25 × 8 = 3.50 + 18.00 = 21.50.
:::

:::step
The slope, 2.25, is in dollars per mile: each additional mile adds $2.25 to the predicted fare.
:::

:::step
The intercept, 3.50, is the predicted fare for a ride of 0 miles. Here that makes sense: it is the base charge for getting in the cab.
:::

:::answer
The predicted fare is $21.50. Each mile adds $2.25, on top of a $3.50 base charge.
:::
::::

Interpret a slope with a sentence like this: "For each one-unit increase in *x* (with its units), the predicted *y* increases (or decreases) by *b* (with its units), on average." The words "predicted" and "on average" matter, because individual cases scatter around the line.

The intercept is the predicted y when x = 0, and it is often meaningless. A model of ice cream sales against temperature predicts sales at 0 degrees, a day the shop may never open. Only interpret the intercept when x = 0 is a sensible value inside the range of the data.

:::trap
**Common trap.** A slope describes an association, not a lever you can pull. If sales rise $15 for every degree warmer, that does not mean heating the shop would raise sales. Causal claims still need an experiment (lesson 3.3).
:::

## Residuals and the danger of extrapolation [Competitive]

A **residual** is actual minus predicted (lesson 3.3). If the 8-mile ride above actually cost $24.00, its residual is 24.00 - 21.50 = 2.50: the model underpredicted it by $2.50, perhaps because of traffic.

**Extrapolation** means using a model to predict outside the range of x-values it was built from. It is risky because the straight-line pattern may not continue. A model of children's height built from ages 2 to 10 might be predicted height = 31 + 2.5 × age, in inches. It works well within ages 2 to 10, but at age 40 it predicts 31 + 2.5 × 40 = 131 inches, almost 11 feet, because people stop growing. Predictions inside the data's range, called **interpolation**, are much safer.

## Decision trees: following the branches

A **decision tree** makes predictions by asking a sequence of questions about a case's features. Each answer sends the case down a branch to the next question, until it reaches an end point that gives the prediction. The parts have names:

- the **root node** is the first question, at the top, which every case passes through;
- **internal nodes** are the later questions;
- **branches** are the answers that connect the nodes;
- **leaf nodes** are the end points, each holding a prediction.

A **classification tree** predicts a category at each leaf; a **regression tree** predicts a number, usually the average outcome of the training cases that reached that leaf.

```figure
type: tree
caption: A loan-approval tree. Follow one path from the root to a leaf for each applicant.
root:
  label: Credit score 680 or higher?
  children:
    - label: Debt under 40% of income?
      edge: "Yes"
      children:
        - { label: Approve, edge: "Yes" }
        - { label: Decline, edge: "No" }
    - label: Has a cosigner?
      edge: "No"
      children:
        - { label: Approve, edge: "Yes" }
        - { label: Decline, edge: "No" }
```

An applicant with a credit score of 700 and debt at 45% of income goes right at the root (700 is at least 680), then answers "No" to the debt question, so the tree predicts **Decline**. An applicant with a score of 640 who has a cosigner goes the other way at the root and ends at **Approve**.

Trees are popular because they are **interpretable**: every prediction can be written as a short chain of rules, such as "credit score 680 or higher and debt under 40% of income, so approve." That matters wherever decisions must be explained to customers, doctors, or regulators. Typical applications include loan approval, medical triage, predicting which customers will cancel, and screening for fraud. A tree that grows too deep can memorize its training data, a problem lesson 5.8 covers along with how trees choose their questions.

## k-means: the algorithm step by step

**K-means clustering** is an unsupervised algorithm (it uses no labels) that divides data points into k groups called **clusters**. Each cluster has a center, its **centroid**, and every point belongs to the cluster with the nearest centroid. The algorithm repeats two moves until nothing changes:

1. **Choose k**, the number of clusters.
2. **Initialize** k centroids, often by picking k data points at random.
3. **Assign** every point to its nearest centroid, usually measured by straight-line (Euclidean) distance.
4. **Update** each centroid to the mean of the points assigned to it.
5. **Repeat** steps 3 and 4 until the assignments stop changing. The algorithm has then **converged**.

::::example[One round of k-means]
Two centroids sit at (1, 1) and (6, 6). Assign the point (5, 1), then find the new centroid of a cluster holding (2, 3), (4, 7), and (6, 5).

:::step
Distance from (5, 1) to (1, 1): $$\sqrt{(5 - 1)^2 + (1 - 1)^2} = \sqrt{16} = 4$$.
:::

:::step
Distance from (5, 1) to (6, 6): $$\sqrt{(5 - 6)^2 + (1 - 6)^2} = \sqrt{26} \approx 5.10$$. The point joins the cluster centered at (1, 1), the nearer one.
:::

:::step
A centroid is the mean of its points, coordinate by coordinate: x = (2 + 4 + 6) / 3 = 4 and y = (3 + 7 + 5) / 3 = 5.
:::

:::answer
(5, 1) is assigned to the centroid at (1, 1), and the cluster's new centroid is (4, 5).
:::
::::

Try it: step through k-means one move at a time. Watch the points change color as they are assigned, the centroids jump to the means of their points, and the total squared distance shrink until the clusters stop changing.

::widget{name="kmeans-stepper"}

k-means minimizes the **within-cluster sum of squares** (WCSS, also called inertia): the total squared distance from each point to its centroid. Three practical cautions:

- **Scale the features first.** k-means works with distances, so a feature measured in thousands (income in dollars) swamps one measured in single digits (number of children). Standardize the features to z-scores before clustering (lesson 1.7).
- **Starting points matter.** Different initial centroids can give different final clusters, so software usually runs the algorithm several times and keeps the best result.
- **Shape matters.** k-means works best for round clusters of similar size and is pulled around by outliers.

Common applications include customer segmentation, grouping similar documents, and reducing the colors in an image.

::check{id="kmeans-applied-01"}

## Choosing k: elbow and silhouette [Competitive]

k-means needs k before it starts, so analysts try several values and compare.

The **elbow method** runs k-means for k = 1, 2, 3, and so on, and plots the WCSS for each. WCSS always falls as k grows (with one cluster per point it reaches 0), so the goal is not the lowest value. Look for the **elbow**, the k after which adding clusters gives only small improvements.

```figure
type: line
caption: An elbow plot. WCSS drops steeply up to k = 3, then levels off, so 3 clusters is a good choice.
xLabel: Number of clusters k
yLabel: Within-cluster sum of squares
series:
  - points: [[1, 400], [2, 210], [3, 90], [4, 75], [5, 65], [6, 58], [7, 52], [8, 47]]
```

The **silhouette score** measures how well each point fits its cluster. For one point, let a be its average distance to the other points in its own cluster and b its average distance to the points in the nearest other cluster. Then:

$$
s = \frac{b - a}{\max(a, b)}
$$

Scores run from -1 to 1. A score near 1 means the point sits snugly in its own cluster, near 0 means it lies on a boundary, and a negative score means it is probably in the wrong cluster. A point with a = 2 and b = 6 scores (6 - 2) / 6, about 0.67. Analysts often choose the k with the highest average silhouette score. The business need matters too: a marketing team may simply want four segments it can act on.

## Matching problems to algorithms

Most applied questions come down to one move: identify what the output should be.

| The output you want | Task | Typical algorithms |
|---|---|---|
| A number (price, demand, time) | **Regression** | Linear regression, regression trees |
| A category (spam or not, approve or decline) | **Classification** | Logistic regression, decision trees |
| Groups, with no labels given | **Clustering** | k-means, hierarchical clustering |
| Unusual cases (fraud, faults) | **Anomaly detection** | z-scores, distance from cluster centers, isolation forests |
| Items that occur together | **Association rules** | Market basket analysis, such as the Apriori algorithm |
| Items a user may like | **Recommendation** | Collaborative filtering, content-based filtering |
| Future values over time | **Forecasting** | Time-series models that capture trend and seasonality |

A few of these deserve a sentence each. **Anomaly detection** flags cases that differ sharply from the normal pattern, such as a card used in two countries within an hour. **Association rules** find if-then patterns in transactions, such as "shoppers who buy chips often buy salsa," which stores use for shelf placement and promotions. **Recommendation** systems use either the habits of similar users (collaborative filtering) or the features of items a user already liked (content-based filtering). **Forecasting** predicts future values of a time series, such as next month's electricity demand, from its own past.

:::tip
**Test tip.** Ask what the answer looks like. A number means regression; a label from a known set means classification; groups nobody defined in advance mean clustering. "Bought together" means association rules, "suggest" means recommendation, and "next month" or "next quarter" means forecasting.
:::

:::takeaways
- Linear regression predicts a number: y-hat = a + bx. Interpret the slope as the change in predicted y per one-unit change in x, with units, on average.
- The intercept is the prediction at x = 0 and is often meaningless. A slope shows association, not a lever.
- Residual = actual - predicted. Extrapolating beyond the range of the data is risky; interpolation is safer.
- A decision tree routes each case from the root through internal nodes to a leaf that holds the prediction. Trees are interpretable as chains of rules.
- k-means: choose k, place centroids, assign each point to the nearest centroid, move each centroid to its points' mean, and repeat until assignments stop changing. Scale features first.
- Choose k with the elbow of the WCSS plot or the highest silhouette score; silhouette runs from -1 to 1.
- Match the output to the task: number (regression), category (classification), unlabeled groups (clustering), unusual cases (anomaly detection), items together (association rules), suggestions (recommendation), future values (forecasting).
:::

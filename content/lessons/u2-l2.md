title: Quartiles, percentiles, and outliers

The mean and median describe the center. To describe the spread in a way that one extreme value cannot hijack, statisticians cut the sorted data into quarters. Quartiles give you the five-number summary behind every boxplot, the interquartile range, and the standard rule for flagging outliers. Test questions ask you to compute quartiles and fences by hand, interpret percentiles, and decide whether a value is an outlier.

## Quartiles and the five-number summary

**Quartiles** split sorted data into four parts, each holding about a quarter of the values:

- **Q1** (the first quartile) has about 25% of the data below it.
- **Q2** is the median, with 50% below.
- **Q3** (the third quartile) has about 75% below.

Statisticians compute quartiles in more than a dozen slightly different ways. The method taught in U.S. high-school statistics, and used by TI-83 and TI-84 calculators, is the **median-of-halves** method:

1. Sort the data and find the median.
2. Split the data into a lower half and an upper half. If n is odd, leave the median out of both halves.
3. Q1 is the median of the lower half; Q3 is the median of the upper half.

::::example[Quartiles with an odd count]
Find the quartiles of 6, 7, 15, 36, 39, 40, 41, 42, 43, 47, 49.

:::step
The 11 values are already sorted. The median is the 6th value, 40.
:::

:::step
Leave 40 out. The lower half is 6, 7, 15, 36, 39 and the upper half is 41, 42, 43, 47, 49.
:::

:::step
The median of each half is its middle value: Q1 = 15 and Q3 = 43.
:::

:::answer
Q1 = 15, median = 40, Q3 = 43.
:::
::::

With an even count, the halves split cleanly. For 1, 2, 4, 7, 8, 9, 10, 12, the median is (7 + 8) / 2 = 7.5, the lower half 1, 2, 4, 7 gives Q1 = (2 + 4) / 2 = 3, and the upper half 8, 9, 10, 12 gives Q3 = (9 + 10) / 2 = 9.5.

The **five-number summary** lists minimum, Q1, median, Q3, and maximum. For the first example it is 6, 15, 40, 43, 49. These five numbers are exactly what a boxplot draws (lesson 3.2).

::check{id="quartiles-01"}

## Percentiles and percentile rank

A **percentile** is a value below which a given percentage of the data falls. The 90th percentile of test scores is the score that about 90% of test takers scored below. Q1, the median, and Q3 are the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles.

A student's **percentile rank** says what percentage of the group scored below them. In a class of 40, a student with 30 classmates scoring below has a percentile rank of 30 / 40 = 75%, the 75th percentile. (Some definitions count values "at or below" instead of strictly below; with large groups the difference is tiny.)

:::trap
**Common trap.** A percentile is not a percent correct. Scoring at the 90th percentile means you outscored about 90% of the people who took the test, not that you answered 90% of the questions correctly. On a hard test, 60% correct could be the 90th percentile.
:::

::check{id="quartiles-02"}

## The interquartile range

The **interquartile range** (IQR) is the distance from Q1 to Q3:

$$
\text{IQR} = Q3 - Q1
$$

It measures the spread of the middle 50% of the data. For the first example, IQR = 43 - 15 = 28.

Unlike the range, the IQR ignores the extremes entirely, so it is **resistant** to outliers. Change the maximum from 49 to 4,900 and the range explodes while the IQR stays at 28. That makes the IQR the natural partner of the median: the median for center, the IQR for spread, whenever data are skewed or contain outliers.

::check{id="quartiles-03"}

## Fences: the 1.5 x IQR rule

How far from the middle must a value be to count as an outlier? The standard rule, from the statistician John Tukey, builds **fences** 1.5 IQRs beyond the quartiles:

$$
\text{lower fence} = Q1 - 1.5 \times \text{IQR} \qquad \text{upper fence} = Q3 + 1.5 \times \text{IQR}
$$

Any value below the lower fence or above the upper fence is flagged as a **potential outlier**. Values more than 3 IQRs beyond the quartiles (the outer fences) are sometimes called **extreme outliers**.

::::example[Is it an outlier?]
Is 45 an outlier in 12, 14, 15, 15, 16, 18, 19, 21, 45?

:::step
There are 9 sorted values, so the median is the 5th, 16. The lower half is 12, 14, 15, 15, so Q1 = 14.5. The upper half is 18, 19, 21, 45, so Q3 = 20.
:::

:::step
IQR = 20 - 14.5 = 5.5, and 1.5 x 5.5 = 8.25.
:::

:::step
Upper fence = 20 + 8.25 = 28.25. Lower fence = 14.5 - 8.25 = 6.25.
:::

:::answer
45 is above the upper fence of 28.25, so it is a potential outlier. No value falls below 6.25.
:::
::::

In a boxplot, the whiskers stop at the most extreme values that are still inside the fences, and any outliers are drawn as separate points.

:::tip
**Test tip.** Fence questions take four quick steps: Q1 and Q3, then IQR, then 1.5 x IQR, then add and subtract. Write the four numbers in a column on your scratch paper; it takes under 30 seconds.
:::

Flagging a value is not the same as deleting it. An outlier might be a typo, or it might be the most important point in the data. Lesson 3.5 covers how to decide.

::check{id="outlier-rule-01"}

## Other outlier rules: z-scores [Competitive]

A second common rule uses the mean and standard deviation. Compute each value's **z-score**, the number of standard deviations it lies from the mean (lesson 2.5 covers z-scores fully):

$$
z = \frac{x - \bar{x}}{s}
$$

Values more than 2 standard deviations from the mean (|z| > 2) are often flagged as unusual, and values more than 3 away (|z| > 3) as outliers. With mean 70 and standard deviation 5, a value of 88 has z = (88 - 70) / 5 = 3.6, so both rules flag it.

The z-score rule has a weakness. The mean and standard deviation are themselves pulled toward the outliers they are trying to detect, which can hide an outlier, especially in a small or skewed dataset. The 1.5 x IQR rule uses resistant quartiles, so it is usually the more reliable choice for skewed data.

::check{id="outlier-rule-02"}

## Why software gives different quartiles [Champion]

Because there are many quartile methods, the same data can produce different answers on a calculator, in Excel, and in Python. pandas, NumPy, and R all default to **linear interpolation** between data points:

```python run
import pandas as pd

s = pd.Series([1, 2, 4, 7, 8, 9, 10, 12])
print(s.quantile([0.25, 0.5, 0.75]).tolist())
```

```output
[3.5, 7.5, 9.25]
```

By hand, the median-of-halves method gave Q1 = 3 and Q3 = 9.5 for these same values; pandas gives 3.5 and 9.25. A third method, Tukey's hinges (used for the box in R's boxplots), includes the median in both halves when n is odd. Neither answer is wrong; they are different definitions. The medians always agree.

For the test: when a question does not name a method, use the median-of-halves method. Well-written questions either name the method or use data where the methods agree. If a computed quartile differs from yours by a small amount, a different method is almost always the reason.

::check{id="quartiles-09"}

:::takeaways
- Q1, Q2 (median), and Q3 split sorted data into quarters: the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles.
- Median-of-halves: find the median, split the data (leaving the median out when n is odd), and take the median of each half.
- Five-number summary: minimum, Q1, median, Q3, maximum. It is the basis of the boxplot.
- A percentile rank is the percent of values below a score, not the percent correct.
- IQR = Q3 - Q1, the spread of the middle 50%. It is resistant to outliers.
- Fences: Q1 - 1.5 x IQR and Q3 + 1.5 x IQR. Values outside are potential outliers; beyond 3 x IQR they are extreme outliers.
- z-score rules flag |z| > 2 or |z| > 3, but the mean and SD can be distorted by the outliers themselves.
- Software interpolates quartiles (pandas gives Q1 = 3.5 where the hand method gives 3). Medians always agree.
:::

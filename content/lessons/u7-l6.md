title: Logic versus probability
---

Logic gives certain answers, but the real world rarely gives certain facts. A sensor reading is noisy, a symptom has several possible causes, a rule has exceptions. One objective asks you to discuss the differences between logic-based and probability-based reasoning, so this lesson sets the two side by side, separates probability from fuzzy logic, and shows how Bayes' rule updates a belief when evidence arrives.

## Logic-based reasoning: certain conclusions

**Logic-based reasoning** derives conclusions from facts and rules using formal logic, so that if the premises are true, the conclusions are certainly true. Every statement is either true or false, with nothing in between. From "every employee receives a badge" and "Ava is an employee," a logic-based system concludes, with certainty, that Ava receives a badge.

This is the reasoning of lessons 7.3 to 7.5: predicate logic, rules, deduction, and the forward and backward chaining of expert systems. AI4K12 pairs logical deduction problems with resolution theorem proving and IF-THEN rules. Logic shines when the knowledge is exact and complete: tax and eligibility rules, checking that a chip design meets its specification, configuring a product from known parts.

Its weakness is brittleness. Classical logic has no way to say "usually" or "probably." If a system concludes "Tweety flies" from "birds fly" and then learns that Tweety is a penguin, classical logic cannot simply withdraw the conclusion; a rule with exceptions is, to logic, a false rule.

## Probability-based reasoning: degrees of belief

**Probability-based reasoning** represents uncertainty with probabilities, **degrees of belief** between 0 and 1, and updates them as evidence arrives, so it reaches the most likely conclusions rather than certain ones. A spam filter reports that a message is 97 percent likely to be spam; a self-driving car estimates how likely a shape in the fog is to be a pedestrian; a diagnosis system ranks possible diseases by probability.

Probability handles exactly what breaks logic: noisy measurements, incomplete information, and rules that hold only most of the time. "Birds fly" becomes "a bird flies with probability 0.9," and learning that Tweety is a penguin simply moves that belief down. AI4K12 pairs statistical inference problems with Bayesian networks (lesson 7.7). The approach owes much to Judea Pearl, who championed probabilistic reasoning in AI, published *Probabilistic Reasoning in Intelligent Systems* in 1988, and received the 2011 Turing Award for a calculus of probabilistic and causal reasoning.

## Strengths and weaknesses side by side

| | Logic-based reasoning | Probability-based reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Truth values | True or false | Degrees of belief from 0 to 1 |
| Conclusions | Certain, if the premises are true | The most likely, with a stated confidence |
| Uncertainty, noise, exceptions | Handled poorly; brittle | Handled naturally |
| Explanation | Clear, step-by-step proofs | Numbers that can be harder to interpret |
| What it needs | Rules written by people | Probabilities estimated from data or experts |
| Best fits | Rules, regulations, proofs, configuration, planning with known facts | Diagnosis, prediction, filtering, sensor data, anything noisy |

To choose, ask whether the problem's facts and rules are exact. If they are, and you need guaranteed, explainable conclusions, use logic. If the evidence is noisy, incomplete, or only usually reliable, use probability. Many real systems combine them, attaching confidence to rules as MYCIN did with its numerical degrees of certainty (lesson 7.5).

::check{id="logic-vs-prob-03"}

## Fuzzy logic is a different idea [Competitive]

**Fuzzy logic**, introduced by Lotfi Zadeh in a 1965 paper on fuzzy sets, also uses numbers between 0 and 1, but they mean something different. In fuzzy logic, a number is the **degree** to which a vague property holds. A room at 24 degrees Celsius might be "warm" to degree 0.7: not fully warm, not fully cool, but warm to a large extent. Membership in categories such as tall, fast, or warm is a matter of degree rather than yes or no.

Probability, by contrast, describes uncertainty about something crisp that is either true or not. Compare:

- "There is a 70 percent chance of rain tomorrow." Tomorrow it will either rain or not; the 0.7 measures uncertainty. That is **probability**.
- "The bathwater is hot to degree 0.7." There is nothing uncertain; the water simply is somewhat hot. That is **fuzzy logic**.

Fuzzy rules such as "IF the room is warm THEN set the fan to medium" let controllers blend smoothly between settings instead of switching abruptly, which is why fuzzy logic is used in appliance and industrial control.

:::trap
**Common trap.** Fuzzy logic is not a kind of probability. Fuzzy values describe vagueness, meaning how much something has a property. Probabilities describe uncertainty about whether something is true.
:::

## Updating beliefs with Bayes' rule

Probability-based reasoning revises beliefs with **Bayes' rule** (lesson 2.7). Its parts have names you should know:

- The **prior**, $$P(H)$$, is the belief in a hypothesis before seeing the evidence.
- The **likelihood**, $$P(E \mid H)$$, is how probable the evidence is if the hypothesis is true.
- The **posterior**, $$P(H \mid E)$$, is the updated belief after seeing the evidence.
- The **evidence**, $$P(E)$$, is the overall probability of the evidence, counting every way it can occur.

$$
P(H \mid E) = \frac{P(E \mid H)\, P(H)}{P(E)}
$$

::::example[Update a belief with evidence]
In a bank, 1 percent of card transactions are fraudulent. A fraud detector flags 90 percent of fraudulent transactions, and it also flags 5 percent of legitimate ones by mistake. A transaction is flagged. How likely is it to be fraud?

:::step
Imagine 10,000 transactions. The prior says 1 percent, or 100, are fraud, and 9,900 are legitimate.
:::

:::step
The detector flags 90 percent of the 100 frauds, which is 90, and 5 percent of the 9,900 legitimate transactions, which is 495.
:::

:::step
Of the 90 + 495 = 585 flagged transactions, 90 are fraud. The posterior is 90 / 585, about 0.154.
:::

:::answer
A flagged transaction is only about 15 percent likely to be fraud. The evidence raised the belief from 1 percent to about 15 percent, but because fraud is rare, most alerts are false alarms. Ignoring the prior, the **base rate**, is the most common error in probabilistic reasoning.
:::
::::

Beliefs can keep updating: when new evidence arrives, today's posterior becomes tomorrow's prior. A second, independent alert on the same transaction would start from 15 percent, not 1 percent, and push the belief much higher.

**Naive Bayes** (lesson 5.8) is this kind of reasoning applied to classification. For each class, it multiplies the prior by the likelihood of each observed feature, assuming the features are independent given the class, and then picks the class with the largest result, or divides each result by their total to get posteriors. A spam filter that sees the words "free" and "winner" weighs how likely those words are in spam versus normal mail, scaled by how common each kind of mail is.

:::takeaways
- Logic-based reasoning derives certain conclusions from true premises; statements are true or false. It is precise and explainable but brittle with exceptions and uncertainty. AI4K12 pairs it with resolution and IF-THEN rules.
- Probability-based reasoning uses degrees of belief from 0 to 1, updated with evidence, and reaches the most likely conclusions. It handles noise and exceptions. AI4K12 pairs it with Bayesian networks; Judea Pearl championed it (1988 book, 2011 Turing Award).
- Use logic when facts and rules are exact and guarantees matter; use probability when evidence is noisy or incomplete.
- Fuzzy logic (Zadeh, 1965) measures the degree to which a vague property holds, as in "warm to degree 0.7." Probability measures uncertainty about whether a crisp statement is true, as in "a 70 percent chance of rain."
- Bayes' rule: posterior = likelihood × prior / evidence. The prior is the belief before the evidence; the likelihood is P(evidence given hypothesis); the posterior is the belief after.
- Respect the base rate: a 90 percent detector for a 1 percent event gives only about a 15 percent posterior. Today's posterior becomes tomorrow's prior.
- Naive Bayes multiplies the prior by each feature's likelihood, assuming independence given the class, and picks the most probable class.
:::

Reinforcement learning is the third major family of machine learning, and it learns in a completely different way from the other two: not from labeled answers or from patterns in data, but from the consequences of its own actions. It is how game-playing systems taught themselves to beat world champions, and it helps tune today's chatbots. Test questions ask you to name the parts of a reinforcement learning problem, tell it apart from supervised learning, explain exploration and exploitation, and, at the champion level, work with discounting and the Q-learning update.

## Agent, environment, state, action, reward

**Reinforcement learning** (RL) is learning to make a sequence of decisions by interacting with an environment and receiving rewards or penalties, with the goal of collecting as much total reward as possible over time. Five terms describe every RL problem:

- The **agent** is the learner and decision maker: a game-playing program, a robot's controller.
- The **environment** is everything the agent interacts with: the game, the room, the market.
- The **state** is the current situation the agent observes: the positions on a game board, the robot's location and sensor readings.
- An **action** is a choice the agent makes: move left, pick up an object, buy or sell.
- A **reward** is the number the environment sends back after an action. Positive rewards encourage the action; negative rewards, or penalties, discourage it.

The agent and environment repeat a simple loop:

```figure
type: flow
caption: The reinforcement learning loop. The agent acts, the environment responds with a reward and a new state, and the cycle repeats.
cycle: true
steps:
  - "Agent observes the state"
  - "Agent chooses an action"
  - "Environment returns a reward and a new state"
```

Take a video game agent that learns to collect coins. The agent is the program controlling the character, the environment is the game, the state is what is on the screen, the actions are the moves the character can make, and the reward is the points gained for a coin or lost for falling off a ledge.

## Policies and trial and error

The agent's strategy is its **policy**: a rule that says which action to take in each state (or with what probabilities to choose each action). Learning in RL means improving the policy until it earns as much reward as possible.

There are no labeled correct actions. The agent discovers good behavior by **trial and error**: it tries actions, sees what rewards follow, and shifts its policy toward what worked. Often the reward is **delayed**. A chess agent may learn whether it won only at the end of a game, and it must work out which of its many earlier moves deserve the credit or the blame, a challenge called the **credit assignment problem**.

That makes RL very different from supervised learning:

| | Supervised learning | Reinforcement learning |
|---|---|---|
| Feedback | The correct answer for every example | A reward signal, often delayed |
| Data | A fixed, labeled dataset | Experience the agent generates by acting |
| Goal | Predict labels accurately | Maximize total reward over time |

:::trap
**Common trap.** A reward is not a label. A label tells a model the right answer; a reward only says how good the outcome of an action was, so the agent must still search for better actions itself.
:::

::check{id="rl-basics-02"}

## Exploration versus exploitation

Every RL agent faces a dilemma. **Exploitation** means choosing the action that has worked best so far, collecting the reward it already knows about. **Exploration** means trying other actions to find out whether something better exists. An agent that only exploits may get stuck with a mediocre choice forever; an agent that only explores never cashes in on what it has learned.

The same trade-off appears in everyday life: always ordering your favorite meal guarantees a good dinner, but you will never find out whether something on the menu is even better.

A simple, common strategy is **epsilon-greedy**. With a small probability epsilon (written $$\varepsilon$$), such as 0.1, the agent takes a random action to explore; the rest of the time, 90% here, it takes the best-known action. Epsilon often starts high and shrinks as the agent learns, so it explores early and exploits later.

::check{id="rl-basics-03"}

## Markov decision processes and discounting [Competitive]

RL problems are usually described formally as a **Markov decision process** (MDP), which has five parts:

- a set of **states** S;
- a set of **actions** A;
- **transition probabilities**, the chance of landing in each next state after taking an action in a state;
- a **reward function**, the reward for each state and action;
- a **discount factor** $$\gamma$$ (gamma), between 0 and 1.

The word "Markov" refers to the **Markov property**: the next state depends only on the current state and action, not on the whole history of how the agent got there.

The agent tries to maximize its **return**, the total of its future rewards, with later rewards **discounted**, or shrunk, by a factor of $$\gamma$$ for each step of delay:

$$
G = r_1 + \gamma r_2 + \gamma^2 r_3 + \cdots
$$

::::example[A discounted return]
An agent will receive a reward of 5 at each of the next three steps, and $$\gamma = 0.8$$. What is its return?

:::step
The first reward counts fully: 5.
:::

:::step
The second is discounted once: 0.8 × 5 = 4. The third is discounted twice: 0.8 × 0.8 × 5 = 3.2.
:::

:::answer
G = 5 + 4 + 3.2 = 12.2.
:::
::::

The discount factor sets how far ahead the agent looks. With $$\gamma$$ near 0, future rewards shrink almost to nothing, so the agent is **short-sighted** and grabs immediate rewards. With $$\gamma$$ near 1, future rewards count almost as much as present ones, so the agent is **far-sighted** and will accept small costs now for large payoffs later.

::check{id="rl-details-01"}

## Q-learning [Champion]

**Q-learning** is a classic RL algorithm. It learns a **Q-value** for every state and action: $$Q(s, a)$$ is the expected total discounted reward from taking action a in state s and then acting well afterward. Once the Q-values are learned, the policy is simple: in each state, take the action with the highest Q-value. For small problems, the values are stored in a grid called a **Q-table**.

After each step, the agent updates the Q-value of the action it just took:

$$
Q(s, a) \leftarrow Q(s, a) + \alpha \left[\, r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s', a') - Q(s, a) \,\right]
$$

Here r is the reward just received, s' is the new state, $$\max_{a'} Q(s', a')$$ is the best Q-value available from the new state, and $$\alpha$$ is the learning rate. The quantity in brackets is the **error**: the gap between the new estimate (reward now plus the discounted best future value) and the old one.

::::example[One Q-learning update]
The current value is Q(s, a) = 2. The agent takes action a, receives reward r = 1, and lands in a state whose best Q-value is 4. With $$\alpha = 0.5$$ and $$\gamma = 0.9$$, what is the new Q(s, a)?

:::step
New estimate: r + γ × (best next value) = 1 + 0.9 × 4 = 1 + 3.6 = 4.6.
:::

:::step
Error: 4.6 - 2 = 2.6.
:::

:::step
Move partway toward the new estimate: 2 + 0.5 × 2.6 = 2 + 1.3 = 3.3.
:::

:::answer
The new Q(s, a) is 3.3.
:::
::::

Q-learning does not need to know the environment's transition probabilities; it learns directly from experience, which is why it is called **model-free**. When there are far too many states to list in a table, such as every possible screen of a video game, a neural network can estimate the Q-values instead, an approach known as a **deep Q-network**.

::check{id="rl-details-02"}

## Where RL is used

Reinforcement learning fits problems that involve a sequence of decisions, feedback that arrives over time, and an environment the agent can practice in, whether real or simulated:

- **Games.** DeepMind's AlphaGo beat the top professional Lee Sedol 4 games to 1 at Go in March 2016. Its networks were trained by combining supervised learning from human expert games with reinforcement learning from games it played against itself.
- **Robotics.** Robots learn to walk, grasp, and balance, often practicing first in simulation.
- **Recommendations and resource control.** Systems learn which suggestions or settings lead to good long-term outcomes.
- **Chatbots.** Models such as ChatGPT were fine-tuned with **reinforcement learning from human feedback** (RLHF), in which human ratings of the model's answers become the reward signal (lesson 6.4).

:::tip
**Test tip.** Look for an agent that acts, gets rewarded or penalized, and improves over many tries. "Learns by trial and error," "maximizes reward," and "plays against itself" all point to reinforcement learning.
:::

::check{id="rl-basics-04"}

:::takeaways
- Reinforcement learning: an agent learns a sequence of decisions by acting in an environment and receiving rewards, aiming to maximize total reward over time.
- Parts: agent, environment, state, action, reward. The policy maps states to actions.
- RL learns by trial and error from rewards, often delayed, not from labeled answers. Assigning credit to earlier actions is the credit assignment problem.
- Exploitation uses the best-known action; exploration tries others. Epsilon-greedy explores with probability epsilon.
- An MDP has states, actions, transition probabilities, rewards, and a discount factor. The Markov property: the next state depends only on the current state and action.
- Return G = r1 + γ r2 + γ² r3 + ... Low γ is short-sighted; high γ is far-sighted.
- Q(s, a) is the expected discounted return of taking a in s. Update: Q ← Q + α[r + γ max Q(s′, a′) - Q]. Deep Q-networks replace the table with a neural network.
- Uses: games (AlphaGo, 2016), robotics, recommendations, and RLHF for chatbots.
:::

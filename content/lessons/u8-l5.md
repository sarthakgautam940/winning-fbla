title: Privacy risks and protections

Data science runs on data about people, and AI models can leak what they learned from it. The test asks how models leak private data, why "anonymous" data often is not, and how techniques such as k-anonymity, differential privacy, and federated learning protect people. Two small calculations come up: finding k for a table, and reasoning about the privacy parameter epsilon.

## Memorization and training-data extraction

**Privacy** is people's ability to control information about themselves: what is collected, how it is used, and who can see it. The information at stake is **personal data**, which the European Union's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) defines as any information relating to an identified or identifiable person. A name is personal data, and so is a combination of facts that points to one person.

Large language models are trained on enormous collections of text gathered from the web, and that text can include names, phone numbers, and other personal details. Models mostly learn general patterns, but they can also **memorize** rare pieces of text and reproduce them word for word. **Training-data extraction** is an attack that queries a model to make it output memorized training data.

Two studies made the risk concrete. Nicholas Carlini and colleagues (2021) extracted hundreds of verbatim sequences from GPT-2's training data by querying it, including names, phone numbers, and email addresses. Milad Nasr and colleagues (2023) asked ChatGPT to repeat a word such as "poem" forever; the model eventually diverged and began emitting memorized training data, and the researchers recovered over 10,000 examples for about $200 of queries. NIST's Generative AI Profile lists data privacy among the 12 risks of generative AI, describing the leakage, unauthorized use, or de-anonymization of personal and sensitive data.

## Membership inference and model inversion [Competitive]

A model can leak information even when it never repeats a single training record.

**Membership inference** asks a narrower question: given a data record and query access to a model, was that record in the model's training set? Reza Shokri and colleagues (2017) showed this attack against models trained by commercial machine-learning services from Google and Amazon. Membership alone can be sensitive. If a model was trained only on records of patients with a certain condition, learning that someone's record was in the training set reveals that they have the condition.

**Model inversion** uses a model's outputs to reconstruct sensitive information about the people in its training data. Matt Fredrikson, Somesh Jha, and Thomas Ristenpart (2015) recovered recognizable images of people's faces given only their names and query access to a facial recognition model, by exploiting the confidence values the model returned with each prediction. One countermeasure they explored was returning only rounded confidence values.

| Attack | What the attacker learns | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Training-data extraction | Exact text the model memorized | Phone numbers recovered from GPT-2 |
| Membership inference | Whether a specific record was in the training set | Was this patient's record used to train the model? |
| Model inversion | A reconstruction of sensitive features | A recognizable face from a face-recognition model |

The main defenses work on the data and on what the model reveals: remove personal information from training data before training, train with differential privacy (later in this lesson), limit the detail in outputs such as confidence scores, and control who can query the model.

## What happens to what you type

Prompts are data too. Anything you type into a chatbot is sent to the provider's servers, and what happens next depends on the provider's policies and your settings. Treat a public chatbot like someone else's notebook: once you write in it, you no longer control where the words go. In 2023, Samsung employees entered confidential information into ChatGPT, including source code from a faulty semiconductor database and the content of a confidential meeting. Samsung first limited prompts to 1,024 bytes, then temporarily restricted employees' use of generative AI tools.

Two terms from privacy law describe what is at stake. **Consent**, in the GDPR's definition, is a freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous indication of a person's wishes, made by a statement or a clear affirmative action. Checking a box buried in pages of terms is a weak form of it, and sharing other people's data with a chatbot gives them no say at all. A **data breach**, which the GDPR calls a personal data breach, is a breach of security leading to the accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, alteration, unauthorized disclosure of, or access to personal data. Every copy of a prompt that a provider stores is one more place a breach could expose it.

The safest habit is **data minimization**, one of the GDPR's core principles: collect and share only the data a task needs. Before asking a chatbot to fix a spreadsheet formula, remove the customer names and account numbers; before asking for help with a letter, leave out other people's health or school records.

:::tip
**Test tip.** For a "what should the employee do?" question about pasting data into a chatbot, the best answer almost always removes or replaces the personal or confidential details and shares only what the task needs.
:::

## Anonymization and re-identification

**Anonymization** removes or alters information so that people can no longer be identified by any reasonably likely means. Truly anonymous data falls outside the GDPR's data protection rules. **Pseudonymization** replaces direct identifiers, such as names, with codes, so the data can no longer be tied to a person without additional information kept separately. The GDPR still treats pseudonymized data as personal data, because whoever holds the key, or enough outside information, can link it back.

:::trap
**Common trap.** Replacing names with ID numbers is pseudonymization, not anonymization. The records still describe real, findable people.
:::

**Re-identification** is linking supposedly anonymous records back to the people they describe, usually by combining them with other data. The danger comes from **quasi-identifiers**: attributes such as ZIP code, birth date, and sex that are not unique alone but identify people in combination.

- **Sweeney (2000).** Latanya Sweeney estimated, from 1990 census data, that 87 percent of the U.S. population was likely uniquely identifiable by 5-digit ZIP code, gender, and date of birth alone. A 2006 re-analysis by Philippe Golle found about 63 percent; either way, most Americans are unique on those three fields.
- **AOL (2006).** AOL released about 20 million search queries from roughly 650,000 users, with names replaced by ID numbers. Reporters for The New York Times used the searches to identify user No. 4417749 by name, and AOL removed the data and apologized.
- **Netflix Prize (2006 to 2010).** Arvind Narayanan and Vitaly Shmatikov showed that the "anonymized" Netflix Prize ratings could be re-identified by matching them with public movie reviews on IMDb: 8 movie ratings with dates known within 14 days uniquely identified 99 percent of records. After an FTC inquiry and a privacy lawsuit, Netflix cancelled a planned second contest in March 2010.

U.S. health privacy rules offer a checklist approach: under HIPAA's Safe Harbor method, health information counts as de-identified once 18 listed types of identifiers are removed and the organization has no actual knowledge that the rest could identify someone.

## k-anonymity [Competitive]

**k-anonymity**, formalized by Latanya Sweeney in 2002, requires that every record in a released table be indistinguishable from at least k minus 1 other records on its quasi-identifiers. Tables are made k-anonymous by **generalization**, replacing a value with a less specific one (ZIP 02138 becomes 0213*), and by **suppression**, not releasing a value at all.

To find k, group the records that share the same quasi-identifier values. The table is k-anonymous for k equal to the size of the smallest group.

::::example[Finding k]
A clinic releases this table. ZIP, age, and sex are quasi-identifiers; diagnosis is the sensitive attribute. What is k?

| ZIP | Age | Sex | Diagnosis |
|---|---|---|---|
| 021** | 20 to 29 | F | Flu |
| 021** | 20 to 29 | F | Asthma |
| 021** | 20 to 29 | F | Flu |
| 021** | 30 to 39 | M | Diabetes |
| 021** | 30 to 39 | M | Diabetes |
| 022** | 30 to 39 | M | Flu |
| 022** | 30 to 39 | M | Asthma |
| 022** | 30 to 39 | M | Flu |

:::step
Group the records by ZIP, age, and sex: (021**, 20 to 29, F) has 3 records; (021**, 30 to 39, M) has 2; (022**, 30 to 39, M) has 3.
:::

:::step
The smallest group has 2 records, so every record matches at least 1 other.
:::

:::answer
The table is 2-anonymous (k = 2). But look at the second group: both of its records say diabetes. Anyone who knows that a particular man aged 30 to 39 from ZIP 021** appears in the table learns that this person has diabetes, without ever finding the exact row.
:::
::::

k-anonymity hides *which record* is yours, but it can still leak *what is true of you*. If everyone in a group shares the same sensitive value, an attacker who knows you are in that group learns it anyway (a homogeneity attack), and attackers with background knowledge can narrow things further. These weaknesses led to l-diversity (2006), which requires several well-represented sensitive values in each group, and t-closeness (2007).

::check{id="privacy-tech-03"}

## Differential privacy and federated learning [Competitive]

**Differential privacy**, introduced in 2006 by Cynthia Dwork, Frank McSherry, Kobbi Nissim, and Adam Smith, promises that the result of an analysis is about the same whether or not any one person's data is included. It works by adding carefully calibrated random noise, scaled to how much one person's record could change the answer. Because your presence or absence barely changes the output, the output reveals little about you.

The privacy-loss parameter **epsilon** (ε), also called the privacy budget, sets how close "about the same" must be:

- A **smaller ε** means stronger privacy, more noise, and lower accuracy.
- A **larger ε** means weaker privacy, less noise, and higher accuracy.
- Budgets add up over repeated releases: NIST's example is ε = 1 per user per day, which totals ε = 365 over a year.

The U.S. Census Bureau used differential privacy for the 2020 Census, setting a total privacy-loss budget of ε = 19.61 for the redistricting data. The noise was not applied to the apportionment counts that divide seats in Congress among the states.

**Federated learning**, named by Google researchers H. Brendan McMahan and colleagues (2016 to 2017), trains a shared model across many devices that keep their data locally. Each device improves the model on its own data and sends only the model update to a server, which averages the updates. In Google's 2017 test on Gboard, its phone keyboard, phones improved the suggestion model on the device and sent back model updates, not what users typed; a Secure Aggregation protocol let the server see only the average of many updates. Updates can still carry information about the data behind them, which is why Google paired federated learning with Secure Aggregation.

| Technique | What it protects | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| Data minimization | Data never collected cannot leak | Less data to analyze |
| Pseudonymization | Hides direct identifiers | Still personal data; can be reversed |
| k-anonymity | Which record is yours | Leaks shared sensitive values; loses detail |
| Differential privacy | What any one person's data reveals, even whether they are included | Noise lowers accuracy (set by ε) |
| Federated learning | Raw data stays on each device | Model updates can still leak information |

::check{id="privacy-tech-04"}

:::takeaways
- Privacy is people's control over information about themselves; personal data is any information relating to an identifiable person.
- LLMs can memorize and leak training data: GPT-2 leaked names, phone numbers, and emails (Carlini and colleagues, 2021); a "repeat poem forever" attack pulled over 10,000 memorized examples from ChatGPT for about $200 (Nasr and colleagues, 2023).
- Membership inference asks whether a record was in the training set (Shokri and colleagues, 2017). Model inversion reconstructs sensitive features, such as recognizable faces from confidence scores (Fredrikson and colleagues, 2015).
- Prompts are data: minimize what you share. Consent must be freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous; a data breach is unauthorized loss of or access to personal data.
- Pseudonymization (codes for names) is still personal data; anonymization means no one can reasonably be identified. Quasi-identifiers enable re-identification: ZIP, gender, and birth date identify most Americans (Sweeney, 87 percent); AOL 2006 and Netflix Prize data were re-identified.
- k-anonymity: every record matches at least k minus 1 others on its quasi-identifiers; k is the size of the smallest group. It can still leak a group's shared sensitive value.
- Differential privacy: results barely change whether or not you are included; smaller ε means stronger privacy and more noise, and budgets add up. Federated learning keeps raw data on devices and shares only model updates.
:::

This unit is about how AI systems take in the world, represent what they know, and reason with it. It opens with the framework that ties the field together, the five big ideas of AI, and then looks closely at the first of them: perception, how machines turn raw sensor signals into meaning. Expect questions that classify an example by big idea, ask what a sensor measures, and separate sensing from perception.

## The five big ideas in AI

The AI4K12 initiative, whose guidelines for teaching AI appear among this event's official references, organizes the whole field into five big ideas:

| Big idea | The idea in one sentence | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Perception** | Computers perceive the world using sensors. | A phone unlocking when it recognizes its owner's face |
| **2. Representation & Reasoning** | Agents keep representations of the world and use them to reason. | A navigation app planning the fastest route on a road map |
| **3. Learning** | Computers can learn from data. | A spam filter improving as users mark messages as spam |
| **4. Natural Interaction** | Intelligent agents need many kinds of knowledge to interact naturally with people. | A voice assistant noticing a caller is upset and slowing its speech |
| **5. Societal Impact** | AI can affect society in both positive and negative ways. | A hiring tool audited for whether it treats applicants fairly |

The course follows the same map. Unit 5 covered learning, Unit 6 covered much of natural interaction, Unit 8 covers societal impact, and this unit covers perception and representation and reasoning.

## Intelligent agents and the sense-deliberate-act cycle

An **agent** is anything that perceives its environment through sensors and acts on it through actuators (lesson 6.1). AI4K12 adds a test for when an agent counts as intelligent: it must run a non-trivial **sense-deliberate-act** cycle.

1. **Sense**: gather information about the world.
2. **Deliberate**: use a representation of the world to reason about what to do.
3. **Act**: carry out the decision, then sense again to see what changed.

```figure
type: flow
steps: [Sense, Deliberate, Act]
cycle: true
caption: The sense-deliberate-act cycle. An agent is intelligent when the deliberate step does real reasoning rather than a fixed reaction.
```

A garage-door opener senses a button press and acts by opening the door, but it deliberates about nothing, so AI4K12 does not count it as intelligent. A robot vacuum that maps a room, works out where it has already cleaned, and plans a path does real deliberation. So does a self-driving car, which senses traffic, predicts what other drivers will do, and chooses how to steer. This is the same cycle as robotics' sense, plan, act (lesson 6.2).

## Sensors and how they encode the world

Every perception system starts with sensors, which measure physical quantities such as light, sound, temperature, and pressure and turn them into numbers:

| Sensor | What it measures | How the data is represented |
|---|---|---|
| Camera | Light | A 2D grid of pixels holding brightness or red, green, and blue values (lesson 1.5) |
| Microphone | Sound | The sound wave sampled thousands of times per second, one number per sample |
| Lidar | Distance, using laser light | A depth image: the time each laser pulse takes to bounce back gives a distance for each point |
| Radar | Distance and speed, using radio waves | Like lidar, a map of distances, from reflected radio waves |
| GPS | Position on Earth | Latitude and longitude, computed from the timing of signals from several satellites |
| Accelerometer | Acceleration | Three numbers, one for each perpendicular axis (x, y, z) |

Every sensor has limits. A camera has a fixed resolution, struggles when very bright and very dark areas appear together (its dynamic range), and sees only certain colors of light; a microphone has a limited sensitivity and range of frequencies; and every sensor adds noise. Some sensors also detect what people cannot, such as infrared, ultraviolet, or ultrasonic signals. Because each sensor has different strengths, self-driving cars combine cameras, radar, and lidar, a practice called **sensor fusion**: cameras read signs and traffic lights, lidar gives precise distances, and radar keeps working in fog and rain.

::check{id="big-ideas-05"}

## Sensing versus perception

**Sensing** is detecting a signal: light falling on a camera chip, pressure on a pad, sound reaching a microphone. **Perception** is extracting meaning from sensory signals using knowledge: recognizing that the light pattern is a face, or that the sound is the word "yes."

An automatic door that opens when someone steps on a pressure pad senses but does not perceive; it reacts to a single measurement with no interpretation. Examples of perception include reading license plates or ZIP codes, unlocking a phone by recognizing a face, tagging people in photos, identifying an object from a picture, and understanding a caller's spoken request.

Perception depends on knowledge, and in modern systems that knowledge is often statistics learned from huge collections of examples, such as millions of labeled images (lesson 5.10) or the word patterns that power predictive text on a phone keyboard.

::check{id="big-ideas-04"}

## Feature extraction and the abstraction pipeline [Competitive]

Perception works in stages, from the raw signal up to more and more abstract features. The first step is **feature extraction**, which pulls useful patterns out of the raw data.

- In images, feature extraction usually begins with **edge detection**, finding places where brightness changes sharply, such as the border between a dark object and a light background.
- In speech, a **spectrogram** shows how a sound's energy is spread across frequencies over time. **Formants**, bands of concentrated energy, help identify vowel sounds.

Each stage builds on the one below it, forming an **abstraction pipeline**. In vision, pixels form edges, edges form contours, contours mark the boundaries of surfaces, surfaces make up objects, and objects make up scenes:

```figure
type: flow
steps: [Pixels, Edges, Contours, Surfaces, Objects, Scene]
caption: The vision abstraction pipeline, from raw data to meaning. The layers of a convolutional network learn a similar hierarchy (lesson 5.10).
```

Speech follows the same pattern, from the waveform to sounds, to word parts, to words, phrases, and sentences. Knowledge also flows back down the pipeline. "Recognize speech" and "wreck a nice beach" sound nearly identical, and knowledge of likely words and context at the higher levels settles which one was said. In the same way, a scene's layout lets a vision system infer that one object is partly hidden behind another.

## Where perception fails [Competitive]

Perception can fail at every stage. Sensors can be overwhelmed by darkness, glare, fog, or noise. Signals can be ambiguous, and even human perception is fooled by optical illusions. And a system can only perceive well what its training data prepared it for.

That last point raises a fairness problem. Perception systems often work worse for groups that were underrepresented in their training data or that are physically harder for the sensors to capture. AI4K12 gives two examples: faces with dark or low-contrast features, and children's speech, which is higher-pitched and less clearly articulated than adults'. A 2018 study called Gender Shades found that three commercial systems that classified gender from face images had error rates of up to 34.7 percent for darker-skinned women, but at most 0.8 percent for lighter-skinned men (lesson 8.2).

The remedies are the same as for any data problem: collect training data that covers everyone who will use the system, test accuracy separately for each group before release, combine sensors where one alone falls short, and keep people in the loop where mistakes carry high stakes.

:::trap
**Common trap.** A system that is accurate on average can still fail badly for one group. Overall accuracy hides gaps that only per-group testing reveals.
:::

:::takeaways
- AI4K12's five big ideas: Perception, Representation & Reasoning, Learning, Natural Interaction, and Societal Impact.
- An agent is intelligent when it runs a non-trivial sense-deliberate-act cycle; a garage-door opener does not, while a robot vacuum that maps and plans does.
- Sensors encode the world as numbers: camera pixels, microphone samples, lidar and radar depth images from reflection timing, GPS latitude and longitude from satellite timing, and accelerometer readings on three axes. Sensor fusion combines their strengths.
- Sensing detects a signal; perception extracts meaning from it using knowledge. A pressure-pad door senses; a license-plate reader perceives.
- Feature extraction starts with edges in images and spectrograms in speech. The vision pipeline runs pixels, edges, contours, surfaces, objects, scene, and higher-level knowledge resolves lower-level ambiguity.
- Perception fails from sensor limits, ambiguity, and gaps in training data. Systems can work worse for underrepresented groups (Gender Shades, 2018: up to 34.7 percent error for darker-skinned women versus at most 0.8 percent for lighter-skinned men), so test accuracy group by group.
:::
